10. Challenge

Zeus did not recognize the man as he stepped forward.

"Identify yourself, challenger," he ordered.

"I am Nestor from Oschia," the man responded. "I am the son of the King and Queen there, and born to rule. Even so, in Oschia I may not marry my sister. And even should she die untimely, then I still would not be able to marry my niece."

The Megara fell still. A princely challenger. Such things were heard of - princes born to rule, raised to fight, but who had no place in the succession which went to their sisters - while the power increasingly went to their sister's husbands. The only place in the world for such a one was to find a village where they respected the challenge-right on New Year's Day. And thus, here he was.

Four challengers had lined up this New Year's Day. It would have been five, Zeus reflected, if Achilles had lived. Could he have beaten all five, and one of them Achilles? He doubted it. Could he beat four? Each bout took a good deal out of him, and he was no longer in his prime.

But, he had to face it, he did not know this man. Was it possible that he had been sent by the Goddess to take over from him? To marry Hera and continue their work together? Was he strong and intelligent? It was possible, but Zeus had no way of knowing. But then, of course, he was only a mortal. And, more than that, a mere man. The Goddess would decide. He merely had to have faith.

Zeus decided to play for time.

"Tell me your story," he said. "What is your training? Why did you come here? What battles have you fought - and won?"

"I was born in Oschia; my mother was the Queen there. My father was King as you are, in the antique fashion, and when I was but a babe, he was killed and another took his place. When I had lived ten summers, my mother died and my sister became the Queen. I trained with sword and spear and shield, and became a captain of the front rank. I led our armies for five summers and we won many victories. Oschia now has three other villages paying us tribute, and we defend them well against all comers. When I had twenty-two summers, I challenged my sister's husband at the challenge because although he was strong with a sword, he was a fool and angered our allies and enemies both. I killed him, but could not marry my sister. I became outcast, but another man, wise and just, married my sister in my stead. I know not whether he can defend his title this New Year, but that is no longer my concern. As I travelled, I heard talk of the mighty Zeus and the lovely Hera, so I wandered here. You may be mighty, and you may be wise, but I know that I have no fear in challenging you for the hand of the lovely Hera. Should I lose, I will die or be once more outcast. But what is that to me, one of the lost?"

"True it is," said Zeus, "that should you lose, you will either die in the attempt or be cast out again. Have you considered that a man such as yourself would be welcome here, if not as Consort to the fair Hera, at least as a Captain of the First Rank? You could serve here, and should you find yourself unhappy with my rule, you could challenge me then?"

"A fair offer, mighty Zeus. Well do they call you wise, both among your allies and enemies. A lesser man might accept such an offer, removing all risk from the future. But, I ask you, what is life without risk? And I remind you: I was born to rule. There is safety in hiding behind a woman's skirts, but there is no honour as a man. I may never be able to be a woman, or to have or hold land in my own right, but I will be as great as I can be - or fail in the attempt. No, Zeus, it is you or I: Hera can only have one consort, and it must be whichever of us demonstrates our prowess to the Goddess.

"Fair enough," conceded Zeus. "All of the combatants go and prepare yourselves. At noon, we will return here for the challenges."

Zeus faced Nestor down the length of his sword.

The first three challengers had been tough, but Zeus had dealt with them. Old sparring partners, they had known each others styles and strategies. In the end, Zeus had been mighty enough to overcome them, and all three would be outcast before the New Year celebrations started at dusk.

Nestor was an unknown, but had the advantage of having seen Zeus fight three bouts. And, of course, the advantage of being fully rested for this bout. For once, Zeus was uncertain - and it showed. He could see Nestor sizing him up, sneering at him. But he had long ago learnt the virtue of patience. If he saw any weakness in this younger man, it was arrogance. Let him play to that.

"You have challenged me, stripling," he said. "If you want Hera, you need to defeat me. If I am undefeated by dusk, I remain Zeus, Hera's handfast. I am happy to stand here all day."

Nestor continued to look at him for a while, then suddenly charged towards him, bronze sword flashing in the early afternoon sun. Zeus parried in form, then turned away, trying to get behind him. But oh, Nestor was quick. Three times they came together, exchanging a flurry of blows, before Nestor backed off, hesitant. He had judged Zeus and old man, and past it. He had seen him beat three challengers before him, but dismissed them as also-rans. Now he understood the speed and strength in Zeus' sword arm and began to regret his challenge. Maybe he should have taken Zeus' offer.

Zeus saw him reflecting and hoped for a moment's inattention. He moved to his left and snapped his sword out to the right, but Nestor was fully awake and ready for the challenge. In moments, they were exchanging blows again but now Zeus was on the attack and Nestor retreating across the Megara. Zeus fainted left and, as Nestor went to cover, Zeus hooked his left foot around Nestor's right and pulled. Unready for the move, Nestor lost balance. As he tried to recover, Zeus jumped inside his guard, batted his sword away and held his sword at Nestor's throat.

Nestor dropped his sword, then fell to his knees.

"I concede," he said. "I shall never be King."

"On the contrary," said Zeus. "I see everything you told me about yourself was true. While I beat you, I accept that I did so through a trick. You have proved yourself a fine fighter and a worthy foe. Come, this is Hera: she will be your wife and I will be outcast. Here," he said, giving him his sword, "have my sword."

"Zeus is cast down," cried the villagers. "Let Zeus rise up."

And Zeus, who had been Zeus so many times, and then not been Zeus, only to be Zeus again, left the Megara, and left the village.

Meanwhile, the villagers married Nestor to Hera, and acclaimed him Zeus.

The ritual of the New Year never grew old, thought Hera.

It symbolizes everything we believe in. The power of the Goddess; the sense of renewal; the returning of life after death; the act of procreation; and the fertilization of the ground. Only Athene truly understood the ways and mysteries of the Goddess, but Hera had experienced the ritual for so many years that it was part of her lifeblood.

As ever, she danced in the circle of women around the fire, infused by the rhythm of the drums, guiding the movement of her feet. To her right was Athene. All the other women were the ritual priestesses of the Goddess, who would choose the men with whom they would demonstrate the act of procreation. She, of course, was reserved for him who was Zeus.

She picked him out in the crowd and called him forward. Young and virile, his body was tanned and hard, much like the man she had called Zeus when the day had dawned. A waste, she thought, I could grow to love this man, but the Goddess knows the ritual.

He joined in the dance with her and the drums continued, became more insistent. Slowly, the outer circle filled as the priestesses called more men to join them and they circled around the fire in opposite directions. In spite of his repeated exertions this afternoon after the wedding - and they had been repeated - she could see that Zeus was erect already and just waiting for her. She was ready too, but could feel the tension flooding through her as she waited, the expectation building as she used her power of decision to make him wait.

Finally, as he came around again, she knew the moment was right. Reaching out with her right hand, she grasped that member that was now so large and bulbous. She pulled him towards her, then put her left hand around him, holding her against her breasts. Consumed in the moment, she was barely aware of others doing the same up and down the circle. She pushed him to the ground, straddled him and went to work with a will. Twice she came, then a third, before she finally brought him to release. Around the fire, most of the other couples were already spent, and the villagers watched, expectant.

At the moment he was at his weakest, still pumping his life-affirming seed into her, possibly implanting him with his child, a flash of firelight on bronze shone to her right. Suddenly, she was pushed off her husband as another man - a stranger and yet so familiar - appeared in the firelight. The bronze dagger plunged into Zeus' throat and the blood gushed forth.

"Zeus is dead!" called all the villagers around the fire. "Zeus is dead."

And then, as Hera stood, holding the stranger's hand and lifting it above their heads, they cry changed.

"Long live Zeus!"

And Zeus, who had begun the day as Zeus, spent an afternoon as an outcast while the Goddess turned her face away from Zeus, became Zeus again.

And Athene went to work on the dead body which had been Zeus for half a day, and whose only legacy in the village would be a child.
