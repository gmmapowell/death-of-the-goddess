8. Thesmophoria

The night felt pitch black under the stars.

Polo was leading this fifth hunt of the Thesmophoria. The previous four had all been successful, and the boars had been collected in the stock yard just outside the village. Polo had not been involved with those, but had spent the past two days tracking down this boar. Unlike fending off predators such as lions, or hunting animals for food, bringing in animals for sacrifice required the animals to be surrounded and captured alive, either by getting close enough and putting a rope on them, or managing to stun them for long enough to rope them. It was then a question of managing to lead them back in spite of their bucking and running. Often, more than one rope would be needed to constrain the boar, and all the men on the hunt would be exhausted by the time the boar had finally been shepherded into its cell.

A fluff of moonlight came through the wispy cloud, briefly illuminating the forest ahead of them. There was no sign of the boar, obviously; it was still another hour's jog through the forest to where Polo had been marking its passage on the last couple of nights. But it helped him to see that they were on the right trail, still.

Behind him, staggered out over a furlong or more were the thirty men he had been assigned for this task. He had described to all of them where he had tracked the boar and, in any case, most of them had been this way once or twice in the past four days capturing one of the previous boars. And he wasn't sure he wanted a hunter helping him who was so bad at tracking he couldn't follow a man jogging twenty paces in front of him.

Polo adjusted the rope around his shoulders as he ran. His, of course, was the lead rope, the first - and hopefully only - one they would use to subdue and capture the boar. As a precaution, four of his men also carried ropes. Those carrying the ropes were the five "leaders" of this expedition, and each had five men under his command. Hunting at night was the one activity in which, by age-old tradition, men were trusted to look after themselves and did not need any women to supervise them. Polo had no idea why this should be, but apparently the Goddess had ordained it so.

As they moved further to the north, the landscape changed from rocky and barren to more hilly and forested. It was wetter here too, leading to little brooks that, from time to time, they had to cross. Ultimately, all of these flowed into The River, which then flowed down and past the eastern side of the village. Polo - along with most of the men from the village - was familiar with this route and this forest, since it supplied many of the things that could not be found in the village itself: wood and game being the most common two. Frequently, parties of men would come up here to bring back lesser or greater quantities of wood and, at times of festival such as now, when wild boar were needed, hunting parties would be dispatched to recover them from the woods.

As he crossed one of the brooks, he stopped jogging and started walking. For starters, it was hard to keep on jogging as the terrain became hillier and more uneven; equally, he was somewhat out of breath and needed to be in peak physical condition when he came into contact with the boar; but most importantly, he was close enough now that he wanted to start looking for trails and signs.

He knew where he had seen it on the past couple of nights and felt he knew its routine. Around dusk, in common with many of the other animals, it had spent time by the side of the small lake in a hollow a little further up the hillside. It had then retreated and gone about three furlongs until it had snuffled its way into a thicket which, it presumed, meant it was safe from attack overnight. It was, of course, safe from all its normal predators, who worked alone and could not invade or destroy the thicket. Against a group of organised and well-armed men, on the other hand, it might as well have stayed by the lake all night, drinking.

He crested the small rise and worked his way down the trail towards the lake. He hoped to pick up the scent and the trail here and follow it back - along the path he had seen it take - to the thicket. A stone's throw from the nearest part of the lake he paused; sitting down and remaining still, he watched carefully to see small animals and birds coming and going from the lake. Needless to say, putting any of these to flight could easily give away his position and identity as a predator and scare the boar into flight.

The five men directly under his command came and silently gathered behind him. If they were silent, how did he know they were there? he wondered, and then dismissed the thought as irrelevant. It didn't matter if the animals in the forest knew that they were there, as long as they weren't frightened by the fact that they were there. It wasn't other animals that animals ran away from; they were surrounded by them: it was danger. And the main sign of danger was noise. When there was a commotion something unusual was happening, and that was probably bad.

He could neither hear nor sense the other four groups gathering back between him and the top of the rise. But he had no doubt that they were there, or that, looking down with a touch of moonlight reflected off the lake, they could see the parties in front of them. But, even if they weren't, it was too late to do anything about it now; if they were needed, the commotion he and his men would cause struggling by themselves would be big enough to attract them to the right spot.

He slowly got up and led his men down to the lake and slowly around it. Loping close to the ground, he sniffed at the trails of the various animals and stared at the various prints in the mud by the lake shore. There! he could see the unmistakable prints of a boar, moving around where it had come to drink at nightfall. And - there! - were the tracks it left as it started off to find its thicket. Moving ever slower, he followed those now up the hillside, away from the lake.

Behind him, he could feel the men fanning out. As he came closer, they would need to surround the thicket. They were fanning out now to be in position. At the moment, everything was going to plan.

He looked ahead, trying to make a thicket appear out of thin air, but it would not. He had not gone wrong - he could still see the hoof prints in the clay soil in front of him. One pace, another; yet still the thicket did not appear. It certainly could not have disappeared since yeaterday. He could feel his pulse steadily increasing.

There it was! The thicket suddenly resolved itself out of the darkness ahead, taking form from the liquid darkness. He took another four steps, then stopped and signalled by raising his arm in the air and then pointing. The men behind him did not stop, but simply kept going and surrounded the thicket.

He removed the rope from around his shoulders and carefully laid it on the ground in front of him. He undid the knot that had held it together, then found the noose and made sure that it was tied correctly. The hemp felt warm and soft in his hands. He grasped it carefully - he did not want to let it go accidentally - but ensuring that when he was ready it would float free and not be trapped.

Across from him he heard what sounded like the repeated warble of a woodjay. In fact, he was sure, it was Wayne mimicking the sound closely enough so as not to alert the boar, but distinctly enough that Polo recognised it for what it was: Wayne, the most important of his colleagues in this endeavour, was in place. Hopefully the others were also: he had not heard the honking noise of a goose to indicate disagreement. He waited, listening carefully for any noise coming from the thicket. He couldn't hear anything, but he didn't really expect to. The boar would either be resting or watchful - either way, he would expect it to be close to silent.

It was his turn to signal. Pursing his lips together, he gave forth the cry of a gull, looking for its home in the night. On this signal, Wayne was supposed to stand and simply walk into the thicket. He couldn't see, hear, smell or sense whether this was happening but, again, there was no responding signal to indicate that Wayne was unable to follow the plan.

Suddenly, ahead of him, he could hear a scuffling and the noise of brambles splitting. He heard the boar squeal - presumably as Wayne stuck him with a pruning-hook - and then the forest came alive with sound as every bird any animal within earshot recognised the noise for what it was - danger. They ran, flew or scurried away from the noise as best they can. Polo ducked as a large bird nearly flew through him. But from the thicket, the sounds of struggle continued: as yet, the boar felt that resistance was better than flight.

From behind Wayne came the sudden crashing of cymbals as two of the team tried to startle the boar into flight. Faced with both the noise of the cymbals and the direct assault by Wayne, they expected the boar to turn and flee. But still it kept on, convinced that the thicket was its best chance of survival.

The situation was starting to get desperate: if and when the boar did decide to flee, it would not be a gentle trot to safer pastures but a full-throttle charge, barging through anything it found in its way. Polo, holding a single rope by himself, was unlikely to be able to resist that - possibly would not even survive it.

With a giant tearing noise, Polo sensed the boar tear free from the thicket - or possibly tear the thicket free from the ground - and charge directly towards him. Steadying himself, he focused on holding the rope in the throwing position.

Stepping quickly to his right, he stretched his left arm out so that the loop in the rope hardly moved, and then he neatly dropped it over the boar's neck as he passed. He let the boar pull up the slack and then felt the tension in the rope pick up as the boar continued its charge. He took two steps, then wrapped the body of the rope around a tree and fell to the ground.

The boar's neck snapped back as the rope tensed. Denied its freedom once again, it turned and raged. Although it was caught on the rope, Polo knew that this was now the critical moment in the struggle. One man, however strong or experienced, could not win a test of strength against a boar. Polo's advantage at the moment was solely down to the single coil of rope that he had run around the tree. An advantage that would completely disappear if the boar just walked back to the tree and unlooped itself.

There was a crashing through the underbrush and suddenly Buddy was there with another rope. As he arrived, he tossed it around the boar's neck and pulled. Within moments, he too had run a coil of rope around a tree trunk and succesfully tied it off.

From behind him, he sensed movement and Wayne appeared, pruning hook levelled in his right hand. With a quick jab, he lunged it into the head of the boar, just behind the ear. Within seconds, the boar was on the ground, stunned.

They had the boar. Now they just needed to get it home.

"It is the season of Death," intoned Bougainvillaea at the stockade the following evening as the sun set.

        As we see the death of the Sun every day,

        As we see the death of the Moon every month,

        So every year we see the death of the Sisters,

        And with them, the death of the grain.

       

        Into the earth it will be buried,

        To grow strong with the Goddess in the underworld,

        And to return to us in the New Year

        As the Sun turns and the Sisters return to us.

For indeed, after the Sun went down, the Sisters soon followed. In just a few more days, they would not be seen at all until the next year began. And so, it was time for the festival of death, burial and sleep. For this, the Goddess demanded that five boars should show the way: today they would be killed and buried. Once the ploughing was done, they would be brought back up again, as the seed was planted in the soil and they hoped to raise the crops next year. Or as the wind and the river planted seeds inside a woman's body, which would come out again, several moons later, in the form of new life. The ways of the Goddess were ever the same: a cycle of life and death: every day, every month, every year, every lifetime, throughout all the ages.

Bougainvillaea nodded to Poinsettia to lead the first boar forward. With a rope around its neck, the animal, docile now after five nights of captivity, came forward on Poinsettia's command. They led it foward to the edge of one of the cisterns of the dead. As they reached the rim of the cistern, Bougainvillaea reached out her hands and called to the Goddess:

       

        Oh, Goddess!

        Even though it is also the time for your Death, hear us!

        We who worship you, offer you this nourishment

        May itprovide for you over the course of the months to come while you are at rest.

        Know that wharever you do not use,

        Will we take and feed to the fields that they too

        May be nourished in this Time of Death.

Pulling her hands back, she took the long bronze knife of offering and calmly slit the boar's throat from one side to the other. In moments, the boar collapsed and then kicked out in its death throes. When it had ceased moving, Bougainvillaea and Poinsettia reached down and pushed it over the edge. It landed with a distinct thud, following by a rustling and a slithering as the snakes below moved to suck up the blood before it drained into the dry earth.

Four more to go, thought Poinsettia.

Ploughing, thought Clematis, is a job to which men are less ideally suited.

They had the strength, and given how difficult it was to trawl a wooden stick - even one that had a flint or bronze tip - through the dry, barren earth, it was not surprising that they were given most of the work to do. But they weren't capable of going more than a dozen paces in a straight line. And if there weren't someone there to pull them up on it, the furrows would go all over the place. And that would never do. The seeds would then be planted too close together; it would be impossible to walk between the rows; it would be harder to tell sprouting plants from weeds; it would be a disaster. How could these men simply not see that?

Clematis had stopped having her monthly bleeding already and, so the elders of the village told her, she had all the other signs, too. Inside herself, she felt no different, but soon after the harvest next year, she would be giving birth to a baby - a daughter, she hoped - and a granddaughter for Hyacinth. And so the family might continue. Of course, if it was a son, that was one of those things and she was sure that she would love it as you do all living things but, well, it just wasn't the same, was it?

"No, stop," she called out. "That's drifting to the left again." Walking forward, she used her own hoe to push the soil that had ridged up on either side of the furrow back down into it, trampled it back down, and then once again drew a straight line for five or six paces from the end of the completed furrow. "OK," she said, trying to keep the exasperation out of her voice, "let's try this again." And see if you can manage to not muck it up for a moment or two, she thought to herself.

Wayne picked up his hoe and smiled at her. He had a pretty smile, at least for a boy of his age, and she dutifully smiled back. Anything to try and keep these men happy. Now that she was pregnant, she was eligbile to start working her way up the hierarchy among the women. The bottom rung was to be a woman who had proved herself fertile - an infertile woman was no more use than a man: how could they have a stake in the community if they would never be able to pass that on? What would keep them from just living for themselves?

This was, of course, the most menial assignment that was available. Supervising the ploughing and reaping were low-profile jobs that called for dozens of women to supervise all the men to try and complete the job in the shortest possible time. The more senior women were needed to handle the actual burying of the seeds which, of course, was always done at night - the time of death. With all their other responsibilities as well - pots did not stop being needed just because it was ploughing time - the supervising of the men who, Clematis felt, wouldn't really need supervising at all if they were even moderately competent, inevitably fell to the most junior qualifying women. Women for whom it mattered that there would be a harvest next year. Women with a demonstrable stake in the outcome. Women with children.

For a few paces, all seemed to go well. Wayne walked backwards along the line she had scratched in the earth, repeatedly digging his hoe into the ground and pulling it towards him. But before long, Clematis could see that he, too, was drifting from the straight line that was required.

"Stop!" she called again. Wayne looked at her with what she could only describe as a smirk.

"What now?" he asked.

"You've drifted to the right this time."

"I thought you didn't want me going to the left," countered Wayne. Somewhere amongst the men there was a low chuckle. See how she deals with this, they seemed to be saying.

"I don't," she replied sternly. "Or the right. The lines need to be shaft-straight, or the seeds won't grow." She moved forward to clean up again. As she did so, she heard one of the men mumbling under his breath:

"I could show you how straight my shaft is, if you'd like." She decided it was best to ignore such comments. There was work to be done. She didn't want to take all day dealing with discipline issues. And she didn't want the senior sisters to think she couldn't handle it. Yes, from time to time men needed to be whipped. But most of the time it was just a question of making sure you insisted on them doing the job the way it needed to be done.

Over and over again, admittedly. She sighed and told the men to try again.

As the night fell, Bougainvillaea led her senior sisters out to the newly ploughed fields, carrying the sealed jars of seed. With the appropriate ritual words, Poinsettia removed the stopper from the first jar and put in the dipper to remove the seeds.

       

        As the river and the wind place seeds in us, so we place our seed in you, O Earth!

        At this time of Death, we put you to sleep in your grave,

        That as the Sun turns and the Goddess wakes again,

        You will sprout and come back to us in life and in bounty.

With that, she poured a line of seed into the prepared furrow. Behind her, Hyacinth and Tamarisk completed the burial by using their hoes to fill in the soil from the side of the furrow, covering the seed in its darkness. Then they moved on to the next five paces of furrow and repeat the procedure.

Planting seed may not be as back-breaking as digging the furrow, but even so, it is a time-consuming task. The women were at it all night, planting seed, burying seed and incanting the appropriate words. But as they looked up at the Moon, approaching full now, they knew that this was the last night for planting, because tomorrow would see the Thesmophoria begin.

Clematis had hoped to be chosen, but had not.

The five young women chosen were all a little older than her, and had all had their first child. While not actually a requirement, it probably helped that they had also done well in the various sets of games that had been held during the year and come to the attention of the senior ladies in the village.

Technically, this was not a competition but a ritual. But every year there was a sense of competition among the five women as they climbed down into the pits to recover the bodies of the boars that had been thrown there the previous month. Each woman who had been chosen had then recruited a team of five friends to help with the task of manipulating the ropes.

Camelia double-checked that her rope was securely fastened to the tree trunk near the top of the cistern she was planning on climbing down into, tugging it as hard as she could. She then paced out the distance to the rim and leaned over. Way down below her, she could see the carcass of the boar, rotting in the heat of the cistern and still covered with snakes. She turned and checked that the other girls she had brought with her had all their noise-makers with them: drums, flutes, rocks. The noise would be key to her survival - literally.

She looked back over to see Bougainvillaea and Poinsettia saying the ritual words to open the festival, and then commanded the young women to "go over the edge". Immediately, the girls started bashing their instruments and making a deafening noise. Under cover of that, Camelia threw her rope over and down into the cistern. It fell perfecly, just a couple of paces from the body of the boar.

Climbing down rapidly, hand over hand as her legs, wound tightly around the rope, slid down, she was near the floor very quickly. Pausing for a moment, she picked out her strategy: seeing where the snakes were and avoiding them, she fell the remaining distance to the ground.

Picking up the end of the rope, she wound it under and around the body of the boar and pulled it tight, all the while keeping an eagle eye out for any of the snakes that might come and attack her. In no time, she had the boar safely trussed and she turned her face upward to signal her rope-women that the boar was ready to go. Immediately, they started pulling on the rope and pulling it up.

Of course, the snakes were not best pleased that their offering was being taken away again. A number of them hissed and raised their bodies from their floor. Camelia pulled the bag which she had brought down with her from her back. From within it, she pulled a number of barley cakes, each of which was in the shape of a boar, and had within it both blood and meat from a real animal, collected at the moment they had been sacrificed. She placed these on the rocks near to the snakes. Although it was not a competition, and there were no judges, everyone knew, and everyone spoke about, and most of the bragging rights consisted of, how closely you went to the snakes to offer them these treats; and whether you offered them to the most active snakes. For most women, this ritual was at best a once in a lifetime experience, so very few stinted at the risk of coming too close to a snake. Camelia certainly didn't.

Her five trophies placed directly in front of the most menacing snakes, Camelia was done and ready to leave. She looked up to see the boar hanging directly over her and almost at the lip of the cistern. Above that, she could see two of the women trying to pull it out over the edge. Behind them, she could hear the raucous din of the noise-girls continuing unabated; the noise was deafening but seemed to have little influence on the snakes.

She started to do the performative dance that was demanded of the women in this situation. It was said to distract the snakes and confuse them. They considered a stationary woman in the cistern as a target, but a woman doing a dance was something else: confusing. They would wait and evaluate before attacking her; by which time, it would be hoped the rope would be back down here and she could escape.

The dance itself was old as time and was said to be based on the snakes' own movements. All of the women chosen for this task today had spent some portion of the last few weeks learning it and preparing to perform this ritual that might simply be the difference between life and death.

Suddenly, with a crack, the rope came flying down to land within two paces of her. Effortlessly adapting the steps to her dance, she leapt into the air and caught hold of the rope two cubits up in the air. Using an efficient hand-over-hand technique - with her legs again supporting her weight - she quickly shimmied up the rope to the lip of the cistern where, by putting her feet against the edge of the lip, she was able to push her body away from the cistern wall and simply climb up using the rope to support her weight.

And she was up! Turning back to the cistern, she bowed to the snakes she revered as representatives of the Goddess on earth still below, and then turned to look around. Three of the five boars were at the top of the cisterns, but she was the only hunter back up. As she looked around, all of the members of her team applauded her and cheered her success.

It wasn't a competition, but ...

Carnation never made it back out of the pit. Although she had successfully roped her boar, and her team had pulled it up, she had not been able to avoid the attentions of the snakes and had been fatally bitten. The price of failing to appease the snakes was to become their possession. Bougainvillaea and Poinsettia went to the edge of the cistern and looked down. Below, Carnation was lying on the floor of the cistern, writhing in pain, just paces away from the rope which she would never reach. Behind them, the girls had stopped making their noise, respectful now in the face of death.

Poinsettia looked down into the cistern at the girl writhing and murmuring at the bottom. "It is a shame," she commented. "We will never know what message the Goddess put in her mouth."

Under cover of darkness, the women gathered to mix straw and blood. Blood and meat from the boars were mashed up and mixed with straw from the previous year's harvest. As they did this, and lay it out on the floor of the Megara, they were seeing how this is the reversal of that. At harvest, they break apart the stem from the life; at burial, they have to put the two back together. As the night progressed, the pile of fertilized straw grew, until finally all the blood and meat from the boars' carcasses had been used up.

The morning after was the morning of burial.

The seeds, of course, had already been buried in the furrows. But now was the day of mourning, and the day of covering, when the seeds would be hidden from the view of the Sun until the time of resurrection should come.

On a day of death, there was no singing. On a day of death, there could be no eating. On a day of death, there was no joy.

One by one, the women took as much of the prepared straw from the Megara as they could, and took it into the fields. They spread it out across the furrows, covering and burying the seeds. Then they sat down on the blankets of straw they had made and prayed silently to the Goddess. On every occasion, in every location, they prayed for her to see their devotion and to give increase to their work.

And then they stood up again, and moved, slowly and in mourning, back to the Megara to collect more straw, to take to another field ...

Clematis took her final armload of straw from the diminishing pile on the floor of the Megara out into the far field as the Sun slowly descended in the west. She placed it on the ground and sat on it. For the first time in the day, as she faced the Sun and prayed, she felt the connection. As she was harbouring life inside her, so the ground was harbouring life. It was not visible to anybody; indeed, they had spent the whole day covering it up in the same way that her body covered up the life growing within her, but it was the same thing.

The ground was not dead, the way she always felt it was at this time of year, but alive. Alive, and growing. Alive and full of potential, soon to be revealed!

Just like her daughter, growing in her womb.
