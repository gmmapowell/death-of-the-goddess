8. Thesmophoria

Cronos was still put out by the failure of his cattle raid the previous month.

"We could have used some of those cattle as our offering to the Goddess," he grumbled as he rose out of bed. "It is not easy tracking down wild boar in the forest."

"If it were easy, the Goddess would not need to demand it, would she?" countered Rhea, reasonably, she felt, but increasingly irked by Cronos. She was starting to feel that the New Year - with its new start, and most importantly, new Cronos - could not come soon enough.

"But what if we are not ready? Tonight is the night, is it not?"

"You must have more trust in the men of this village," she responded. "If the Goddess wills us to be ready, we will be ready. If she has already turned her face from us, then we must expiate that sin, or suffer worse consequences."

Cronos turned away from her, his feelings clearly written all over his face. He did not have the faith in the goodness of the Goddess his wife exuded. Maybe it was something to do with being a woman - or maybe to do with being a woman born into a position of power. As his daughter would be, after his death.

Putting on his tunic and sandals, Cronos huffed off to see if they had managed to bring in the fifth boar needed for the ceremony.

Shortly after he arrived at the specially constructed holding pen, Jacceus came in from the forest with his hand of soldiers: a little bloody and battered, but leading a boar by the ropes they had taken with them.

"He is a brave one, that," Jacceus said to Cronos. "He will do the Goddess much honour in his death."

"He fought you long and hard, by the look of it," Cronos replied. "Was it a hard struggle?"

"It was," confirmed Jacceus and launched into a long narrative of the hunt. They had initially attempted to capture another boar, but that one had died rather than be captured; only while he was in his death throes was this other bull stampeded to flight. "Carenus and Oeoragus were trampled immediately he burst from the thicket," Jacceus explained. "But Carenus managed to hold on and wound him and wind him." He pointed to the boar's flank. "He inflicted all these wounds even as he was being trampled. I think he'd already lost the use of his legs by then."

Cronos whistled. The past few months had been difficult, to say the least, and much of it reflected on him and his leadership - and none of it well. Two more of his men lost. The village could ill-afford this rate of attrition.

He watched as the boar was placed in the pen, lost in thought. Then he realized there were other things demanding his attention, and turned south towards the fields where the ploughing was in progress. Hopefully that, at least, was nearly done.

If Amphissa had ever carried Macareus' child, the Goddess had taken it away again. It felt like a dream - a brief interlude in her life between girlhood and widowhood - and now she was returned to the Goddess. And as such, she was eligible to take part in the rituals of the Thesmophoria, something she had assumed she had missed out on because of her path into marriage. If she had given birth to a child with Macareus - especially if it had been an always hoped-for girl-child - she would have been committed to the path of a married woman, no matter that her husband had been taken from her untimely. But now, she had a second chance.

The fields had been ploughed; the seed had been planted; the boars had been slain and cast into the pits. Now all that remained was the ritual of recovering the boars - to bring them back from death into life, thus symbolizing the resurrection that they wanted from the seed that had been buried: in the spring, it should sprout back into the world of life.

It was a ritual, not a competition. How many times had she been told that over the past weeks as she prepared for this important role in the life of the community. Five women would be lowered down over the side, and five boars needed to come back up. Then, and only then, could the five women try to come back up themselves.

All in the face of a den of snakes who would literally see their food supply being removed from them.

As the Goddess' agents on earth, the snakes must be treated reverentially. It was not simply a case of evading them, and certainly not one of killing them. Rather, they must be negotiated with. Each of the women climbing into the pit would take with her an offering in place of the boar she was removing. And she had to hope that the snakes would accept the offering.

For that reason, much love and devotion had gone into the preparation of the offering cakes over the past few days. They had to be perfect: succulent, tasty and comprising all the elements that delighted the Goddess in the form of the snakes. Blood, most important, the rich smell of it causing a tang and a snap in the air: a substitute for the fresh blood of the boars. Much of it was grain, both an offering in its own right but a reminder to the Goddess of just how important grain was to the village and how they would be soon lying the grain to rest in the soil over the course of the winter to spring back anew in the spring, ready for harvesting. And mixed in were bodily fluids from the woman herself, identifying this as her specific offering, thus gaining her the privilege of access on the cavern floor.

Amphissa took hold of the rope and moved to the canyon edge. When all five women were ready, Rhea signalled and all the other women at the edge of the cavern starting beating their instruments and making the loudest noise they could manage. As the noise started, the snakes instinctively fled from the boars, and the five women jumped into the cavern, holding on to their ropes and shinning down the side of the cavern as quickly as they could.

In just a few heartbeats, Amphissa was on the cistern floor. It was not a race, but she was pleased to see that she was the first one down. Looking around, she spotted the boar nearest her - the one that she needed to bring up - and checked to see if there were any snakes around. Seeing none, she ran forward, her rope in one hand and the canvas bag with her cake in the other. Reaching over the boar, she placed the canvas bag on the ground and opened it up to expose the cake. Hopefully, any snakes which returned would go for the fresher scent of the cake rather than the more putrid stench coming up from the boar.

As quickly as she could, she tied the rope around the feet of the boar, making sure that even in her haste the knots were all tied tightly. It would be a disaster if the boar were to come loose and fall. Aside from the extra time it would take, it could damage the boar and even possibly land on her. When she was done, she shouted, waved and tugged on the rope to pass the message that the boar was ready for lifting. Almost immediately, the rope became tense and the boar started to lift into the sky.

Not that it was a race, but hers was the first boar off the ground.

Before long, the boar disappeared over the cistern rim, and then seconds later the rope was falling back down. She reached out and took it, then leaped up, climbing against the cistern wall as quickly as she could. Her feet slipped twice, but then she was up and over the rim.

She was up first. Not, of course, that it was a race.
