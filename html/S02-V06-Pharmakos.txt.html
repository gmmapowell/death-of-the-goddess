22 Pharmakos

Cronos and Rhea looked at every hut closely.

For the day of atonement, everything must be absolutely perfect; anything less than perfection would show insult to the Goddess. Moreover, at the same time they needed to select the Pharmakoi. Two pharmakoi needed to be chosen to offload any remaining guilt - anything not only unintentional but overlooked. One atoned for the sins of the men, carrying them away; the other atoned for the sins of the women. Both were, of course, men themselves: the Goddess demanded the sacrifice of men to atone for the community.

As they walked into Amphissa'a hut, and saw Amphissa and her husband standling there, the Goddess called to Rhea as clearly as she had ever done in her life. It pleases me to have this one came the voice and immediately Rhea responded, pointing straight at Macareus and intoning the ritual words as she had so many times before: "Macareus, the Goddess calls you to serve her as a Pharmakos".

Cronos was rocked. Here was a man he trusted and leant upon; a man who had been married just this season; and a man, who, he thought, might one day be Cronos in his turn. But a man who, even if he survived the experience would be outcast from this village. Cronos turned to remonstrate with Rhea - but then saw the look on her face: she did not wish this for herself; it was the word of the Goddess speaking through her.

While Macareus was stunned, he knew the ritual, he knew his place, and he knew that unlkely as he was to be chosen, it was still a possibility. He had prepared, as all the men in the village prepared and was content with his lot. If nothing else, this was an opportunity to serve the Goddess - and the community - giving his livelihood and his life in order that the village might atone for their sins with the Goddess.

He knelt before Rhea to accept the commission she gave him. He bent his head while she hit him with the ceremonial leek, completing his appointment. Later, he knew, that same leek would be used to give him a thorough beating. Without so much as a glance at his wife, he arose and led the way out of the door.

The moment they had gone, Amphissa broke down and cried. Collapsing to the floor, she knelt until all the tears had wracked out of her. She still wasn't sure if she was carrying his child, but she thought so - but would never have the opportunity to tell him so. Even if Macareus was not dead yet - and might not even die during the ritual - he was no longer her husband; he belonged to the Goddess now. He might as well be dead. And her life, too, lay in tatters, child or no child.

By the time the procession returned to her hut, a few fingers of sun later, she had composed herself and tidied up the mess she had caused in her misery. The beaters, led by Cronos and some of his standing army, came in and screamed and shouted, chasing her - and any hidden spirits - outside into the open air. As she ran outside, screaming herself, she saw Macareus leading the procession. Now formally attired as a Pharmakos, he had the blank expression suited to one who thought nothing, felt nothing and accepted everything. As she joined the back of the procession, the two Pharamkoi led them forward through the village, collecting ever more villagers on their way out of the village, taking all the spirits and evils with them.

It was not enough, never enough, to assume that the spirits would follow them. Everywhere they went, it was essential to screech, yell and make as much noise as possible, scaring any spirits away from the village. And the worst was yet to come. To ensure that all the spirits left, that all the evil was removed from the village, it was essential to beat the Pharmakoi. Her first time attending the Pharmakos as an adult and she would have to beat the man whom she had come to love as her own husband over the last few cycles of the moon. And then cast him off into the wilderness to survive by himself as best he could. How could the Goddess be so cruel?

It was a test, she realized. A test of her strength and fidelity. She must remain strong for the whole village; remain strong even as she knew Macareus would remain strong.

Eventually the procession led out of the village and into the wilderness to the west. The two Pharmakoi stopped, turned and knelt on the ground. Now was the time of the beating: they knew what was coming and were prepared. Macareus looked straight ahead of him, his eyes focused on the far distance, maintaining the strength and purity he knew was the price of carrying his burden to the Goddess. To take all the evil spirits out of the village that the villagers could live in peace and prosperity was a service beyond his highest aspirations. With only one compatriot, he was protecting the village for the next year; a greater service even than he had signed up for when he had joined the army. Possibly greater even than the service of being chosen as Cronos.

The beating began.

As the blows rained down, he held his composure, suppressing his instinct and training to retaliate. He took the blows and endured, holding himself stiff in the kneeling position for as long as he could, trying to distance himself from the injuries to his body - just as he had practised so often during the army drills. And trying to stare into the distance, knowing that those delivering the blows would be known to him: Cronos, his fellow soldiers, his father, his wife. At the thought of Amphissa, he nearly broke and lost concentration. Quickly, he re-focused his mind on the here and now, focusing on the sensation of being beaten, over and over, by all the villagers wielding nothing more than leeks.

But, ineffectual as each blow might be, in total they started to add up. He felt, rather than heard, the body of his fellow Pharmakos collapse to the ground next to him. The beating continued, of course. The beating would continue until Rhea was convinced all the evil spirits had been driven out.

Eventually, he lost consciousness.

When he woke again, he found himself beaten and bloody. Dazed, he looked around him to regain his bearings. By this time, all the other villagers had left; his only companion was his fellow Pharmakos - dead, as far as he could see. The ritual was supposed to be one of expulsion rather than death, but it was always possible to go too far, he supposed.

Moving his head slowly, he saw the platter in front of him; that was all the food that was provided for them for as long as they should continue to live. And there, slithering over it - he recoiled in shock, setting off searing pains through his head, and throughout his body; a mistake. There, slithering over the sum total of food for the rest of his life was a snake. A snake that, now it was aware of another sentient being in the neighbourhood, reared back and looked him in the eyes, poised to strike.

It was only then that he realised what had happened to Euphorygus, his fellow Pharamakos. He had survived the beating, only to be left out here as food for a snake. The snake - one of the snakes, he corrected himself, as he noticed more slithering over the area - had bitten him and killed him.

He knew that any sudden motion would make him a target. But not to move would leave him a potential victim of the snakes. Did Cronos and Rhea know that this would be his fate? Specifically, since Cronos only lived for a year, did Rhea know when she chose him that she was, in fact, condeming him to die. Not that it made much difference, he supposed: he had definitely be condemned to a death sentence; the only difference was whether it was here, now, or if he could defer it by using his wits for a number of days or weeks.

The snake watching him settled down and slid back to its meal. Its meal, now, because there is no way that Macareus would attempt to challenge it for the food: to do so would invite certain and sudden death. Probably, difficult as it was to contemplate, his best strategy would be to lie here until full dark by when, hopefully, the snakes would have all left the area.

Mercifully, he lost consciousness again.

In the dark of the night, he could hardly see anything.

He listened intently, trying to pick out the noises that would indicate the presence of snakes. Hearing nothing, he clambered up to his feet. Staggering, he started to move westwards. To move to the east would take him back to the village. Apart from the harm he would do to the villagers, they would undoubtedly kill him on sight. The only safe move was to head westwards into the unknown.

Slowly he staggered onwards, narrowly avoiding falling into one of the cisterns. Everything out here now was a danger and a risk. He had nowhere to stay, nothing to eat; his only source of water would be any river or pond he could find; his only food anything he could kill.

He was now an outcast from the village - the only life he had every known.
