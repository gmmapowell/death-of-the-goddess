14. Lucretia

"You realize what this means of course," Arruns said to Sextus a couple of days later.

"No, what?"

"Octavius will not be King."

"Because our sister died."

"Yes, the fact that he was married to her will matter for nothing now she is dead. In fact, I've heard it said that in some of the towns of Latinum, a king is deposed once his wife dies, and sometimes the king is even put to death."

"Barbaric. At least that doesn't happen here."

"Otherwise our father would no longer be the King of Rome. And Octavius would have had his opportunity to inherit."

"So who does become King?"

"You mean, who becomes Queen? Because the answer to who becomes King is 'the husband of the Queen'."

"And that is?"

"As far as I am aware, there is only one candidate, which would be Lucretia."

"And that means that our oh-so-pious friend Collatinus will be King? Really? Him?"

"Who, indeed, could have guessed? She seemed so far removed from the throne at the time, and even just a few days ago ... it was bound to be Octavius."

"Except, as you said, it's not that Collatinus will be King, it's that Lucretia will be Queen."

And with that, he left it hanging.

"How goes the military operation?" Sextus asked Collatinus that evening over a beaker of wine.

"Apart from the fact that our forces are bored from having nothing to do, it's fine."

"I'm bored, too," commented Arruns.

"Aren't we all?" asked Gaius.

"Well, you could come and help with the work that we supposed to be doing," retorted Collatinus angrily. "It didn't used to be too bad, you four skiving off when you were supposed to be working, but now that Octavius doesn't care about anything anymore, it's too much work just for me."

"Poor boy," mocked Titus. "Maybe you should get the Brutus to help you out. I'm sure he'd be a barrel of laughs."

"Oh, get lost," responded Collatinus wearily.

"I need a wife," said Sextus suddenly. "I need to get married and settle down. I need to have children to follow me. Who knows anything about wives?"

"Do you have anyone in mind?" asked Arruns.

"No, not really. I'm not even sure of what I want in a wife. Your wife, for instance, Gaius, always seems up for a laugh. And yours, Arruns, seems somewhat quieter, but is great to look at. And I assume she's good in bed. I think that would be important to me. What do you think, Collatinus?"

"I don't know what would please you," he replied. "I know that my wife pleases me - in bed and out of it. She is everything a Roman matron should be - quiet, speaks only when spoken to; efficent, keeps the household running smoothly; peaceable; elegant. Yes, she is truly a queen among women," he continued without realising what he had said or how it would inflame Sextus' jealousy.

"A real paragon of virtue. I assume she spins wool, as well," Sextus mocked.

"Of course. I am sure that if we were to drop in unannounced, right now, that we would find her with her distaff in her hand, spinning. She is not one of these modern types to find drinking and gossiping more productive than spinning wool."

Arruns laughed. "How easily you are deceived, good Collatinus. Surely you cannot believe that your wife is home alone, spinning. Spinning you tales, perhaps. She is probably at home 'entertaining' one of your slaves."

In his corner, Brutus sat up, shocked by the accusation. He knew Lucretia personally, and knew that she was just as virtuous as Collatinus believed her to be. He would swear to it in court - for all the good the word of a madman would do. And then he relaxed again, remembering his part: he was a fool.

"Well, I suggest we put it to the test. It is too late this evening, but if we set off for Rome at mid-afternoon tomorrow, we can be there around this time, if we use the post horses. We can drop in on your wives, Arruns, Gaius and Titus, and see what they are doing. Then we can go over to my house and see what my wife is doing. And then you can admit that I have the most virtuous wife in the world."

"Let us do that!" cackled Sextus. "I do not think it is virtue that I value most in a wife, but let us first see this virtuous woman and then maybe I will be convinced."

It was easier than they thought to visit the three brothers' wives.

When they arrived at Arruns' house, the steward told them that his wife was out. When he enquired where, he was told at Gaius' house. So they went there. When they arrived, the steward admitted them into the atrium, where all three wives were gathered, drinking wine and chatting about the latest rumours and scandals in the city.

"Well, Gaius," drawled his wife, "how very good of you to drop by. And bringing such handsome companions too. Did you miss me so much that you had to abandon your siege of wherever-it-was to come and see me?"

"Arruns," said that son's wife, getting up to greet him with a hug and a kiss. "What an unexpected pleasure. We received no word. Has Ardea fallen?"

"Titus," greeted his wife. "Have you come to make a baby brother for little Titus?"

"No," replied all three, suprisingly close to in unison.

"I have decided to get married," explained Sextus. "And so I am looking for a wife."

"Well, there's no point looking here," exclaimed Gaius' wife. "We're all already taken, and by your brothers, at that! Imagine the scandal if you were to marry one of us. They'd still be talking about it in a hundred years!"

"Indeed not," said Sextus. "But I am trying to decide what I want in a wife. And somehow, I don't think it's what you three have to offer."

Failing to realise that he had mortally offended the three women, he turned to his companions and said, "right, lads, let's go and see what Collatinus' wife has to offer."

They set off, and within less than an hour, arrived at Collatinus' modest house. They knocked, and the door was promptly opened by the steward, who greeted Collatinus formally. He introduced his companions, and then led them into the atrium, which was deserted.

"Where is your wife, Collatinus?"

"She will be where I said she would be," he replied. "In her room, working on her spinning."

They walked through the atrium to the residential part of the house. There, in the second cubicle on the left, was a woman, sat upright in a chair, distaff in her hand, spinning wool. Just as Collatinus had said she would be.

And it was clearly not a set-up: the whole room reflected this personality, as did the speed and efficiency with which she was working. She did not rise to greet them.

"Lucretia, these men are the King's sons: Sextus, Arruns, Titus and Gaius."

Now she rose and, putting her distaff to one side, bowed before them. "Your graces, I am sorry that you have found me working. Did the steward not take account of your needs? What can I do to make your comfortable? I apologise for not being ready to greet you, but I received no word."

"That," replied Sextus, "is because we sent no word. Deliberately. We wished to surprise you and find out in what manner you occupied yourself in the evenings. I am desirous of finding myself a wife, and was amazed when Collatinus told me what a paragon of virtue he had married. I refused to believe it until I had seen it for myself."

"How ungallant of you, sir," she replied formally. "I am sure that there is nothing exceptional about my behaviour, and if you were to drop in on any other woman in Rome this evening you would likewise find her occupied as I have been."

"Sadly, we have dropped in on three other women this evening - my brothers' wives, to be precise," answered Sextus, waving his arm around to indicate the three men to whom he referred, "and they were not so occupied. But rather, they were occupied in drinking wine and gossipping. It seems to me," he added darkly, "that that is not the kind of wife I want."

Turning to Collatinus, he added, "you are right, Collatinus. You have the most virtuous wife in Rome. I doubt I could find a better if I tried."

Two more weeks went by.

Two more boring weeks with nothing for Sextus to do but sit and drink and think about things. Collatinus didn't even seem to appreciate that he was now next in line for the throne. He just kept on doing his duties as the tribune that he was. It would be so easy for him to die when the time finally came to storm Ardea. And then Lucretia would be free for him to marry. Marriage between cousins wasn't exactly the done thing, but it was pretty much how all the royals had managed it for generations. Grandsons marrying granddaughters. It wasn't technically incest, just ... a little close.

Brutus came in to see if he needed anything. He waved him away. Of course, sometimes you ended up with idiots like Brutus from too much inbreeding. But what did that matter to him? He would be king; it didn't really matter what happened to his children. Although, maybe, if he didn't have any daughters ... this stupid tradition of the woman inheriting the right to the throne would die out and one of his sons could just inherit the throne with the power.

And over and over came the thought ... all he had to do was convince Lucretia to marry him in the event that Collatinus were to die, and then arrange for Collatinus to die. It was all so simple.

Arruns came in the room.

"I have been thinking," he began. "It is time I was married. And I have picked out a wife."

"You have?" said Arruns, amazed. "I didn't realise you had been away from the front."

"I have not. But I am going to go tonight and propose."

"To whom?" asked Arruns, incredulous. "Who is this paragon of virtue? Do I know her?"

"You do indeed," replied Sextus. "You met her when I did two weeks past. I have chosen Lucretia."

Arruns burst out laughing. "A good one, my brother. The only problem is that, as you observed, she is virtuous and, uh, already married."

"I do not see that as an obstacle," countered Sextus. "Her husband will only live as long as we are sat here doing nothing. The moment that we storm Ardea, he will die. On my sword if not someone else's."

Arruns looked at him in shock. Surely his brother could not be serious? But he seemed perfectly serious. He was going to kill a fellow officer? And the heir to the throne at that?

"I beg you, brother, give up this plan. It is sacrilege to kill the anointed of the gods. You will be damned for all eternity. This plan will not prosper."

Sextus looked at his brother, shocked. Could Arruns really not see the beauty of this plan? Had he spoken out of turn to the wrong man? Would he need to kill this brother to silence him as well?

"You are right, Arruns. It was just a passing fancy. Get our brothers and let us drink wine! Brutus!"

Brutus came immediately from where he had been waiting for a summons.

"Fetch wine, Brutus! We need to drink!"

Another week had gone by.

In spite of his brother's objections, Sextus still could not see any real flaws in his plan. He was sure he could arrange for Collatinus to die in the assault on Ardea, when it finally came. He was less sure what to do about Arruns, but if the wedding was a fait accompli, he would probably not say anything. He really just needed to go and see Lucretia and tell her what he had planned. It wasn't a long ride to Rome.

He left off thinking and took up acting. "Brutus! My riding cape, I'm going to Rome!"

Brutus brought the cape within minutes and Sextus was up and out of the tent, heading to the line of post horses near the camp entrance.

"Come on, man! I haven't got all day to stand here waiting for a horse!" he yelled at the postmaster.

This was the final stop before Rome, and they seemed to have run out of horses or something. It was so frustrating, he was so close. He knew that his plan was going to work, and that he would be charming Lucretia in no time at all.

Finally the man produced a horse. Sextus mounted it, and went on his way to Rome.

The steward opened the door.

"Greetings, Sextus Tarquinius," he intoned, respectfully. "How may I be of assistance to you, this evening?"

"I am here to see the lady of the house," replied Sextus, brusquely. "Is she in?"

The steward looked at him blankly. "Yes, she is in, why would she not be?"

"Then I would like to see her."

"I'm afraid that isn't possible, sir, without the master's permission. And he has not sent word."

"I am his word. Do you think I would have come without his consent?"

"I do not know, good sir. But I do know that he gave explicit instructions before he left for the war that nobody was to be permitted to enter the house without his instructions. I understand you came before, but you came with him; so that is his granting you permission. But I have not had word that you would be returning."

"Well, I have. Now go and fetch the Lady Lucretia before I kill you," he added threateningly, one had on his sword, ostentatiously displayed on his belt.

"Yes, sir," he said, summoning another slave to his side and instructing him silently to fetch the lady to the atrium.

Sextus moved into the atrium himself and looked around. As before, the house was kept in immaculate trim: yes, this was the woman for him.

And then she appeared.

In spite of the late time of day, and the fact that clearly nobody was expected, she was beautifully presented. Her hair and gown were perfect, and she had the perfect expression on her face: gently welcoming, submissive, yet quietly confident.

"How can I help you sir? Are you well? Is my husband well?"

And the perfect greeting! His heart soared. What a catch this woman was. But what to say about her husband.

"It was actually about your husband that I wished to speak to you, madam. Yes, he is well, but I am concerned that he will not be for much longer."

A flash of distress appeared on her face. "Why is that, good sir?"

"I believe he has formed a death wish. I think he feels guilt about the amount of time it has taken us to capture Ardea, and that his only option to assauge that guilt is to die during the storming of it."

She took this in, processing it. "And what would you have me do, sir?"

"Marry me."

She looked at him in total shock and disbelief. It was all she could do to stammer out a response. "I do not understand you, sir. You know that I am already married."

"Yes, to a complete loser who does not deserve to be called your husband, let alone King of Rome! He is an unwortthy pile of dung that doesn't understand what he has lucked into by marrying you. He is not worthy!"

"You appear to be somewhat overwrought, sir. May I suggest you leave before you say or do something you might come to regret?"

"Leaving would be the one thing I might come to regret, Lucretia. I have come to claim you for my wife, and my wife you will be."

"No, sir, that cannot be. I am married to Collatinus, and until he is dead, I am not free to marry another. Indeed, even if he were to die this evening, I would not be free to marry for another nine months to prove that I am not carrying his postumous child. Surely you know the law?"

Sextus lost his cool.

"Woman, you will be carrying my child by the time you see him next!"

Stepping forward, he pulled her elegant gown off her, and proceeded to rape her, right there in the atrium. She never stood a chance of resisting.

Brutus arrived at the house of Spurius Lucretius first.

"Good sir, I know you think of me as everyone else does - a mental defective - but that is just a part I have had to play to preserve my life from those 'royals' who would have threatened it. As, I believe, they are about to threaten the chastity of your daughter."

"What are you saying? Who would threaten my daughter when her husband is away?"

"Sextus Tarquinius. Possibly him alone. I heard him discussing it with his brother Arruns a few days ago, and I told Collatinus, but he laughed it off. Told me that it was just a joke at my expense. I considered that, and considered it was not worth breaking my disguise to contradict him. But this evening, he ordered me to find his riding cape, and then set off for Rome. I set off as soon after as I could, leaving a message for Collatinus to follow when he returned. I would expect him shortly."

At that moment, the messenger from Lucretia's house burst in.

"Your lords, I have grave news. Sextus Tarquinius just turned up at the lady's house and threatened the steward with death if he did not admit him. Considering it the lesser of two evils, he admitted him but sent me here with this message: ready yourselves and come at once to her rescue."

The two men exchanged glances. Would the two of them be enough? Where could they find more men to back them up at this time?

"Valerius, the son of my commander, Volesus, is at his home nearby," said Brutus. "We could call on him."

"And I will leave a message for Collatinus. If, indeed, he comes here, he will know the score. But I imagine he will ride straight for his own house."

"In that case, we had better hurry. We would not want him to encounter Sextus before we arrive to back him up."

In the end, they all arrived at around the same time.

Brutus was surprised to see that Arruns had arrived with Collatinus. But not as surprised as Arruns was to see the change that had come over Brutus. Where the fool had been, there was now a cool, competent commander. Collatinus, while not expecting the shift, was less surprised. He had never treated Brutus as a butt, and had felt sorry for the young man.

The steward admitted them to the house. And immediately kneeled on the floor in front of Collatinus.

"I am sorry, sir, that I could not keep the man from the house. I should, nevertheless have tried, even at the cost of my own life. I beg you, sir, once you have questioned me, put me to death for dereliction of duty."

"Get up, man!" said Collatinus, gently. "If I had realised that Sextus would come here with a sword, I would have retained some soldiers to protect you all. But it never occurred to me. Nor should it have occurred to you. But I am grateful that you lived. You were able to send a message to Spurius, here. And you are more use to me alive than dead. Tell me, where is the foul traitor?"

"He left, sir. When he had had his pleasure, he took his things and left."

"And where is my wife?"

"She is in her room, sir."

"Bring her here."

Lucretia came, eyes downcast. When she saw her husband and father in the atrium, she gave a little start, and then dropped to her knees and buried her face in her hands.

"Forgive me, lord, for my body has betrayed me - and I have betrayed you. Sextus Tarquinius came here last night, and against my will, enjoyed my body. And when he had taken his pleasure, he left. He said that he wanted to marry me, but such as he will never marry me.

"I swear to the truth of all this, and call upon the god Mors to be my witness."

So saying, she withdrew a dagger from the fold of her gown and plunged it into her heart.

"No!" cried Collatinus, darting forward to hold her. "You do not need to do this. I believe you!"

"If you believe me, then hunt this tyrant down. And his father who made him so. Let none other be tyrant over Rome so long as Rome shall live."

As he looked into her eyes, the light slowly went out of them. And with Lucretia, the goddess of the KIngs of Rome, died too.

The goddess Carmenta was dead.
