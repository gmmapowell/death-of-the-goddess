33 Titans

A week later, as the moon shrank to a half, Zeus led the men out to hunt.

As the sun set, they gathered in the fields west of the village to honour the Sun as it fell. Then they turned to the east, watching the stars turn overhead as they waited for the moon to rise. As it rose, they stood, then fell on their knees in reverence.

               

Oh, moon, we see your face!        

Oh, moon, we hear your voice!        

Oh, moon, grant us good hunting!        

Oh, moon, watch over us!

And then, with the pale silver light of the half moon, Zeus led his band of warriors northwards into the forests.

Here was the traditional power of men. Here, away from the village and the power of the Goddess, men could find themselves and know that they, too, had a place in the world. The Goddess had corrupted the natural order of things: this planting of seeds, the management of fields, the watering and nurturing of plants, all this Zeus and his men knew to be unnatural. In the natural order of things there were men and beasts: there were the weak and the strong. The strong took what they wanted - and the weak died. Zeus knew in his very bones that this was the case.

But in the village, all the men felt weak. And they did not want to be weak.

So, not in secret but also not in the open, they carried on their communion with the Titans. Not a secret society, but an inner cadre of the chosen, they crept out of the village at night, always at the final quarter of the moon, to hunt and to celebrate the rites of the Titans.

Who were the Titans? Zeus did not know. He knew what his father had told him, which his father had told him, which he had had from some of his ancestors, men all: that once, they had lived in the mountains, far to the north, and life had been different there. In the north, it was cold, and Goddess did not like the cold, so she did not go there. In the north, man needed fire to keep warm, and there were Titans who managed the fire. Some of them had mountains that were alive in fire, and they forged bronze under their mountains and made weapons and armour like nothing else that had been seen in the world. And they shared their fire and food and weapons with any man who would acknowledge their strength and power and share with them.

So even here, in the south, where the Goddess reigned supreme and without equal, Zeus and his fellow hunters and warriors still kept up the rituals of communion with the Titans. Who were they? Zeus did not know, but he knew they had power and they loved men - not women. And that was enough for Zeus - and all his companions - needed to know in order to leave their homes for the night and go hunting and feasting.

Apollo was the first to see the boar.

It had finished eating and drinking - probably hours before - and had nestled into a thicket a bowshot away from the lake where it had no doubt been drinking the evening before. An obvious hiding place, and they had followed its tracks away from the lake.

A few glimmers of moonlight shone down here and there through the forest, but their eyes had all long since adapted to the gloom. Zeus had to admit that Apollo had very sharp eyesight in order to be able to spot the boar in the middle of those bushes - a black animal hidden in a dark place with almost no light to see by.

The crouched down while the others gathered around, looking for the boar; most of them not seeing it, but knowing that it must be there, in front of them, where Zeus and Apollo were looking so intently.

They had done this together many times before; they worked and trained together in the army; they had planned and discussed this on the march up here; in short, they knew what they were doing. When they were all squatting in a small group behind Zeus and Apollo, Zeus turned and tapped each on the shoulder and indicated what he wanted them to do. Quickly, effortlessly and most importantly silently, the hunters spread out around the thicket.

Time passed.

The angle of the moonlight slowly shifted as they waited, knowing that it was critical for everyone to be in place before anything started to happen. Suddenly the stillness of the night was broken by the distinctive call of a grebe. Zeus smiled. The boar might just think it was a bird on the lake, but he knew the intonation that told him that Deiophobos was responsible for the bird call. He was ready, and since he had gone the furthest, everyone else should be ready. If nobody made an answering bird call, Deiophobos would charge the boar in a few dozen heartbeats.

The time dragged. Zeus and Apollo were alert and ready, their spears facing forward, expecting the boar to break free of the thicket and charge in their direction - away from the attack. Remaining alert - listening for every little sound in the almost pitch black - for an extended period was wearing, but their adrenaline was fired up and they were ready.

Suddenly, there was a clattering on the far side of the thicket as Deiophobos launched into action. Shortly after him, two or three of the others followed suit and came charging towards the thicket, intentionally making all the noise they could manage. The time for silence and stealth had gone for them and their only thought was to stampeded the bull into flight - ideally away from them and towards the silent part of the forest - where Zeus and Apollo waited with spears planted.

From the thicket they heard a grunting, then the noise of the boar starting to move. It stood, listening and sniffing the air. It was too wise and experienced to just charge off mindlessly - it wanted to know what was out there first. But Deiophobos kept the pressure on, coming from upwind and making as much noise as he could. When we was close enough, he threw his spear into the thicket, striking the boar a glancing blow.

Anyone who trained with the army knew that a thrown spear was almost never going to cause a serious injury. If you wanted to hurt someone with a spear - even one with a very sharp bronze point - you had to move up close and personal, plant your feet and thrust with all of your strength - or theirs. But thrown spears had one key benefit - in battle or in the hunt: it takes a great deal of discipline to be on the receiving end and know that you can almost always just "bat away" a spear. Everybody else - and all animals - will sense they are under attack and flee.

The boar abandoned caution, turned and fled out of the thicket, away from the noise, the stench of human and the violence of a thrown spear. It charged out into the relatively open forest and through patches of moonlight. As it did so, it saw something glinting ahead: it did not know what it saw, but one thing went through its animal brain: danger.

Zeus and Apollo were ready, waiting to take the onrushing boar on their spears, planted in the ground to give maximum resistance and to allow the boar to simply kill itself rushing onto them. This was the "pure" kill, the one that showed that the Titans favoured this hunt; that they themselves wanted to partake of its flesh.

At the last moment, it turned. While it did not know what the spears were, it knew that danger lay that way and turned sharply right, just in time. As it did so, one of its hind legs flicked out, kicking at Apollo's spear and cutting itself on the flank. Now irritated beyond measure, it found its feet and charged forwards, across the line of waiting spearmen - the worst possible outcome for the hunters.

Zeus and Apollo responded immediately. Grasping their spears, they pulled them out of the ground and turned and charged after the boar. Zeus was on it in a flash and drove his spear with all his strength into its rump. On the other side, Apollo thrust his spear into the boar's flank. Neither blow was mortal; moreover the boar was no longer charging directly at any of the other men. A chase was now inevitable.

Memnon reacted too slowly. Further down the line, he had the time to prepare: move his spear and replant it in the line of the boar. But he was caught in indecision: should he try and kill the boar or should he flee and then chase? By the time he had decided, the boar was on him, kicking his spear away and trampling over Memnon without a thought. Flung on his back, the boar's left hind leg caught him full in the chest, planting its whole weight on him as it pushed off, crushing his chest. His life flowed out of him as he gasped for breath, the blood flowing onto the ground.

It was nearly dawn by the time they had dragged the dead boar out of the forest onto the plain.

Once the boar had been caught and killed, they had divided into two groups: a smaller group had set to with axes, chopping and collecting firewood. The larger group had gathered around the boar, dragging it and at times carrying it out of the forest. By the time they arrived at the prepared site, the fire was already roaring. Quickly some of the men went to work, cutting and dressing the boar. The choicest cuts, along with the heart and blood, were dedicated to the Titans. Set to one side, they would be the first offerings when the sun rose from his slumbers and showed his face. In this southern land, the Sun was almost the only reminder of the fire they held in their ancestral memories.

As the sky to the east became light, everything was finally ready. The men drew up in a half circle behind their fire, facing east. With the hunting moon directly overhead, the Sun boiled up over the horizon and they greeted it warmly. It was time for the feast to begin!

               

To you, O Sun, ball of fire!        

To you, Titans of Sky and Air!        

To you, Titans of Fire!        

To you, Titans who Rule the Sky!                        

We welcome you to our Feast!        

We welcome you in our Hearts!        

Eat, Drink and be Merry!        

Join us for our Revels!

"Dionysus! Give the Titans a drink!" Dionysus came forward with an amphora filled with that nectar of the Gods - wine - and poured it into the flames. The flames drank it up with an audible relish.

"Apollo! Feed the Titans!" Apollo stepped forward and cast the heart and the entrails of the boar into the fire and watched them be consumed.

"Give the Strength of this boar to the Titans," Zeus shouted the third time, and then stepped forward, picking up the cup containing the boar's blood. With reverence, he poured it onto the fire.

Bowing before the fire, with the Sun illuminating it from behind, Zeus spread his hands wide in homage.

"We have given you drink, O Titans! We have given you meat, O Titans! We have given you the strength of a boar, O Titans! Now come down and join us in this feast."

The men set up the spit over the fire and began to roast the carcass of the boar. As they did so, Dionysus went around, giving each man a cup of wine to drink.

It was good to hunt, thought Zeus, feeling the spirit of the Titans waft through him. This was what it meant to be a man.
