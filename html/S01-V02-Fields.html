2. In the Fields

"I do love this time of year," said Pip, leaning on his hoe for a few moments. "The weather, the promise of spring, the simple rhythm of hoeing. This is the life, I always say."

Polo stopped his own hoeing and looked up at him. "I know what you mean. After those months of shivering in the huts with almost nothing to do, it's good to get out and be busy. And it's great when the sun shows her face earlier every day and for longer. And really good that her sisters are back up before her. It's going to be a good year, I can feel it in my bones."

"Says the man who said the winter was going to be mild," chuckled Pip, starting to hoe up the weeds between the barley shoots again. "There were times there where I thought I was going to freeze to death in that hut with only the four of us to keep each other warm."

They continued to hoe for a little while in companionable silence, basking in the warmth of the early spring sunshine. They heard the noise of footsteps running towards them from the village and turned to look. A girl was running towards them from the village. She slowed down as she approached.

"Hullo, Sprig," called Polo. "What message do you bring from the Ladies?" He waited a few moments while Sprig recovered her breath.

"Tamarisk reports that a lion has been seen to the south; all men are commanded to help her immediately." Having left her message, Sprig ran off to the next pair of men in the field to pass on the same message. Immediately, Polo and Pip took their hoes and ran off to find Tamarisk.

Twelve men had gathered around Tamarisk, who was standing on the rock which served as an observation platform to the south of the village, by the time Pip and Polo arrived. Tamarisk turned to address them all. "We saw a lion stalking to the south here," and pointed her arm in that direction. "Obviously her intentions have not been revealed to us, but we have to assume she and her cubs are hungry and that we look like good food to her."

She looked around the assembled men. "We must disabuse her of this notion."

Quickly, she organised the men. They might only be men, and dispensable, but nevertheless she wanted to keep as many of them alive as possible. And, most importantly, the lion had to be turned away - or killed - before she dared to attack the settlement. So much depended on the lion.

Four of the men she sent to the right, four to the left. The other six she sent heading directly towards the lion. As others arrived she would deploy them as need dictated.

Polo and Pip ended up leading the central group heading straight at the lion. Tamarisk had confidence in their ability and experience. While they were not so old as to have started losing their strength, they were old and wise by the standards of the village. They probably could not remember the number of fierce animals they had faced off in their lives. Those sent round the edges, meanwhile, had less experience, and while they might come into the fight, were really there to watch and learn from the sidelines.

By now, the big cat had settled down into the long grass. Pip took this as a good sign. When lions were serious about killing prey, they tended to hunt in packs - as he and Polo were now. One animal - however fierce - was never a match for a group. Pip and Polo had ducked down the moment they had left Tamarisk and, along with the other four men from the village were hurrying forward in a crouch. They were not realistically attempting to catch the lion by surprise - she had to know they were there - but the best possible result was to put the lion to flight. And, Pip knew from experience, she was more likely to flee if the noise, light and people appeared suddenly from the grass. She might attack, but even so, it would be a reaction rather than a planned attack.

Pip raised his hand to signal a halt. The other five men with him paused in mid-stride. He held out his left hand and jerked it. Dirk and Buddy started moving again, but now circling to the left. With his hoe, he gestured to the right and Finn and Goldie ran that way. Pip gave them a few moments to get into position, then looked Polo in the eyes and yelled "Charge".

Polo bounced up, waving his hoe for all he was worth and sprang forward. As he had looked in Pip's eyes, he had seen the fear there: a fear that mirrored his own. As often as they had done this - and Pip and Polo had led numerous such charges over the past two seasons - they had yet to take so much as a scratch. But there was something atavistically primitive about going up against a wild animal. They would lie about it afterwards to the women they wanted to impress with their merit, claim that they were as calm as the evening breeze, but the fact was from the moment they had started moving towards the lion, their hearts had been racing.

"Waaawaaawaawaa," Polo yelled, charging forward. He couldn't see the lion yet, but he trusted Pip's eyesight and judgment about where the lion was: jump too early and they gave the lion too long to consider its options. Too late, and the lion could spring on them before they were set. Against the lion's strength, weight, teeth and claws all they had were some feeble hoes - and their wits, courage and experience.

From the sides, they heard the other four charge forward, also braying at the top of their lungs. By offering a wide front to the lion, the intention was to get it to turn and run back to the south - away from the village. If any of the men failed to be intimidating enough, it could easily see a weak link and attack that way - and that way lay disaster.

There it was! Pip had become concerned he had misjudged the position of the lion and leaped too early. But no, it was where he had thought it was, but concealed by both the grass and a small bush. The lion, of course, used its sense of smell much more than its sense of sight for stalking prey. Pointing his hoe forward to indicate that he had a bead of the lion, he shouted louder and ran faster - mainly to scare the lion but also to give an outlet to his fear.

The lion pricked her ears up and looked at them. Her head, then her body, came up and she looked at them, baring her teeth. This was the decision time: if they faltered, she would decide that they were prey and attack; if they dominated the situation, she would run. Pip threw his head back and started to sing: there was nothing like singing to cover your own fear as you crossed those final yards into glory or oblivion.

The lion blinked first, turned and trotted off. Not out of fear; merely thwarted. There would be easier game elsewhere.

"It was nothing," boasted Wayne, later in the day. "We shook our rakes at it, and it ran off. Lions are supposed to be dangerous, but I don't see it."

Sprig was impressed. She had not seen the lion, but she remembered times when other lions had killed some of the men of the village. Wayne must be very brave to have not felt any fear.

"Don't pay him no mind, dearie," said Hyacinth, her mother. "Men like to think they are important because they scare the occasional lion. But none of that matters. How would they live without food from our fields? Where would they sleep if we did not give them shelter? What would they drink from if Auntie Chrys didn't make the pots? And where would they be buried? Really, apart from that, what are men good for?"

Wayne dropped his eyes, abashed. He had forgotten the Lady was here, focused as he was on the girl, a couple of seasons younger than him, with whom he had enjoyed playing while they grew up. Now he was counted a man, with a full load of work in the fields as well as his occasional duties chasing lions or fighting other villagers. She was still a girl although, to look at her, she would come of age at some point in the coming season. The goddess willing, of course. Always, everything with the permission of the goddess.

To cover his embarrassment, Wayne turned back to the task in hand - raking away any stones from around the barley sprouts. He was never quite sure where the stones came from; more seemed to appear every day - but that too was probably the work of the Goddess. Although why such a benign, caring and loving deity would give them stones baffled him. But that was another thing that was in the hands of the Ladies - he was never likely to come into contact with the Goddess himself. Unless he was chosen in one of the dances at one of the ceremonies, it was unlikely that his life would ever reach a higher pinnacle than maybe one day become the lion Chaser.

It looked easy: it was all very well for Pip and Polo to say that there was skill and nuance involved. And all very well for Tamarisk to say that you worked your way up, observing and learning as a Fielder before you could become a Surrounder and then, possibly, if you didn't die first, a Chaser.

He bent down and picked up the stones they had extracted and put them in his bucket. It was nearly half full now; Hyacinth would not let him go back to the village until it was full - unless it became dark first, of course. Even now, the days were quite short and it was an effort to finish all the work in the fields before the Sun set, leaving the sky to her younger sisters.

Hyancinth had wandered off to do one of those "administrative" tasks that the Ladies did - not real work, for sure, not like picking up these stones. He glanced up to see Sprig. He gave her a quick grin and was pleased to see that she returned him a shy smile. He was sure she liked him. He felt a faint swelling and reminded himself she was still a girl - and too young to do that, even if she did feel that way about him. No, best not to think that way and get on with moving these stones. He picked up his rake again and moved on to the next patch. Sprig followed him.

"A little faster," said Chrysanthemum, holding the wet clay firmly, but not too tightly, as it rotated under her hands. She made it look easy but anyone who had tried it knew what a difficult challenge it could be. Hank had attempted to mould a pot on a couple of occasions when Chrysanthemum had wanted to discipline him - and had done nothing except get the clay everywhere. He obediently moved his hands a little faster - turning the wheel as evenly as he could with all his years of experience. Even this task - designated as sufficiently unskilled for even a man to carry it out - still required considerably more skill than hoeing up weeds in the fields.

As the wheel started to turn more quickly, Chrysanthemum shaped the delicate neck of the flask. The gentle curvature had to not only be functional, but pleasing to the Goddess. Without that, no joy could come to those who drank from this vessel. It was often here that even the most perfect piece of pottery would fall down and be scrapped. As senior potter, it was her job to make sure the work of the others was up to scratch: and she could hardly criticise others if she did not do a perfect job herself. Yes! That was coming together nicely.

Across the room, Anthurium was applying glaze to a pot that Chrysanthemum had finished this morning when the panic with the lion was over. While Hank was too old to be rushing off to fight lions - or people - the disturbance was still enough to distract everyone in the village from what they should have been doing. Fortunately, no damage had come to the pot with the interruption. While a less delicate job than actually casting the pot, glazing was a specialist task. By her side, her daughter Petal was watching and learning. Sometimes, she would practise herself on shards of discarded pots, although her formal apprenticeship would not start until she became a woman in her own right.
