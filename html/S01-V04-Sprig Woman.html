4. Sprig

"Mother, I'm bleeding."

"We must go to Bougainvillaea that she may recognise you in the face of the Goddess."

"Pastinaca!" she called, and the woman came out from the hut behind them. "Take this newly minted woman and her mother to the fallow field, and show her what is necessary. Make sure she understands all that is involved in being a woman and make sure she is ready by the Waning Festival."

Pastinaca bowed and turned away, signalling to mother and daughter that they should follow her out to the field that this year was being rested in service of the Goddess. In this field was the blood split. The blood of men sent to the Goddess, and the blood of women freely given when they were not impregnated by the river or the wind.

They followed her in silence, walking hand in hand to the field which Sprig would enter and somebody else - a woman with a different name and a different future - would leave.

"Remove your dress and lie down," Pastinaca instructed the woman who had been known as Sprig. "I don't know how much you and your mother have prepared for this moment, but your life has just begun. As a nut is not the tree, as the seed is not the barley, so the girl Sprig was not the woman you have become. Only now, does your life truly begin." She lifted the pitcher of water she had brought with her, and, instructing the girl to open her legs wide, poured the water over her, giving the life-nourishing blood and water back to the ground.

"This is the field of unseeded women. Every month, if you have not accepted the seed of the river or the wind, you will bleed as you are now. When you feel the bleeding start, you will come here and share the blood with the soil. When it is day, you will lie like this in the fields, sharing of your bounty with the field. When it is night, you will retreat to the hut in the middle of the field with the other women in the field. When your bleeding is over, you may return to the village.

"This is not a punishment. In her wisdom, the Goddess has chosen that some women will be fertile and have children; other women, unblessed with children, will offer their fertility to the fields. In years to come, the crops will grow in this field and will thank you. Know that all this is part of the cycle of life. In time, as you give back to the Goddess through these fields, she will reward you with children of the river or the wind. When your time comes, you will see that this is no more a punishment than that is.

"But, as this is your first time, it is your right to have another woman of your choice to stay with you and guide you through what will become commonplace to you over the course of your life. Your mother may stay, if you wish, or you can ask for another. And while you are here, we want you to meditate on two things: the gifts that you have received today; and what name you would choose for the woman you have become."

Sprig looked up at her. "I thank you most kindly for your wise instruction, Pastinaca. I would have my mother by my side at this time, as she has been for my sisters before me. While I am sure the others will miss her, I am also sure that they can cope for these few days by themselves."

"In that case, I will leave you to your meditations and discussions. As a Woman, you are expected at the festivals of Women, the first of which will be a few days after you return from here. At the waning festival, it will be expected that you dance with a man. You have seen the ritual before, but now it would be a good time to discuss with your mother any aspects of that ritual which seem unclear to you."

And she left them alone.

The waxing festival took place at sunrise; the waning festival correspondingly took place at sunset. In each case, the half moon was directly overhead at the start of the festival. As always, the villagers had collected wood for a fire and, on this occasion, Sprout, as one of the Keepers of the Flame, was responsible for bringing a flaming branch from the Sacred and Eternal Fire to light it. Bougainvillaea knew that the flame was not as eternal as was claimed; indeed, within her lifetime she remembered having had to go on searches for a wild fire to collect to relight the Sacred Flame. For Sacred it certainly was - whenever it had gone out, it had heralded disaster: not to mention that it had led directly to disasters of a more minor sort. Without fire, food could not be cooked; huts could not be warmed; pottery could not be fired. No, fire was as essential to life as was water, barley or figs. As much as for illumniation, the fire was lit at the great festivals to celebrate the fact that the Goddess had shared the gift of fire with them. And a gift it remained, to be nourished, cherished and preserved; or else to be lost and removed, possibly next time for ever.

Bougainvillaea stepped forward from the circle of villagers into the firelight and, as was her right, began to dance, swaying with the rhythms that were in her head. As she did, some of the women picked up her motions on the hollow drums that they had. The beat became more insistent and started to dictate - rather than follow - the dance. Then other women came in on the flutes. The music picked up and swirled around itself. As the dance continued, Bougainvillaea pointed to other women and called them out into the circle. They, too, began dancing and swirling around the fire in time to the music. The music swelled and grew, and now men joined in as well, slapping their thighs and stamping their feet, getting themselves ready for what they knew was coming - and might, or might not, involve them.

As the moon slowly drifted across the sky, all the women were eventually called to the dance. As the musicians were called, they abandoned their instruments and joined in the swaying and gyrating. Now the music was coming from bodies - stamping, slapping, singing.

Suddenly Bougainvillaea let out an ear-splitting shriek and threw her head back. Everything stopped and the field went silent except for the crackling of the fire. The crackling continued until Bougainvillaea broke it with another shriek:

"Clematis. Clematis, come here!"

Clematis moved from her where she had been dancing to Bougainvillaea's right and stood in front of her, facing her. Bougainvillaea started to move again, beginning the same sinuous dance. Clematis watched her, followed her movements, copied her. In unison they danced together; then the others slowly joined in, all the women moving and singing in a circle around the fire. Outside the circle of women, the men were now on their feet, dancing, moving, circling in the opposite direction around the fire and the women.

"Choose!" hissed Bougainvillaea.

The moment was upon her. In some sense, she felt that she had been preparing for this moment for years: but certainly she had been readying herself for it, alone in the fields for the past quarter moon, thinking about how, who, when, what it would be like. And now was the time. The time of choosing. Except her choice had already been made. And there he was. As the circles moved, she reached out her hand to point. Wayne danced past and she pointed to the man who came after him: Shep.
