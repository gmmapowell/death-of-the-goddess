12. Servius Tullius

Servius Tullius was seven years old when his father died in a fire.

Servius fled the house and, although burned, managed to find a puddle big enough to extinguish the fire. He rolled around in it until all the flames were out, and he was quite muddy.

His father had been adlected into the senate as a grandson of Tullus Hostilius; and it had been expected that Servius would follow in his father's footsteps and become a senator in his turn. But that now seemed in doubt.

"Obviously we must adopt the poor boy," began Tanaquil, with that faraway expression on her face that Lucumo had come to know so well. But even he was taken aback by what came next.

"Otherwise how will he now receive the education and wisdom he will need to marry our daughter?"

It was on the tip of Lucumo's tongue to point out that they didn't actually have a daughter yet, but then he stopped. If she could see far enough into the future to know that this poor orphan was going to marry their daughter, she presumably knew just when said child was going to be born.

"Our second daughter," she clarified, cementing his opinion. "Not the one that I'm carrying at the moment."

He gaped at her. She was pregnant? How long had she known? How far along was she?

"I'm a couple of weeks late," she said. "I've been suspicious for a few days now, but I went to see the priestess this morning and she confirmed it."

"And it will be a daughter?" he asked.

"Indeed so," she responded. "A beautiful little thing. But of no consequence, since in a couple of years, we will have another. And Carmenta travels through the youngest daughter, always."

Her gift still left him bemused. How could she know so much of what was to come? Still, she did, and he certainly found it useful. What she said next, though, left him cold.

"Beware the farmers making a ruckus."

Servius grew into a wise and introspective young man.

At the age of thirty, he married Lucumo's younger daughter, Tarquinia the younger, just after her eighteenth birthday. From that moment on, Lucumo knew he was living on borrowed time. The thing was, he didn't just not know that he could trust his wife; he knew that he could not trust her. In fact, she had told him explicitly that when Tarquinia became pregnant, "your life will be measured in days, rather than weeks, and Servius will take your place."

When he had countered that one way to stop that would be to kill the young whippersnapper first, she had laughed and advised him "against trying it": "If you try to fool the gods, you merely show yourself up as the fool."

Lucumo never met that grandchild. In fact, he never even found out that his younger daughter was pregnant.

One day, when he was hearing cases in his throne room, two farmers came in, arguing about a border fence. They wanted him to rule on their case. The dispute had been going on for some years, it seemed, dating back to their parents' time.

"My father was scared of his father," one explained. "Rather than call him out, or go to the magistrate, he just let him take more and more of our land, until it was obvious to everyone around that he had moved the stones."

"Not so," countered the other. "His father was a gambler, and when he could not pay his debts, he ceded us land in lieu of payment."

"And do you have evidence of this? Did he formally hand over the land in a notarised document?" asked Lucumo.

"No, of course not," responded the farmer. "He knew what he was doing, and so did his family. Note that it's only after he's dead that they are trying it on."

"And you, young man," said Lucumo, turning to the other farmer. "Do you have proof of title to the land you claim? Notarised by a presiding magistrate?"

"Indeed so," he responded. "Along with the survey indicating the original positions of the boundary stones. I have it here," he added, patting a scroll.

Lucumo motioned to a clerk. "Pending review of these documents, I am forced to find for the plaintiff. Case dismissed."

"Outrage!" yelled the loser. "How can you ignore the truth of what I have to say! I swear it before all the Lares!" And he struck the other farmer across the face.

While Lucumo and his guards were distracted, Servius reached behind him, picked up an axe, and wielded it firmly, burying it in Lucumo's head.

The gasps around the room told the whole story. Even the fracas between and around the two farmers ceased.

"I am now the King," intoned Servius.

"All hail the King!" shouted everyone in the room in unison.

Servius was as good a king as it had seemed he might be.

Some kings revel in the power; some in the glory; some just want to make war; others want to be adored. But a few, a very small minority, are actually interested in the work of ruling: the act of making the city run more smoothly. Numa had been such a one; and Servius Tullius was another.

Once he had power, he knew what he was going to do with it. Yes, he carried on fighting battles, but only when he was attacked. But his main goal was to transform the city. It took him all the forty-odd years of his reign, but he succeeded.

First and foremost, he made the city more defensible by encircling it with one, complete wall. Up until now, each of the various hills of Rome had had their own walls, leaving the valleys - the forum, the Circus Maximus, and so on - exposed to enemy predations. His wall encircled all of the city, including the seven hills and all the valleys in between. Where he could, he built the wall slightly up the hills to add defensive strength to them; where he could not, he built the walls higher to compensate. He built the walls of tufa stone: a volcanic rock, it was easy to mine, shape and transport. When fitted together, the stones had a natural sponginess that resisted attack by battering ram or artillery. It was his life's work: over thirty years spent in the construction of walls which, he was sure, were the most impressive a city had had since Troy.

But the walls by themselves were not enough to guarantee the city could be defended. For that, he needed an army. So he conducted a thorough census of the people, grouping them into classes based on their wealth and ability to equip themselves for war. Their wealth, of course, told him how much they had to defend - and thus their incentive to defend it; their ability to equip themselves for war told him how they could defend themselves.

The richest he put to work as cavalry: only the richest in a city like Rome could afford to keep a warhorse, let alone two. The next richest he enrolled as heavy infantry, requiring them to equip themselves with javelins, sword, shield, helmet and body armour. Below that, he appointed others to be pikemen, called upon in emergencies to defend the walls, but only needing a simple pike with which to defend their stretch of wall. Below them, he had slingers and artillery operators. And finally, in the fifth class, he had the non-combatant support staff, responsible for all the day-to-day logistics of supporting an army: carters, bakers, artificers and so on. And everybody knew their job, and what was expected of them.

Although Servius himself was unimpressed by war, he knew the importance of discipline in preparing for war, and the value of preparation when it came to war. And so, he required all the men in the city to carry out their appropriate drills at least twice per month. Servius organised the rotas by which they were drilled, and made sure that in addition to each century drilling together, they trained multiple units together: cavalry and infantry together; heavy infantry with the slingers; and so on. Servius turned what had been a very effective armed state into a war machine.

But a state is only worth defending if it is worth living in. And, Servius had concluded early in his reign, Rome could be a shithole - literally. As the city had grown, so had the distance that bodily excretions needed to be carried to dispose of them sensibly - to wit, on the fields where they would help the crops to grow. A problem that Servius recognised would only get worse as he enclosed the city in a big wall.

The solution came to him one day as he was standing, looking at the Tiber, effortlessly flowing and carrying away all manner of detritus: branches, leaves, even a dead cow. He turned to his colleague, Claudius, and asked him how it worked.

"How does what work, Servius?" responded Claudius, perplexed.

"Look at all that muck just moving," he responded. "Nobody is carrying it; nobody is loading it into carts; it is just flowing. How does that work?"

"The river carries it as it flows out to the sea. Just like if you were trying to swim across it."

"So could we not do the same with our bodily wastes? Wash them away in a river?"

"We could, but it would be a long way to come to the river every time a man wanted a piss," pointed out Claudius. "Some men might only just make it back home by the time they needed another."

"But, what if we built our own river," contended Servius, trying to see through the fog of his own thoughts. "We could build a channel through the forum down to the river."

After a number of experiments, they realised that what they needed to do was to tunnel under the ground, at a level which caused the effluent to flow naturally, without being forced, but without coming out in a rush. They built a number of interconnecting tunnels, all flowing into the Tiber. Any farmer who wanted to avail himself of this fertilizer just needed to come to the outflow and collect as much as he wanted. Any that was left over would flow out into the Tiber and down to the sea.

One day, a number of representatives of the Sabine cities came to see him.

"It is many years ago now that we last had disputations with you, O King of Rome," they intoned. "And in the years that have since gone by, we have become much more one people. And now, the threats from the Etruscans seem to grow, possibly drawn on by your increasing assertiveness. That you see this too cannot be denied - look at the walls that are going up around your city."

"All this is true," remarked Servius. "While we do not fear the Etruscans, we recognize that we have made them jealous of us and our power. All the more so since my father-in-law was Etruscan. I think they felt that he should perhaps have done more to turn the balance of power in their favour."

"And yet, we hear that you are considering marrying your daughter to Lucumo's grandson, Not so?"

"I don't think I have any choice. Lucius is my finest general, and he is popular among both the Senate and People. Does this worry you?"

"Not overtly. But marrying your youngest daughter, that will make him king one day, will it not? And we fear that. We fear that he will be what the Greeks call a tyrannos: a king in love with the power, not the duty. And we fear that your daughter will not have it in her to resist his urges and impulses. That he will become even more proud, even more arrogant, and that he will dictate more than he will consult.

"In short, we would much rather have your other son-in-law, Spurius Lucretius, to be our king. But we know that that is not how the inheritance works."

"Short of killing or locking my daughter Servia Tertia up as a Vestal Virgin for the rest of her life, no, it cannot work that way. As you say, the goddess Carmenta inhabits the soul of the youngest living daughter. You know, since the tradition is more yours than ours, that older daughters only come into the inheritance once the younger daughters are dead, possibly even going back generations. Although Carmenta only knows what happens when there are no daughters left to be found anywhere."

"The line dies out; there can be no kings; and usually the city dies."

"Then let us make that the fate of Veii instead of Rome."

The battle for Veii was not over in a season.

In fact, Veii did not fall to either Servius Tullius or Lucius Tarquinius in their lifetimes. In fact, it was to remain a thorn in the flesh of the Romans for over a hundred years.

The battle to be King of Rome was a lot shorter. Lucius Tarquinius married Servia Tertia before the campaign started; by the end of the year, he was challenging Servius openly.

"I say that the campaign against Veii failed because of the lack of leadership shown by the King. I challenge you for the right to be King," he challenged in the Senate House. "Fight me or admit that you are craven."

"Why would I fight you, my son-in-law?" countered Servius. "All know that you will be King after me; wait, be patient, use the time to build support amongst your fellow Senators; to hone your skills on the battlefield. And then, when my race is run, I will depart gracefully and leave you to be King."

"What will be left of my inheritance, then? You will have frittered it away by fighting losing battles against Veii and other Etruscans. Rome will be but a shell of what it is now."

There was an audible intake of breath around the Senate House. Many of the Senators had themselves been at Veii, and had seen the tactics employed - and who had been in charge of deciding them. And it was not Servius. For Lucius to accuse him of betrayal - and in public - was horrifying. And yet, did anybody dare say anything? Almost whatever happened, this was going to be their next king.

Servius thought and considered. If he accepted the challenge now, he would be as strong as he would ever be. If he won the challenge, he would be secure from another challenge. On the other hand, if he lost ...

He looked around the Senate House and saw what was on every face. They would lose respect for him if he refused the challenge. And more: they feared the man who would replace him. Only one thing to do then, accept the challenge and win, ideally killing his own daughter's husband ...

"I accept this challenge. Tomorrow at dawn in the forum."

Dawn came late in those cold, dark days of December.

The people still called them the days of death, even though the Numa had systemized the calendar centuries ago, filling in the months November, December, January and February, along with the occasionally necessary Mercedonius, when February ended before the Pleiades rose in the sky.

It was also cold and frosty; unusually so. As Servius looked around, it considered it an omen. And not a good one. Hades was cold as well, they said, and it seemed like this was going to be his introduction to Hades. He marched into the forum, openly unafraid but inside the doubt was gnawing at him. Could he best his son-in-law? Could he bring himself to kill him? How could he have been so blind, even after the clear warnings from the Sabine ambassadors?

Tarquin was waiting for him; and now, in this moment, Servius saw him as he really was. Maybe it was one of those premonitions that they say come just before death, but Servius saw the tyrannos arrayed before him. The cruelty was etched on Tarquin's face. This man before Servius wanted to kill him; had, for many years been building up to just this moment.

Servius relaxed. Now he saw what was in the mind of his younger rival, he had no qualms or doubts: it was a simple matter of kill or be killed, just like it often had been in the front lines of battle. There were no more moral issues to consider.

Spurius Lucretius stepped up to officiate.

"This is a bout to first blood for the right to be King of Rome. The challenge weapon is the sword. No missile weapons, shields or armour are permitted. Go with Jupiter."

Servius drew his sword and watched as Tarquin did the same. And then waited. And waited.

"You challenged me, remember?" Servius taunted. "Having second thoughts?"

"Not at all," replied Tarquin, and launched into a quick, vicious, lunging attack.

Servius had not fought in the front lines for thirty years without having learnt to defend himself. Of course, battle in the front lines was different to single combat: you had armour, a shield, and comrades on either side of you to protect you. But a sword was still a key part of your defensive posture.

The battle - for that was what it was - raged for five minutes or more, relentless and exhausting, until both men were breathing hard. Tarquin suddenly backed up, and launched an attack on Servius' exposed left side. As he turned to meet the challenge, Servius slipped on a patch of frost. Tarquin's blow went through Servius' guard and clipped him, drawing blood. Technically, this was a win, but before anyone could react, Tarquin altered the direction of his thrust and drove his sword through his opponent's neck.

There was a muffled cheer, then a shout of "Long Live the King". But none of it was enthusiastic.
