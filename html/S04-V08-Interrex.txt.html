46 Interrex

They decided to call the time between the kings an "interregnum". Practical people, Romans.

And, in lieu of a king, they appointed an interrex - a king between the kings. They chose Gaius Julius Proculus as interrex on account of his age - he had basically already rejected the top job.

"But," he warned them, "I will only do the job for a few days while we sort out how we are going to choose a new king. And to cover up the murder of Romulus."

"He was not murdered!" countered Fabius. "I killed him in a duel, fair and square."

"Indeed so," said Proculus calmly. "I saw you do it with my own eyes. But spare a thought, if you will, for the couple of thousand men and women of Rome who did not see it happen. One hundred exclusive men go into a room with Romulus, and he does not come out alive. What do you think happened?"

And he went out to call the people together into the Forum.

"And a great storm came down, and a great wind, and we were separated from Romulus, much though we tried to stay near him, and then we heard the voice of Jupiter calling from the sky, calling Romulus to him, and then we were blinded by the lightning of the storm, and deafened by the noise of the wind, and then we saw him no more.

"When the storm had passed, we searched everywhere for him, but nowhere could we find him. We know that Jupiter must have called him home, not willing that his burial place should become desecrated over the years."

Nobody in the crowd believed this. Any of it.

But also, nobody in the crowd could challenge it, because they did not know what had happened. And was it not sacrilegious to suggest either that Jupiter could not have summoned up a storm and taken Romulus with him? Or, on the other hand, to claim that Romulus was not worthy to be taken.

"So, is he with the Gods, now, then?" shouted someone in the crowd.

"Indeed he is; he must be, since his body is not anywhere on earth," lied Proculus calmly, knowing exactly what his fellow senators had done with the all-too-physical body of Romulus. "And more than that, I say unto you that he is with the Gods as a God, the God Romulus, who will ever after look after the interests of Rome."

Three days later, Proculus called the Senate back into session.

"I warned you that I would only be interrex for a few days. I have sent out invitations to all the villages round about inviting their finest men to come to the Forum in Rome within ten days. There, they may fight to first blood to determine their right to marry Romula and become King of Rome.

"Needless to say, anybody in Rome is also welcome to contest this trial. Whether a member of this body or not, you are welcome to try your strength in repeated single combat. Yes, that includes you, Fabius.

"But be warned: physical strength will not be enough. You must then convince this body of your suitability to the role. Failure to do so will lead to expulsion or death. Physical prowess alone is not enough to govern Rome.

"And I will resign as interrex before the contest. Catalus, will you take over as interrex for the contest to determine the next King of Rome?"

Catalus rose from his stool. "Yes, I will," he intoned.

In the end, over thirty men presented themselves for the challenge.

Among the contestants was one Numa, who had been born in Cures to one of the queen's daughters, Pompilia, who had been kidnapped at the Consualia; after the war of the capitol, her husband had moved back with her to Cures to be near her mother; twenty years later, she had inherited the throne and developed a close relationship with Rome.

Over the years, the culture of Cures had become more male-centric akin to Rome: Pompilia accepted her marriage to her husband as binding and sexually exclusive. As such, she was no longer able to take part in the New Year's Day fertility rites, which devolved instead on the high priestess. But even so, there was no place for male inheritance of power. Without question, the throne would pass to one of her daughters.

Which left Numa in a place familiar to many men throughout the history of those times: brought up in the royal family, he had education and experience in ruling, but nothing to rule. Which left him two options: stay in Cures as a military man; or go off in search of another queendom looking for a sacred king.

The election in Rome seemed a perfect opportunity: not only was his father a Roman, who had inculcated him with an understanding of the culture and traditions of Rome; but he was uniquely qualified as a monarch-in-waiting. Although he despised war and fighting, thought them unnecessarily inefficient ways of resolving issues, he had been thoroughly trained in all aspects of war, from fighting with shield and spear, to commanding both small units and large forces.

He easily won his first two bouts, drawing blood on his third stroke, and quickly became a favourite to win the physical contest of the duels. His third - and final - bout was against Fabius. Altogether a trickier proposition than the first two. Fabius was an experienced duellist, who had practised all his life, had killed Romulus in a duel, and believed very strongly that might was right and that he had earned the right to rule Rome by the simple fact of having killed Romulus.

Fabius started the duel on the attack: a series of quick blows to head and body, combined with deft shield work pushed Numa back towards the rope marking out the gladiatorial ring. But Numa was possessed of infinite patience and dedication. He knew that while a duel could be lost in the first few blows, it could not easily be won in the first few. He kept his shield up and his spear poised, ready to counter any blows that made it past his shield. Pressing forward with his shield, he used the opportunity to turn Fabius and move closer to the centre of the ring. Fabius turned and sent down another flurry of blows with his spear. Again, Numa blocked them all with his shield, waiting for Fabius to give away his pattern. Everyone had one, he knew.

After a few more combinations, Numa decided to try and upset Fabius' rhythm. As Fabius started to attack, he pushed his shield forward and darted his spear out at Fabius' head. Fabius ducked behind his shield and pressed hard into it, trying to knock Numa off balance. Expecting that, Numa retreated a step, trying to encourage Fabius to fall. Fabius was wise to that trick and backed off. The two men stood for a moment, breathing heavily.

As Fabius moved to close the gap again, Numa leapt forward and to his right, bashing his shield into Fabius' and chancing a blow around the outside, trying to get behind Fabius' shield. It didn't work, but it certainly gave the older man something to think about. Then, suddenly, both of them were thrusting with their spears, parrying blows on their shields and dancing around the ring. Spontaneous applause broke out from the audience: surely one of these two was the man they were looking for?

The fight could not last at that tempo. As the shields came together, Numa twisted his as he backed away. Fabius, expecting a push, stumbled momentarily. But it was enough; pressing hard on his shield, Numa forced Fabius to the ground, then drove his spear through the gap into this thigh, then retreated from the ring, throwing his arms - and weapons - up and shouting "first blood".

The applause was universal.

In the end, four men were chosen to be interviewed in front of the whole Senate.

There was only really one question: "what would you propose doing if you were elected King of Rome?"

Fabius had a simple, direct answer.

"I would work for the greater glory of Rome. I would enlarge the size of the standing army, making it three centuries. I would increase the training amongst the other men of Rome to ensure that when we went on campaign we could always call on at least twenty centuries. I would make war on all those lands that do not accept our hegemony; and I would force all those lands to accept the paterfamilias - the concept that men, and men only, decide how cities are governed. Men are stronger, and strength wins."

Numa had a more nuanced view.

"War cannot give life; it can only take it away. Every time we go off to fight, less men come back than left. Yes, it is glorious to fight and to win battles; yes, sacking cities brings booty and wealth from other places to Rome. But, I ask you, does it actually create wealth? No, it just transfers it from elsewhere to here.

"And often it doesn't even do that. How much money do we spend on fighting wars? How much could we do with the time and materials involved in making shields, and spears? How much time do we waste in training men to fight? Can we not do better?

"I see the need for a standing army; and I see the need to have other men ready to defend the city against attack. But can we not gain more through investments in agriculture; in metalwork; in trade? After all, most cities will not trade with you if they are afraid that you are only scoping out their wealth prior to attacking them.

"But I would rather see us work towards growing our own community: improving farming, improving metalworking, carpentry; improving the salt trade; building ships and barges to improve trade between us and Ostia, and Ostia and the rest of the world around the sea."

In the end, Numa was chosen by an almost unanimous vote of the Senate.

The following week, on a day that was held to be auspicious by all the priests and augurs, Numa married Romula. And settled down to be a King who negotiated before he fought. He dedicated a number of temples, including to Janus, the two-faced god: a god of opening and closing; of war and peace. And he closed the gates of the temple, indicating that Rome was at peace with all her neighbours.

And he invested in improving the way everything was done at Rome. Farming improved returns dramatically; although he built no roads, he levelled and improved the communications along the Tiber from the salt mines down to Ostia. And he built a fleet of barges to transport wealth up and down the Tiber from Ostia.
