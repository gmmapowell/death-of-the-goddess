6. Pharmakos

The sun was baking hot.

Directly overhead, as it always was at this time of year, it was scorching. No time to be outside, thought Clematis as she swept the paths around her hut. But tomorrow, Bougainvillaea had declared, was going to be the day of atonement and expulsion: and everything must be perfect.

Since the harvest had been completed, the stubble in the barley fields had been burnt at nightfall in praise of the Goddess, and other crops had been harvested on an almost daily basis. At the same time, the regular life of the village - making pottery and weaving - had returned to normal after the hiatus of harvest.

And now, as the year turned from waxing to waning, and the cycle of life prepared to begin again, it was time for the village to make peace with the year behind and likewise prepare for what was to come. In the cycle of the life in the village, the day of atonement was, in its own way, as important as New Year, Harvest or any of the other festivals. It marked a transition, a turning point in the life of the village and it fulfilled one of their key obligations to the Goddess.

The hut was clean and perfect; her mother would not have allowed it to be any other way. She still lived here with her mother and younger siblings, and would continue to do so until she had children of her own. At that time, she might take over another hut, if there was one free at the time, or have to build a new one if the village were growing. The last few years had seen excellent harvests and no pestilence, so the young had survived, the old had not died as they might have, and the village was as full as she could remember it. Several new huts had been built over the past couple of years as girls she had grown up with had matured and had children, starting their own families.

She looked around her: everything she could see looked perfect, and she knew in her heart that they were prepared. Nobody would find fault with their hut or surrounding areas. And today - or rather tomorrow - was a day to achieve perfection.

Before sunrise, all the villagers were grouped to the east of the village, watching for the sunrise. At this time of the year, the sky was normally clear and today was no exception. The sky flared purple and orange, then red and yellow and then, with great majesty, the Sun showed her face over the eastern horizon. Bougainvillaea greeted her with the traditional words on this day:

    On this day, as we greet you,

    Know that you see our village in its most perfect state,

    Nothing you see today will cause offence

    And we will drive out all evil from before you.

All the villagers stood there, mute, with their arms raised until the Sun had cleared the horizon. Then Bougainvillaea turned to the assembled villagers and called: "Pip! Finn! The Goddess has chosen you. Come with me. Villagers! Go to your houses. Prepare for the atonement and the expulsion.

The two men called walked forward and stood behind Bougainvillaea. All of the others went to their own huts where they would spend the day in meditation and preparation until they were called to atone.

When the dust settled, Bougainvillaea started walking towards the nearest hut.

Hyacinth was the first to be ready.

She went to the counter where the leeks had been prepared and passed them out to her daughters and the two sons still young enough to live at home. All six of them now held a leek in their right hand, in preparation for the visit that was about to come.

Bougainvillaea put her head through the door and asked the ritual permission for entry through a door. Hyacinth responded with the appropriate, polite response of confirmation. Leaving her attendants outside, she stepped into the hut and looked around. She had had no doubt that everything would be perfect here; Hyacinth was like that and had brought all of her daughters up well. In truth, Hyacinth was reaching an age where she deserved to be given a more important role in the community.

"All that I can see is clean and fresh; I see no impurities here," she intoned the ritual phrase of approbation. "But there is one thing lacking. We do not know what Spirits may lurk in dark corners or in our souls. The only remedy is to beat them out." With that, she turned and went outside.

Immediately she had left, bedlam erupted inside the hut, with each of the family members laying about them with their leeks, beating each other and yelling "Out! Out! Out!". They ran around the hut making a racket and invoking the spirit of cleanliness. "Get out! All unclean Spirits! We have cleaned our hut and you are not welcome in a land of cleanliness!"

After running around the hut several times and thoroughly beating each other, they rushed out in the harsh noonday Sun. Immediately they saw Pip and Finn, they started to beat them with their leeks. They stood patiently under the punishment, now well used to it after walking around half the village and receiving the same treatment everywhere they went. They knew that the Goddess was watching and that their future - both in this life and as the cycle turned - would depend more on this moment of privilege than any other.

Clematis watched as the Sun started to descend in the sky from her position near the middle of the procession. Once she and her family had driven all of the evil spirits from their hut into the two Pharmakoi, they had watched as the procession moved on to the next hut and then had joined the rear of the procession. As the winding procession travelled from hut to hut, they had followed in their place, singing the hyms of the Goddess, of atonement and cleanliness.

Pip and Finn had been repeatedly beaten and accepted their lot, their fate soon to be determined for this life and the next turn of the cycle. If they had done really well in this life, they might even have the good fortune to return as women in the next life.

Now the villagers went forward once more to beat the final sins both out of themselves and the Pharmakoi. Where before the violence had been contained within some form of restraint, now all the villagers piled in, beating the two men bloody until they fell to the ground. As each villager reached the end of their strength, they threw the leeks at, near or in one of the cisterns and retreated back towards the village, where the huddled watching the final sunset.

As sunset came, so did the snakes, issuing out from the cisterns, attracted both by the offerings of leeks and the smell of the human blood. They slithered over to find the two offerings on the ground, bleeding and writhing in pain but still alive.

One of the snakes sat up and sniffed the air, its long, forked tongue stretched out to capture the faintest of scents. Yes, this was a human victim, still alive but abandoned by its friends. It lunged forward, biting the man in the neck, then retreating.

As the venom spread through the man, his writhing increased, moving from agony into dementia. As he writhed, he called out. His voice was strained and rough, his words broken and indistinct, but never meaningless.

"Beware... men. Men with bronze sticks. Coming. Let me go! Let me go! They come, they burn." He suddenly sat up and pointed a finger at Bougainvillaea: "Beware! They are strong! They challenge the Goddess and all who believe in her." And then, as if the effort of speaking had finally worn him out, he collapsed again on the ground and stopped breathing.

Finn was more measured in his death throes. "The world ... changes. I cannot see what happens. It is a blur. But the world will never be the same."

Bougainvillaea was shocked. Never before had she heard such prophecies from the victims sent before them to commune with the Goddess. They had fulfilled their duties to the Goddess in exemplary fashion. The harvest had been abundant. What could possibly change ... and what could Pip possibly have meant about men being behind the change? And challenging the Goddess? Who would dare? Who could dare? What would it even look like to challenge the Goddess? She had no answers, no answers at all.
