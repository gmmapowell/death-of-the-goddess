43 Rape of the Sabines

"Why," Romulus asked tentatively, "do I have the very strong feeling that I am not going to like what you are about to suggest?"

"Because you are afraid of my brilliance. And because you are under the thumb of the lovely Carmenta and her daughters."

Romulus rolled his eyes. He had to admit that, while he would have been a King without Catulus, he would certainly not have wielded the same level of power without the man.

"You have two, if not three, interleaved problems," expostulated Catalus. "First and foremost, you have anointed a hundred fearsome warriors to be 'Conscript Fathers' and yet they have no women on whom to father children. Secondly, you have the selfsame warriors - and a few hundred lesser warriors - who want to fight an enemy, and yet have no enemy to fight."

"And thirdly, since you mentioned a third?"

"Well, given those two problems, you have a group of about a hundred fearsome warriors, whom you agreed could depose you if you did not meet their needs, who are not at all happy with the job that you are doing as King and might, if pushed too far, decided that deposing you was in their best interests."

"And replace me with whom? You?"

"I imagine they would follow the same protocol that we used to at Alba, and appoint an interrex to govern while they sat and chose a new King. Who would then be forced - not that much persuasion would probably be needed - to marry the lovely Carmenta or one of her daughters. Any serious rival candidates for queen would then be sent off to worship Vesta - or beheaded, at their choice.

"But, yes, I would imagine a number of votes would swing my way, if only because when the interrex asked, I would put forward my very clear case: that I would immediately carry out the plan I am suggesting to you now. So, in a way, the plan is in charge and will happen. It's really just a question of whether you get the credit or it goes to me. Assuming it works, of course."

"Assuming," agreed Romulus, wearily. He was now totally convinced he would hate this plan, but once again had been completely outfoxed by Catulus. Was the man just that much more clever than him? Or was he just able to play with his mind and run rings around him? He could never tell. But, once again, he had to admit the self-evident: the man's plans generally worked.

The lull in conversation lasted a little while. Catulus was clearly unwilling to speak further until Romulus had assented to the plan; or at least assented to hearing the plan.

"Go on then," Romulus finally forced out.

"Well, it's really simple," said Catulus, smiling. "The men want women, but you don't have any. You need to get some from somewhere. The men want to fight, but there is an uneasy peace at the moment in this region. The two go together: we either start a war to get women, or else in getting some women, we will start a war."

"That is the nub of the problem," complained Romulus. "There is, as you say, a truce of sorts in place. Nobody trusts us enough to give us their daughters in marriage, but we can't take them from our defeated enemies. This is neither simple nor brilliant."

"You are trapped," countered Catalus, "in your pretty wife's apron strings. She wants peace, and so you are hamstrung looking for a peaceful solution. Whereas the solution lies in provoking a war."

"We have one hundred fierce warriors. And, as you say, a couple of hundred others. That isn't anything like enough to assault any of the towns of the Sabines or the Etruscans. The men may want to fight, but they don't want to die."

"But, you agree, they would be willing to defend our towns? And that such a defensive action, behind these walls, would almost undoubtedly be victorious?"

"Yes," said Romulus, guardedly, "but why would they?"

"The cunning part of the plan is to provoke them into attacking us. And to do it while solving our other problem."

"Our other problem?"

"Yes, suppose, for a minute, that we were to advertise far and wide that we were going to hold a special festival for Neptune. Suppose, for a minute, that we were to advertise free food, and entertainment. Suppose we were, in particular to make it attractive to all the young women in the neighbourhood."

"Yes..."

"And then, suppose, when they were all happily in their cups at the end of the evening, we were to ambush them, beat the men and evict them from the town, while keeping the women behind - say, around about a hundred young, nubile women."

"The men would not be armed, so they would need to go home and arm themselves..."

"And we would know they were coming and be ready to defend ourselves ... possibly even ambush them on the way."

Romulus thought long and hard.

"You're right," he conceded at last. "I don't like this plan. And what I don't like about it more than anything else is how right you are that it is the only reasonable way forward. And you are definitely right that I don't want to have to explain it to Carmenta or the girls.

"I think the only thing to do is for you to take full responsibility, Catalus. You're in charge: plan a wonderful feast. I will of course attend. Delegate the thuggery to Thalassius. He will know what to do. On the day, expect a significant portion of the population - including our women - to attend. I'll leave a garrison here to make sure that 'we are not tricked' or something. Just make sure none of our women get raped. And make that very clear to Thalassius. Conscript Father or not, anyone found raping a Roman woman will have his balls cut off."

"Yes, O King," murmured Catalus. "It shall all be as you wish."

In the end, probably four hundred men and women walked up the Palatine to attend the Consualia.

Catalus had done his job well: not only had he advertised the event far and wide, and made it live up to the billing with the free food, drink and entertainment, but he had come up with a whole faux history of the Consualia which meant that no man was allowed to come armed; no man was allowed to come unless accompanied by at least one, preferably more, women; and that part of the ceremony was to award prizes to the most beautiful women in attendance.

The more Romulus thought about it, the more he was sure someone would smell a rat. But the fact that his own wife and daughters had gone with the messengers to some of the various towns passing on the word and inviting friends and correspondents, all in total ignorance of what was really being planned, meant that their honest, genuine excitement rubbed off on all their acquaintances. By the day of the festival, there was no other topic of conversation in Rome, or indeed any of the neighbouring towns.

As the day wound down, everybody started to gather in the forum, ready for the entertainment to begin. First up, while people were gathering, there were jugglers and circus tricks. Then, when most of the guests were there, the mimes started. Funny, unscripted plays with almost no dialogue and predictable plots, usually involving a man's wife cheating on him, only to be discovered (usually in the act) and mock fights breaking out.

And finally, the main event. Twenty pairs of gladiators came onto the stage, ready to do combat to first blood. The same ritual as would happen at a dead man's funeral to decide who would inherit. As they paired off, Catalus shouted "strike" and they began to fight to first blood. As each man was tagged, they walked off the stage into the milling crowd, who were still being plied with free beer and wine.

The ten winners took a moment to catch their breath and were announced by Catalus. "Here we have Brutus, and Fabius, my own son Catalus, and of course we have Thalassius." At the mention of his name, Thalassius threw up his arms and yelled "Strike!". Chaos erupted.

All through the crowd, the men of Rome, denied the right to marry neighbouring women by the custom that women here were free and were the owners of everything, rose up and snatched the young women from the crowd. They set upon the visiting men with cudgels and beat them towards the nearest gate. It didn't take much effort: before long, the men were in headlong flight for the exits, taking what women they could with them. The rest were kidnapped and taken back across to the Aventine, to become wives for the first time in their life.

The repercussions were surprisingly long in coming.

Romulus had expected an attack to come within a month or two; but as Sextilis became September, then October, he realised that no attack was going to come this year. And over the course of that time, the women settled down to their lot. It obviously wasn't the same as it had been growing up in a women-dominated society, but once they had accepted their loss of station, they came to the understanding that this was forever and their daily lot was little changed. The everyday tasks of growing and preparing food; being pregnant and having children; and looking after the community were all much the same. The only thing that really felt different was being limited to one sexual partner - and the changes in the community that came from not celebrating the festivals in the traditional manner. Sexual flamboyance was out, and everything military was in.

In the end, more than a year had gone by before there were any reprisals. And even that was so slow in being organised that Romulus had over a week's warning from his spies.

"They're starting to marshal in Caenina," one reported early in November the following year. "They've brought all the harvest in, and they're starting to arm and train for combat."

"Whatever took them so long?" asked Romulus, who had been waiting for this moment for so long.

"I think it was politics as much as anything. While the women are in charge there, and have lost their daughters, the men are the ones who'll have to do the fighting, and they didn't really lose anything. And I think they recognise that beating us is not going to be easy - particularly if we have the advantage of defending the Aventine."

"Hmmm," acknowledged Romulus.

The pause grew extended for some time. Then Romulus jumped up and said, "Send for Catalus at once. We need to start planning."

"Why only one of them?" demanded Catalus when he had been given all the information. "We must have offended all of our neighbours, and yet just one of these cities has the courage to attack us, and even that after more than a year! What gives?"

"We think they tried to organise a co-ordinated response, and spent some time trying to get Titius, the warleader of the Sabine confederacy, to attack us, but he felt that we had basically done them a good turn, so wasn't going to try and punish us."

"What? How did we do them a good turn?" exclaimed Catalus.

"He thinks they had too many women in their villages, and that they needed to do something about it. We helped out by taking some of them away without actually killing them."

"Then why would they not sell them to us when we asked?"

"Because that was a political decision that was in the hands of the women leaders. Who, understandably, did not want to sell their daughters into what they considered to be a life of slavery. But Titius is a man, making a man's decision after the fact, which is that even if they stood a chance of beating us in battle, they would not stand very much of a chance of recovering the women. And, in reality, would probably cost the actual lives of more men dying in battle than the number of women who had been abducted.

"Then why Caenina, on their own? Why now?" asked Romulus. "If this Titius didn't think they could win with all the Sabine tribes, then what prospect does just one of them have, by themselves?"

"I imagine that one of the women you abducted was related to their Queen," answered Catalus. "As to why now, possibly they have elected a new Queen. Or possibly this year's Sacred King wants to go out with a bang rather than just being executed at the New Year's festival, which will be in March or April, I would think. Maybe you abducted his favourite lay."

Romulus shook his head. On such things turned the fates of whole communities. The warleader missed his favourite piece of tail. As if there wasn't plenty more where that came from in Caenina.

"Well, at least they took the bait," commented Romulus. "Now we just need to take advantage. Stay or go, do you think?"

"Oh, definitely go," said Catalus. "If we were up against the might of all the Sabines, I think we'd need the walls to even up the advantage, but against just Caenina? They'll be lucky to put a full century out in the field. And we have, what, four centuries now?"

"Easily," responded Romulus. "You know, at the rate the local wastrels have been coming in, we're going to need more women soon. But we've been able to keep up with arming them all, and training is a compulsory condition of asylum here."

The hundred men weren't even really in a column.

Each of them was acting as if they were out on a countryside walk, except with weapons. If they had any scouts out, they were simultaneously very good at hiding and very bad at scouting, Romulus thought as he watched the "column" go by. This, he thought, is going to be easy.

He had occupied a couple of hills either side of the obvious route between Caenina and Rome. Two centuries were hidden there on each side of the road. The century coming from Caenina seemed - and probably were - oblivious. Romulus turned to look at Catalus, his eyes silently asking, "can they really be this dumb?". And the small nod of the head from Catalus confirmed that he really believed that this was the way things were done around here - archaic.

When the last stragglers were in the narrow defile, he simply stood up, and without so much as looking around him, charged down the hill. This was going to be easier that stealing a rattle off a baby.

The two Roman forces met in the middle of the defile, with the hundred men of Caenina trapped between them. In moments, half the Caeninae were dead, and the other half surrendered. Not so much throwing away their weapons as never having got around to drawing them. The battle was largely over before it had started.

"Right then," said Romulus. "Who is in charge here?"

"Appius was," replied one of the men, pointing at a corpse. "But you just killed him."

"Right, well, then, what's your name?"

"Cornelius."

"Very good. Does anyone have any objection to Cornelius being in charge of you rabble? No? Good. You're now in charge, Cornelius, and that will last for as long as you do what I say. Understand?"

"Yes, lord."

"Very good. You are going to turn around now, and go back to Caenina. You are going to walk up to the gates, and get whoever is left there to open them. And then you are going to hold them open until we arrive. Is that all clear?"

"What is to stop us walking in, then closing the gates and holding the town against you."

Romulus pretended to think for a bit, and the pointed at Catalus. "You explain, Catalus."

"That's easy," he said. "You like being alive, don't you?"

The moment that the gates were opened, Romulus led his 400 out of the forest and charged the gate. Before anyone inside Caenina could react, Romulus and his men were inside, waving their swords and spears around and threatening death and destruction to anyone who opposed them.

He set his men to emptying out the huts and herding the people to the central square. When they were all gathered there, he leapt up onto a makeshift platform and addressed them.

"Look, it's really very simple. We needed women to have as wives for our families. You refused to let us marry your young women, so we were forced to take them for ourselves. They are happy now. They are contented wives and mothers.

"But you couldn't let go. You couldn't let us have wives and children. The penalty for that has been death for half the warriors of this town. And the further penalty I am going to impose is the destruction of Caenina. Before we leave tomorrow, we will burn down the whole town. And you have a choice.

"You can burn with the town, if it means that much to you. Or you can wander from here, without possessions or land. Or you can come with us and swell the burgeoning population of Rome. No, you won't have any possessions or land, except what you can carry with you; no, you won't be entitled to rule; no, you cannot be free but will have to marry the men that I designate to be your husbands. But you will be alive, you will have a place in the community, and you will be reunited with your daughters.

"You have until we leave tomorrow to decide."

He was unsurprised by how many of the villagers - both men and women - decided to make the ten mile trek to Rome in his baggage train carrying what they could while Thalassius led half a century of Roman soldiers from hut to hut burning everything that remained.
