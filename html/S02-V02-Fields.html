2. A Day in the Fields

"Strike high, then thrust!"

Cronos was working the men through the basic drill of fighting men. Every day, some of the men would take part while others worked in the fields. He had a core of men whose first responsibility was to always be ready to fight: four or five hands' worth. But the others came and worked out with the soldiers once or twice a month, except during the harvest season. It was probably a good compromise - there was a lot of other work that needed to be done to keep the community alive - but Cronos was invariably frustrated by the lack of skill that these part-timers showed. And it often seemed that they had forgotten everything that they had been taught the last time they had been here.

"No, no, no, if you expose yourself like that, you are just asking to be skewered," he yelled at a particularly idiotic young man who seemed to think that this was a day off in the hayfields, rather than a serious activity that could one day be the difference between life and death. Specifically his life or death.

Today, there was no risk of being skewered. The bronze swords were too valuable, too fragile and two sharp to be used for day-to-day training. Instead, they used wooden training swords to beat at each other. It wasn't the same of course - the wood was nothing like as heavy as bronze, and the swords didn't have the same balance - but it was better than not having any bronze swords to hand when they actually needed to fight.

"Come here. Let's go through this slowly," he said, pointing to the young idiot. "Now, stand opposite me. Like this. That's right. Now don't plant your feet as if you are going to stand there all day; fighting is all about motion. You need to be ready to pick your feet up and move them backwards and forwards. You need to be ready to dodge a thrust and put all your weight behind your own thrust. Stand on your toes!"

Cronos took up his guard position and waited for the man to copy him, which he did - slowly and lugubriously. Cronos snapped his sword out and hit the man on the arm before pulling his sword back.

"Hey! I wasn't ready!" he complained.

"Be sure to mention that to any enemy soldiers you might meet," hit back Cronos. "You never know, it might work. Try again. And be ready!" Suddenly, he lost his temper and jumped forward, slapping the idiot's sword to one side and, stepping inside his guard, shouldered him in the face, knocking him to the floor. Cronos flicked his sword out and left it across his neck. "Dead," he pronounced. "Leave the sword there, get up and go back to work. You are no use to me."

Enarete leaned back on her hoe.

The barley was growing nicely and she was sure that it would be a good harvest, but clearing out the weeds from this field was backbreaking work. But somebody had to do it. She had been here all morning, just working her way along this row of barley, often on her hands and knees, scrabbling out the extraneous plants that should not be there.

And then she noticed the blight.

When she had been down amongst the weeds, it had not been obvious, but now it was. On the leaves and the burgeoning stalks of the barley, a discolouration was there. She pulled at some of it and sniffed it. Without doubt, that was barley blight. If she didn't do something quickly, that could soon take over and kill the whole crop. Dropping her hoe, she moved up and down the furrow, looking closely at the state of the barley she passed. The blight was quite widespread, but certainly not taking over the entire field as yet.

Feeling that she had assessed it to the best of her ability, she ran to find Rhea.

Macareus slumped into the river.

It had been a hard drill session. Even without Cronos losing his temper with one of the part-timers - and then taking it out on the rest of them for the rest of the session at arms - the combination of the heat and the effort involved in the gymnastic portion of the drill was enough to reduce them to quivering wrecks. With Cronos in a bad mood, it just became insufferable. They had had to carry four huge rocks (each) along the running course, and then he made them go around a second time because of some perceived failing by one of them. By the time Macareus finally reached the river, Cronos was going back looking for the stragglers who had - literally - fallen by the wayside.

Bathing in the river was generally the best part of the day's drill. Apart from the ability to relax and wipe off the dirt and sweat of the day's activity, there were often women down here doing the village's washing that they could watch and talk to. But not today. For some reason, there were no other people down at the river. Since he would be one of the first to know if the village was under attack, he assumed it had to be something else - but what?

Rhea took the flaming brand from the girl's outstretched hand.

Some of the barley from each end of the blighted patch had now been scythed away to create a firebreak at each end, and all of the surrounding earth had been doused in water to prevent the fire spreading across the ground. If Enarete felt it had been backbreaking work doing the hoeing, the combination of scything and bringing all that water from the river - even with most of the villagers dropping their other tasks to help - had left her completely exhausted.

With a few pots of water still around them, in case the fire should spread unexpectedly, Rhea addressed the Goddess to acknowledge the actions she was taking.

    Oh Goddess, we know not what we have done to be blighted like this,

    But we use your holy flame and destroy the enemy,

    We clean out that which is corrupt in our fields

    And will look to do the same in our hearts.

And with that, she touched the flame to the blight and stood back. Within seconds, the barley had become a tower of flames as the fire leapt from stalk to stalk. While the Sun crept down in the sky, the fire burnt and destroyed blight and barley both. Eventually the flames died down and the barley was reduced to smouldering ash. While not the disaster that it could have been if it had been allowed to spread unchecked through the field, the black smudge was a reminder of how fragile was the cereal on which they all depended.

They stamped on the ash to make sure it was all burnt out and then trudged back to the Megara. Days like today were not the sort of thing to celebrate - more to survive.

The work never seemed to come to an end.

Even the "full-time" soldiers were expected to carry jars of water back from the river to the village to put in the irrigation channels at the end of the day. Cronos called it "character-building exercise" but the truth was that even as a full-time soldier, there were aspects of village life which still called upon you. And it was certainly better than having to spend the whole day in the fields, hoeing, weeding and making sure the cereals were watered; or even working in the craft sheds making pots or weaving cloth from flax.

But today, for some reason, the pots were not where they usually were, so Macareus and the other soldiers had to first walk up to the irrigation pond to find them, before carrying them back to the river just to fill them. He walked stiffly up the hill and through the field.

And noticed the smell of burning. Some of the field had been burnt - intentionally? - during the day. He was sure that if this had been carried out by some other tribe, he would have been alerted and they would have been called to fight them off. But that at least explained why the pots were not where they should be - they were here, having been used to put the fire out.

Macareus picked up two of the pots and walked back down to the river. Stepping into the river, he lowered each of the pots in turn, and filled them as full as he could manage. He passed each of them in turn to one of his colleagues, and in exchange received more pots to fill. When all the pots had been filled, the men picked them up and carried them, once more, back up to the fields.

At the irrigation pond, Macareus put down his pot and tipped the water into the reservoir. When the pot was empty, he took it and stacked it with the others waiting to be carried back down to the river.

Cronos lay down next to Rhea on the straw bedding.

"What a day!" he said. "Do you think we have done something to offend the Goddess?"

"I cannot think what," she replied. "But if we have, we must root it out as thoroughly as we did the blight today. But tell me, are you concerned about discipline at a wider level, or is it just one man? One man I can keep in the fields; he wasn't a full-timer, was he?"

"Goodness, no! He would never make the grade. You know as well as I do how stiff the competition is to become one of the Goddess' own! But no, my concern is not really about one man, it is about the readiness of the village as a whole to resist an attack - particularly a surprise attack. You remember the battle three years ago? I was just a soldier then, of course, but we could very easily have lost. I feel we are not as prepared as we need to be.

"And I can't help feeling that the blight today was a sign. Serious as it could have been in its own right - and I do realise that - the important thing is to make sure you that you take prompt action and root it out completely. And I feel that is the message that the Goddess is sending us today - we need to root out indolence in the community and make sure that we are ready for any attacks. Do you agree?"

"I don't know," she demurred. "I think we should ask the Goddess herself to give us a clear sign."

"A sacrifice?" he asked.

"Tomorrow," she replied, and moved closer to him.

Theano walked among the goats.

For a sacrifice like this, only the best of all the goats would do. It must, of course, be healthy, fit and without blemish. To sacrifice an inferior goat could bring down the wrath of the Goddess on all of them. And as the woman in charge of the offering, it was Theano's responsibility to make sure that the goat was perfect. On top of all that, it needed to be perfect today. Theano knew all the goats of course - knew them nearly as well as the children that tended them on a daily basis - but she needed to sense which of them was ready, today, to go and meet the Goddess and take a message from the villagers.

And bring a response back.

That one! Without question or doubt, she hooked her crook over the small goat Edsel, and led it away. It knew her of course, and knew that she would give it some special malted barley that she carried with her everywhere. What it didn't know was that today was going to be different - and that in a little while it would be burning on the altar.

Cronos laid down the big flint double-headed axe. He picked up the last pieces of wood he had just finished splitting and put them on the pile behind the altar.

Behind him, Theano was approaching with the goat. To his right, Rhea stood with the flint dagger drawn, ready to slice the goat's neck.

When Theano arrived, Cronos turned and picked the goat up, placing it on the altar. Rhea took two steps towards him, reached out, and slashed its throat.

    

    Goddess, if we have sinned, tell us how!

    Goddess, if we must change, show us how!

    Goddess, if there is aught we do not know, tell us now!

    Goddess, lead us in thy paths.

Theano watched the struggles of the goat intently and then, as it ceased moving, signalled to Cronos to place it on the fire. When all was arranged, one of the girls came forward with a fiery brand and passed it to Theano.

"Goddess, accept our gift."

"The Goddess agrees with Cronos. She is afraid."

The others looked at Theano in shock. The Goddess was afraid? Of what? How was that possible?

Rhea, to whom the responsibility for the village ultimately fell, asked the question for all of them: "what can frighten the Goddess?"

Theano shook her head in confusion. "I don't know. But she sent the signs, and they were very clear. The Goddess fears that we will be weak, we will not prevail, and we will be destroyed. And she is concerned that along with us, she herself will perish."

Cronos shook his head dolefully. "I never thought. If I had known, I would never ..."

Rhea cut across him, "no, it is good that we asked. Now we know that our fears are not of our own making, but that even the Goddess herself shares them. I do not know what we can do differently, but we must work diligently to make sure that we are ready for any attacks from outside."

"And be prepared for any other emergencies," amplified Theano. "I think the blight might not just be a warning about our enemies, but also to keep alert to the vagaries of weather and soil."

"We are assailed on all sides," lamented Cronos. "How will we even know in which direction to look for the next crisis?"

"The Goddess will guide us," reassured Theano.
