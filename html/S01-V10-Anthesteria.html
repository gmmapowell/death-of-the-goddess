10. Anthesteria

Bougainvillaea led the villagers out to the cisterns.

The cisterns of the dead. And of the resurrection. For as surely as death followed life, life would one day follow death. All the villagers knew this, for did they not see it acted out around them every day of the year? It was the cycle of the Sun, of the Moon, of the seasons, of the crops. It was the cycle of life: from birth to infancy to maturity to old age and death, leading back to more new life.

And as life started in the east - the Sun, the Moon and the stars all rose in the east, after all - so all life ended in the west. And out here, in the west, was where they buried their dead. Buried in giant cisterns where they could commune with the representatives of the Goddess on earth, the snakes.

And once a year, when all was death, they who were living came out to see the dead and, if they could, release them for one day from their death, for this was the Anthesteria - the time of life-in-death, the day when even the Goddess slept in the underworld. Only the Moon fully sparkled in life; the seeds had been buried and would not been seen again until the year turned again, after the Moon had completed its cycle.

And so here they were. At the cisterns. At the end of the day, as the Sun settled in to sleep, and behind them the Moon rose. But for the next few hours, death was supreme.

"Hear me, O Goddess," chanted Bougainvillaea as she reached the first of the burial cisterns.

        Hear me, O Goddess, who art ever with us, even in your day of death.

        Know that even as we mourn your burial,

        So we release those who have been buried before us,

        Knowing that one day those who come after us

        Will allow us our one day of release

        To be back in the world above.

She motioned to the four priestesses around her and they moved into position and, grasping the handles on the lid of the cistern, lifted it from the pot below and moved it to one side. Bougainvillaea leant over the open cistern and called down into the space below.

       

        Greetings, O Spirits.

        Know that it is now the beginning of the Anthesteria.

        You are welcome to wander among us, and tomorrow we will feed you.

        But you may not take any who chew the buckthorn with you.

       

        It is your choice.

        You may come up and be with us, or you may stay down below.

        You spirits are free to stay or to roam

        For the next three days as you will.

The greeting completed, she led the procession on to the next cistern.

It was a sombre procession that came back into the village around the middle of the night.

It was impossible to see the spirits that chose to came up from the cisterns to join the procession of the living, but there was a clamminess to the air that accompanied them. By the time all the cisterns had been opened and the dead welcomed up, the procession felt encased in chilly fog. But the air was clear, and the night sky gleamed with the full moon and stars.

The women dispersed to their various houses. By the ritual of the Anthesteria, none of them closed their doors behind them, and all of them had bowls of buckthorn on their tables. Both for themselves and to share with any of the spirits that might choose to come and join them. Most of them would sit up now and be awake for the entirety of the the festival - they were just simply too afraid of what might happen if they were to drift off to sleep.

Hyancinth led her family in a recitation of one of the old verses commonly repeated for this day:

       

        Let us remember those who went before us;

        Those who knew the world before us;

        They led their lives in cycles long ago;

        They came before us and left before us;

        They lived the cycles so that we might follow;

        The Sun turned in their lives;

        The Moon went around;

        They were born, they lived and they died

        In the cycle of life.

All of the women in the house took part in the brooding verses, trying as much as possible to keep themselves from thinking about the reality of death - the reality that death stalked among them this night.

The word was passed that the Moon was going down and the Sun was about to rise.

In keeping with the low-key nature of this festival, no trumpets or bells sounded; the message almost passed by osmosis. At this point, Hyacinth went and fetched the bucket of pitch they had prepared the week before and the brushes. Each of the women took a brush and they went to the front door, where they went to work "painting" the door frame with pitch.

"Know that we welcome you into our house today, O Spirits!" chanted Hyacinth.

       

        For today is the Anthesteria,

        And on this day we know you walk around among us.

        But know also that this is our house,

        And that by this anointing on of pitch,

        We make it clear that life may not be taken out of the house.

        You may come and go as you please,

        But you may not take any of us with you.

The daubing done, the women took some of the bowls of buckthorn with them and went out to the east of the village to greet the Sun.

As the Sun rose above the horizon, Bougainvillaea greeted it on behalf of the villagers and the spirits of the dead.

       

        Welcome to our village, O Sun!

        Know that this day, we mourn our Goddess who is dead,

        But live in harmony with our Spirits who are amongst us.

        You are most welcome on this day of Death,

        And we hope that you will shine on the living and the dead.

When the ceremony was over, they all returned to their houses.

The day passed slowly. And mainly in silence.

From time to time, one of the women would say something, either one of the chants associated with the day, or a recollection of one of the women that had gone before them. They spoke when they were moved to do so, but other that that were generally silent, lost in contemplation and concerned that they did not draw too much attention from the spirits.

And eventually the end of the day drew near.

As the day came to a close, Hyancinth gathered up the pots that had been prepared, and shared them among the women of the house. Then, being sure to cover themselves in mourning garments, they took the feast and joined a procession which formed back out to the cisterns to the west of the village.

Here Bougainvillaea led the villagers in laying out the feast for the spirits. A feast that was supposed both to attract the spirits back to the graveyard and also to placate them with the offerings that they had made.

Hyacinth took a pastry from one of the bowls and ate it sparingly. Clematis absently took one as well and munched on it while looking around her. The air looked clear but again, it felt clammy. Even if the spirits could not be seen, they could be felt. Clematis felt the spirit of her grandmother weighing down on her. Hyacinth's mother had been dead for six or seven seasons now, but Clematis felt her presence every year during the festival.

When the feast was over, the women returned to the village and their houses.

By the morning, the festival was considered over.

The problem with chasing away spirits that you couldn't see was that you couldn't see them, and didn't know if you'd succeeded. The hope, of course, was always that the feast would have drawn the spirits back to the cisterns where they needed to be, but, just to be safe, before dawn, it was time for the chase.

Today, it did not need to be signalled quietly. Quite the contrary.

With a blaring blast of sound, the trumpets announced that the day was breaking, and that it was time for all the villagers to begin the chase. Taking whatever was to hand, they starting making as much noise as possible.

Hyacinth picked up a ceramic pot and a wooden stirrer. Clematis picked up a wooden platter and a bronze shaft. The other women in the house reached for their own items. Hyacinth led the others out of the door, beating her pot with the stirrer and trying to make as much noise as she possibly could.

       

        Begone!

        Out of our doors, O Spirits!

        It is no longer the Anthesteria!

        It is no longer the Anthesteria!

       

        Back in your tombs!

        The time of walking with the living is over!

        Out of our doors, O Spirits!

        Back in your tombs!

Hyacinth's cries were echoed by Clematis and the other women and were overlaid and repeated by all the women throughout the village as they ran from house to house, waking and scaring the Spirits, and then driving them back through the fields to the cisterns.

When they arrived, Bougainvillaea and the priestesses went to each tomb in turn, and spoke the ritual words of reburial into each cistern.

       

        Thank you for coming to guest with us,

        Thank you for the lives that you led,

        Now it is time to prepare for the next year

        And time for you to return to the dead.

And with that, the priestesses covered the cistern and moved on to the next.

It was no longer the Anthesteria.
