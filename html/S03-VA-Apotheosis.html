11. Apotheosis

25 years later

Somehow merely burning him did not seem enough for so great a man.

Somehow he seemed more a part of the Godhead that he seemed a mere hero. But could a man be at one with the Goddess, she who was all-in-all?

The whole village was gathered around the pyre this autumn evening. There was a cool breeze coming from the north that seemed to speak to all of them of the loss they had suffered. Some shivered from the cold, some from the uncertainty. Nobody there could remember a time before Zeus - this Zeus - had not been King. To burn him as they did all the heroes did not seem enough.

Agamemnon - the King's oldest son - stepped forward and put the torch to the pyre. Slowly the wood caught and the flames raced around the pyre, crackling and glistening as the sun set behind the hills to the west. The fire raced up in counterpoint to the disappearing sun, evoking tribal memories of the volcanoes they had left behind in the north so many generations ago.

The sun had gone around the earth thirty-three times since Zeus had first become King. Every year he had been challenged and had survived the challenge although, of late, the challenges had been more ritualistic than genuine. Many of the army leaders could have beaten Zeus if they had challenged him, but none of them really thought they would make a better King, so they refrained from challenging him. Those who did knew they would lose but could not resist the temptation of being "King for a Day". And having one day with the luscious Hera.

And in his thirty-three years he had gone from being ruler over one village, to ruler over four, to ruler over more than twenty villages. Nothing was as it was as it had been thirty-three years ago when he had successfully challenged for the hand of Hera in marriage - a marriage that had brought him a share in the leadership of the village.

Even Hera was not the same Hera. Hera the Queen who had married Zeus all those years ago had died twelve summers before Zeus and been cast to the snakes in the way traditional for the women and villagers. Only Heroes were entitled to be burnt on a pyre. After Hera's death, Zeus married one of her daughters - aged fifteen summers - and she became the new Hera.

Over the course of all those years, something else changed: Zeus went from being "the king" in the sense of being the current wife of Queen Hera, to being The King, whose wife was the (younger) Queen Hera. By successfully defending his kingship every time it was challenged, he stopped being the king by virtue of being married to the Queen, and instead became the King in his own right.

And nobody expected that to change.

A funeral pyre just like the other Heroes was not enough for the first man ever to be The King.

Apollo recused himself from competing to be the new King in Zeus' place.

"I have been Zeus' faithful servant for many years now," he said, "and I am old and grey. I look around at the yound men here - many of them sons of our great Zeus and his wife Hera - and I know that I do not have the strength to win a contest at arms.

"No matter that many of you think that I would be the man most able to rule and guide this village, I would only do so for a few years. Better then, that we choose a young, vigorous, virile man to take over and marry Hera. Let him be King, and - whoever the Goddess chooses, hopefully with the help of our dearly departed Zeus - I will be his faithful assistant as I was to the mighty Zeus.

"But, as my final official act as the right hand of Zeus, I will oversee this combat, for combat there must be to decide who shall take on the name and title and role of Zeus. Only combat serves in the eyes of the Goddess to find the one who is worthy to lead us in these trying times.

"And I swear before her, that I will oversee this combat fairly, without fear or favour, and will support whichever of these young men she chooses.

"Now, let all those who would present themselves to her to compete to be the next King step forward."

Twelve men stepped forward to present themselves. Four of them were Zeus' sons, now grown to be men of more than twenty summers. In their prime, and yet still hoping to have another decade or more in which to rule and sire sons of their own with the luscious Hera.

Apollo eyed them all up, then sent them home to arm and prepare for the combat to come.

As the sun rose high overhead, Apollo greeted the twelve in the sight of all the people of the village. Who would not be here for the first choosing of a King any of them had ever known?

Apollo had already decided how to divide them up, pairing stronger men against the weaker, so that after the first round, he expected six stronger men to make it through to the next round while the six weakest would lie dead in the dust to be carried away by the slaves. Each bout went as he expected, with most of them settled in just a few blows.

To bring six down to four, he arranged two melees between three claimants. As before, he made sure that each had one weak candidate and two who were more evenly matched. Within a few fingers of the sun, only two men remained: the only two, Apollo knew, who should have even stepped forward: Agamemnon and Achilles. One or the other would be dead by sunset; the other would be King. But which?

For once, Apollo did not know. He would find out with everyone else.

The two men circled each other again, looking for an opportunity to strike. Each had landed - and suffered - a handful of blows, and each was growing weak. Achilles was worried that his bronze sword was weakening - always a danger with this brittle material - and was considering changing it for another. He knew that Apollo would allow a pause in the fight if he asked for it, but at the same time, he felt Agamemnon was weakening. Stepping forward, he chanced a quick snap at Agamemnon's head. Agamemnon stepped to one side and dodged the blow.

Achilles took two steps back and then ran at Agamemnon, bringing his sword down in a crushing overhand blow. Agamemnon raised his sword out of instinct - and just in time - and Achilles' sword shattered into pieces from the force of the blow.

Before either man could consciously react, Agamemnon put all his force into carrying the parry through into a follow-through that slashed Achilles across the neck. As his blood started spurting out over the earth floor, Achilles fell to his knees.

Brave to the last, he threw his arms open wide and invited the final, killing blow.

Mercifully, Agamemnon supplied it.

King Agamemnon addressed the crowd.

"Today is truly a great day," he began. "But we should never let anything remove from us the memory of the man we lost and burnt yesterday. A man who was more than a hero. A man we can only call a King. A man who knew what his people needed and led them through the wilderness to get there. A man of fire and steel. Truly a man among men.

"And my first act as King is to make sure that we do not forget. Yesterday, west of here, towards the setting of the sun, we burnt the Great Man. As my first act, I will go there, along with any man who will come with me, and I will build out of living stones a monument to the only man I will ever call King. The greatest man that has ever lived. And in doing so, I recognize his spirit now as one with the Godhead, and as we recognize the Goddess as She who Reigns over all, I will recognize Zeus as the God in the Heavens who looks after all His children."

Although the word had never been used before, there could only be one possible meaning for the word God - it was the same as the word Goddess, but with a masculine ending rather than the feminine ending. A male Goddess was a novelty, but Agamemnon could see he had them. It was a novelty that was going to stick. They would call Zeus a God, and the would make offerings at his stone monument. Offerings that would reflect the fact that Zeus was a God of Warriors, not a Goddess of Life.

Yes, this would stick. For all time.

In Mykenai, the Goddess lived on for now - as Queen Hera lived on - but both were now subservient to their male masters.
