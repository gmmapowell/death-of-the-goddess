3. Pharmakos

The harvest had begun, and the first day's reaping had been gathered in the Megara.

Now, the women gathered to prepare the offerings to the Goddess. As usual, the men were excluded from this most important of annual rituals: recognizing that the bounty they had received - and would continue to receive as the harvest continued - was a gift of the Goddess and that only with her help and support would the village continue to survive. To acknowledge their debt, and express their gratitude, they now prepared to offer the fruits of the first day's harvest back to the Goddess and her agents on earth, the snakes.

At the same time, they had engaged to purify the village by sending forth those scapegoats whom the Goddess had chosen. Four men walked among them still, marked by the Goddess, but unknown to the villagers, who in the morning would be revealed to Zeus as the four who must accompany the sacrifice - and thus be sacrificed themselves.

The women stood in a circle around the harvested corn. Led by Hera and Athene, they incanted the prayer to the Goddess,

Goddess  who  art  above  us,
Watch  over  us  now;
Listen  as  we  prepare  Thy  feast;
To  pay  back  a  part  of  Your  bounty.
We  work  here  in  the  Night;
In  the  morning  we  will  greet  Your  Face;
Always  we  think  of  You;
Always  we  worship  You.

Then the women applied themselves to work with a will. As always at harvest time, the nights were getting shorter as the Goddess rose again from her sojourn in the underworld and sprouted forth everywhere they looked: seeds were becoming plants; animals were having young; the Sun rose earlier and earlier; meadow flowers bloomed and showed their faces, hidden over the course of winter. Truly the Goddess was back amongst them. But on the downside, as the days grew longer and the nights shorter, the women had less time at night to prepare the offerings.

Each woman made cakes from the corn they had harvested, mixed together with blood from the bulls held back from the boars of the Thesmophoria the previous winter. This tied together the bounty of this spring with the remnants of the previous autumn, and reminded the Goddess that they had been faithful then, and over the intervening winter, and they were still faithful now.

They had also gathered other fruits: almonds, and figs just coming into season. Everything that they could find from the bounty of the Goddess they collected and arranged on brand new pottery platters crafted especially for this occasion. Everything they prepared was part of the offering, and everything had to be perfect: the plate, the corn offerings, the other fruits, the arrangement. If anything were to be less than perfect, it would send a message to the Goddess that they despised her love and care, and that they did not value her enough to make a perfect offering. In the past, such mistakes had cost the village dear: the Goddess had withheld the life-giving rain; or had given too much rain at once, destroying the crops as they grew; or had sent pestilence to kill the young, or the animals; or had caused the village to catch fire.

At length, all the platters were ready, the offerings arranged beautifully on them; their work here done, they went outside to greet the face of the Sun as she rose in the eastern sky on the morning of the Thargelia and Pharmakos.

The leaders among the men joined them there. As men, they were cut off from the festivals that revolved around incubation and birth: men knew of insemination and strength, but knew nothing of nourishment and growth; such activities were strictly for the women and their Goddess. Men still worshipped the Gods of the mountains and Fire, but recognized that here, in this village, it was the Goddess that controlled their destiny. But both men and women recognized the Sun as a deity. For women, She remained the most visible face of the Goddess; a face that shone down on them day after day; a face that gave life to the crops and warmth to the village. For men, the Sun was a symbol of power, strength and domination, reminding them of their ancestral Gods in the mountains: Gods of fire, spouting from the tops of the mountains, pouring down the sides of the mountains, destroying everything in their path. Although no man in the village had ever seen such a sight, the stories had been passed down from father to son throughout the generations. And the Sun offered the only visible cosmic link to these stories: a ball of fire in the sky, which was released every morning from a mountain on the horizon they could not see.

On the other hand, the Goddess the women worshipped was around them everywhere. Although excluded from her most holy rites, whenever it was allowed the men expressed their devotion and gratitude to Her: She who governed every aspect of their lives on a daily basis.

Slowly, the Sun rose on the horizon, flaming into view and trailing sparks across the sky. All of the leaders of the people - Zeus, Hera, Ares, Athene - stretched forth their arms and chanted the invocation to the risen Sun.

Today was the Thesmophoria. And the Pharmakos.

After the Sun had risen, the villagers returned to their huts.

The men then gathered in the Megara for the choosing. Zeus and Ares were responsible for identifying the four men that the Goddess had marked. How they did this - how the Goddess spoke to them - was never known to any of the other villagers, but the two men walked through the ranks of men lined up for their inspection. And four times, Zeus reached out and tapped a man on the shoulder. As he was touched, the man knew his fate, and knew the importance of not showing that he knew his fate. Straight and stiff-backed, he walked from the assembly up onto the dais in the Megara. Here, they stood in a line, each knowing that today they would be beaten and sent forth from the village. In some sense, they did not know why they had been chosen; but in another sense, they all knew exactly why: the Goddess had found them out. For which exact crimes, they did not know; but the Goddess was all-knowing and would know what they had done even if they did not themselves. And, after all, who could claim to be innocent of all wrong-doing? Thoughts and minor offences flashed through their mind's eye, but these four men knew that the only thing they truly had in common was that they were unmarried men, and the Goddess had chosen them to accompany her offerings of the firstfruits and to be sent forth from the village, taking the offences of the village with them, that they not stain the village further.

When the four men had been chosen, Zeus called out to the remaining men, "Now go home to your huts and prepare yourselves. Evil is hiding amongst us, in this village and in our huts and in our hearts. The women are already cleaning out the huts and cleaning out the evil they can find. Now you must go and help them. All the evil must be cleaned out and put on the heads of these Pharmakoi. Ares and I will lead them."

The men left, dispersing to their individual homes. Meanwhile, Zeus and Ares went to stand with the four men who remained on the dais.

"Remember, men, that you have been chosen not because you are weak, but because you are strong. You must bear all the crimes and wickedness of the whole village. They will clean it from their hearts and from their houses and they will beat it into you. You must stand strong and hold that suffering, and cleanse it from the village. If you stand strong, the Goddess will reward you for your sacrifice. How she will intervene in your life, I cannot say, for none who leave as Pharamakoi are allowed to return. Once you leave, you will be outside the law; it is true that any who see you can kill you with impunity since no man will exact revenge. But remember, too, that the Goddess is all-seeing and all-powerful. If she chooses to intervene on your behalf, no man can withstand her power. With her help, it is possible you will find a new life far from here, in another village or community where men of strength and dedication to the Goddess are recognized and rewarded. Having carried out the sins of this village, you can yet be purified through your trials and struggles; if you are, you can carry on living whereever there are men who will have you, loyal servants of the Goddess.

"Now, prepare yourselves."

Each of the men took off his regular tunic, and placed over his head the newly fashioned garments of the Pharmakoi. The pristine white robes were fashioned to resist all manner of evil and to give their wearers strength in the time of trial. None of these men were wearing sandals, but if they had, they would have taken them off. Only a man truly at one with his mother, Earth, could carry away with the sins of the village. To have worn sandals at this time would have been to despise his mother and reject her care. Such a man could never succeed in his task.

Within moments, all four men were ready. Zeus indicated that two of them were to follow him, while Ares led the other two. They started to make their way through the village, each procession slowly building with one Pharmakos in each procession for the women and one for the men.

As they passed each hut, the family inside came out, screaming and hissing and violenty set upon the two men. The males of the family would beat the man on the right; the females the man on the left. Once the vicious beatings were complete, the family would wait as the procession moved on and then join it at the rear.

Hopefully they had beaten all of the evil out of themselves and their hut and into the offerings.

At the western edge of the village, Hera and Athene waited with the other women and the platters of offering.

Zeus and Ares led the processions out to meet them, the four Pharmakoi looking beaten and battered, limping slightly, but still showing the courage and forbearance expected of the Goddess' anointed. When they arrived where the women were waiting, Zeus and Ares stepped aside from the processions and allowed the men to continue and bow to the women waiting for them.

Hera spoke for the women, addressing the Goddess rather than the Pharmakoi: "Oh, Goddess, you who sees everything, know that we have obeyed all your commands and this day we send forth these four men, carrying with them all the slights and offences against you that we have unwittingly committed over the past year. As the new year begins, remember us without fault, for we are your loving and obedient children."

She bowed her head in silence for a few minutes. When she lifted her head up, the majority of the villagers turned back to the village. With a quick gesture, she dismissed the pharmakoi: having taken on board the sins of the village, they were no longer villagers but loners of the wilderness. They wandered off: by strict form, they now went in different directions, abandoning each other to their individual fates.

When they were gone, Hera signalled to the women waiting with her and they went westwards towards the cisterns in the wilderness. When there, they laid out their gifts for the Goddess with all due respect. Then Hera invoked the ritual phrases of giving and the women retreated to observe how the Goddess would respond.
