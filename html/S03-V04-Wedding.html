4. Wedding

Doris looked up from milking her goat to see her daughter.

"What are you doing here, Iphi?"

Iphigenia looked at her shyly. She had not known what to do when the bleeding had suddenly started in the fields, but she had felt weak and Xanthe had said that she "needed to go and find her mother" in such a determined voice that she really felt that she had no choice at all. But the closer she had come to her mother, the less she knew what to say to her. She knew how her mother felt about her slacking off.

She burst into tears.

Doris abandoned her goat, who seemed a bit miffed at the neglect, but Doris sensed something was badly wrong with her daughter and that took priority.

"What is it, sweetheart? Has something happened? Are you all right?"

Iphi blubbered for a bit, lost for words. Finally she managed to put things into some kind of coherent order for her mother. "I was working in Xanthe's field. We were pulling up some of the stones and turning the soil over and then ... and then there was all this warmth on my legs and I looked and there was blood everywhere and I felt faint again and Xanthe said I needed to find you and so I left and ... came here," she ended lamely. She lifted up the hem of her tunic to show her mother the blood running down her legs.

"Oh, wonderful!" exclaimed Doris, then looked at her daughter who was clearly lost. "Welcome to the world of women, my dear. You will always be my daughter, but you are no longer a girl: you are a woman."

With a sudden clarity, everything came into focus for Iphigenia. That which made a woman a woman had happened to her at last. She had seen her friends go through this transition but had somehow failed to make the connection when it happened to her.

And then, as suddenly as it had arrived, her sense of wholeness and clarity fled. "What do I do now?" she blubbered.

"Well, first, you need to stop that crying," said Doris in a calming tone Iphigenia was unused to. "And then your father and I need to go and present you to Hera and Athene for acceptance into the ranks of the women of the village."

Her father, Xerxes, was as delighted as her mother. Possibly more so, for while she was still a girl, she was his responsibility and a burden. Once she became a woman he could "offload" her, as he had put it more than once, to some other man who would be responsible for feeding and clothing her for the rest of her life.

"I must go to Zeus at once," he said. "He was telling me the other day that he was looking for a girl ready to be married. I think he has a special officer in mind."

"An officer?" said Iphigenia, going all blubbery and discombobulated again. "I'm not sure I'm worthy of that."

"Oh, rubbish, girl," responded her mother. "The man wasn't born - officer or no - who is worthy of any woman, let alone my beautiful daughter!"

"Your mother is right, Iphi," agreed her father. "I will have to talk to Zeus and make sure that we find somebody who is worthy of you. Fear not." And he left them to go and find Zeus, while the women went to find Hera and Athene to perform the rituals of womanhood.

They met in the hut of the priestess, in sight of the Goddess herself.

As Iphi walked in, the snake slithered over to her, hissing with its tongue and taking in the scent of her. After a little while, it retreated back into its hut.

Hera and Athene looked at each other, then Athene confirmed: "the Goddess has not selected this one; she is not intended for the service of the Goddess."

Hera picked up the thread: "so ladies, prepare for a wedding. I imagine that your father is going to talk to Zeus about a potential husband for you." Looking Iphigenia straight in the eyes, she added, "whoever he is will be very fortunate to have a wife like you. Many healthy children do I see in your future."

Iphi felt she had done a good job of holding herself together during the course of the interview, especially when the snake - the Goddess herself - had been rattling around her, but now she regressed to being the child she had been in the morning and broke down crying.

Hera moved over to her and held her in her arms. "There, there, girl," she soothed. "We all have a path in life and we can trust in the Goddess to choose wisely. Weep not for the girl you are leaving behind today, but press forward in the full confidence that the Goddess will always be there to help and to guide you. And from the new moon onwards, you will have a husband to stand by your side as well. Remember, girl, always trust the Goddess, for She is wise and all-seeing."

And with that, Hera escorted them from the Priestess' hut, leaving Athene with a look of confusion on her face.

Xerxes found Zeus in the first place he looked for him.

"Swing harder," Zeus shouted. "You'll never strike his head off like that, even if you used a sharp, bronze sword."

The man he rebuked looked down and backed away from his combatant. The two of them shuffled around and then took up their positions. It was clear to Xerxes that neither of these men wanted to be here, swinging practice swords at each other. In fact, he knew both of them quite well, and part of the bond the three of them shared was a general sense that ploughing fields was both more valuable work - and more rewarding - than hitting other men with long poles - whether blunt or sharp made no difference.

Zeus saw him now and saw an opportunity to stir things up. "Come, come, Xerxes," he cried. "Here, have my sword and show these two lubbers how it's done."

"I'm not sure," began Xerxes demurely, "that I necessarily have the skills you are looking for here. I would probably just fall on your sword and hurt myself."

"Rubbish, rubbish, Xerxes. Yes, yes, I know you are not a soldier for the ages, but you can at least come here and show these two what it means to put some effort in and swing hard."

If nothing else, Xerxes could see that his friends had enjoyed the short break while Zeus had hectored him. He decided that playing along would gain them some time, if nothing else. He approached Zeus and took his sword. Briefly, he experimented with it, testing its weight and balance. Then he turned to the two men: "which of you would like to go first?"

Timo stepped back, while Isocrates turned to face him. Both Xerxes and Isocrates went into the ready position while Timo took a few steps further back. Xerxes stepped in and swung his sword in a brutal overhand fashion. Isocrates quickly raised his sword in a block, but the weight of the blow pushed Isocrates' sword down close to his head. The pair traded a few dozen blows until Isocrates found himself unable to field a parry and received a solid thunk to the side of his chest. He dropped his sword in shock and then fell to the ground. Xerxes stepped forward and helped his friend to his feet and hugged him. "Sorry, friend," he whispered in his ear.

Zeus slapped his thigh. "Oh, well done, well done, Xerxes. I've been trying to explain this to them for the last palm of the sun and they will not listen. Yes, that's how it's done. Commit to the blow. All of your weight, all of your strength behind the blow. That's what it takes."

Timo stepped forward timidly to take his turn sparring with Xerxes while Isocrates gingerly got back to his feet. Xerxes used the few moments' grace to address Zeus directly.

"Noble Zeus," he began, "while it is always a pleasant opportunity to spar with your soldiers, I am here on a mission of a different kind altogether. My daughter - Iphigenia - has just passed the threshold of womanhood. She has gone to see Hera and Athene and we feel that she is in need of a good husband. Just the other day you said that you had some officer in need of a good wife, I believe? If that is so, my Iphigenia would be just the woman for him!"

"Capital, Xerxes, capital! We must certainly celebrate this. Indeed, I have just the fellow in mind. Young Hector was promoted just last moon to the rank of officer in charge of two hands, and he is in need of a bride to cement his place in the community. What dowry does your daughter bring with her?"

Xerxes and Doris had talked about this on and off for a season or two now. They had three daughters in total, and they had to divide their own property between them. Two of their four living sons had already made good marriages, but in any case they could continue to benefit from the use of the lands they gave to their daughters for the rest of their natural lifetimes.

"She comes with two fields, sire. I would be happy to walk you or Hector around them, show you the bounds and the quality of the soil we have been nourishing for all these years, if it please you, my lord. The best of our lands; our other two girls will not be deprived but I don't imagine they will be able to make such good matches as our Iphi."

"Splendid, splendid. Well, you two, you might as well go, nothing very much you're going to learn today. Come on, Xerxes, let's go and find Hector. If he approves the girl and her land, we've got a wedding to prepare for tomorrow!

"I'll tell you something, Xerxes: it's good that the harvest is out of the way. You know how it is for these young people who want to get married during the harvest season. You can't take a whole day off getting the crops in just for a wedding!"

Xerxes always found Zeus' bluff heartiness a little wearing. But it was a small price to pay for the privilege of his daughter being married to an up-and-coming officer like Hector. And he knew Hector would be enamoured of his daughter and enchanted by the land she brought with her.

Most of the village had gathered in the Megara by noon. Weddings were communal events in which all the villagers wanted to participate.

Hector had dressed in his finest tunic, freshly cleaned for the occasion, with his sparkling officer's belt around his waist. In accordance with tradition, on this day of peace he did not carry a sword with him, although all knew of his prowess with the weapon.

In accordance with tradition, everybody stood waiting for the bride to come in, accompanied by her parents.

From the back of the crowd, cheering started, and then a gap appeared amongst the villagers and three figures walked through. Xerxes was on the right, and Doris on the left. Between them shuffled a diminuitive figure shrouded in a blanket.

By tradition, the groom would not see his bride until after they were married.

By tradition, Athene - priestess of a Goddess who did not countenance the rite of marriage - was not present. In her place, Hera - the Queen whose marriage ennobled Zeus - officiated at the ceremony that tied together the two aspects - male and female - of the Goddess' creation. As the bride's family came to stand in front of her, and opposite Hector, she intoned the ceremonial words of greeting.

"Today, we celebrate in this village the binding of this woman to this man. In doing so, she passes from the province of the Goddess and her priestesses and passes unto this man, Hector, to be her keeper and protector. From now, he shall be her only man and she will not perform the act of life with any other, and she is excluded from the rituals of the Goddess. He promises to look after her and to work for her benefit and to nurture the land that she brings with her.

"May they have long lives and many children and bring joy and prosperity to this village.

"You may uncover the bride."

Hector walked up to her and took folds of the blanket in both hands. Lifting it up gently, he looked underneath at the woman who was now his wife. What he saw pleased him, and, with a strong flick of his wrists, sent the blanket flying, revealing her to all the villagers.

"You are now man and wife for all eternity."
