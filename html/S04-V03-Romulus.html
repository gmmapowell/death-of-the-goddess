3. Romulus

"You are not thugs," thundered Numitor, "you are my sons!"

"And what good does that do us?" countered Remus. "We are your sons, but not your heir. That goes to whoever you allow to marry Julia. Which, given that she is our sister, is not us. So what are we left with?"

"You have a good life; you are captains in the army; you have wives and property. Really, what more do you want?"

"You say we are captains in the army," said Remus. "But you don't like us fighting," finished Romulus.

"You say we must train and prepare for war," continued Remus. "And then call us thugs for fighting our enemies," added Romulus.

"Your enemies," said Numitor. "Precisely. Not the enemies of Alba Longa. Those are the people I expect you to fight."

"And who," asked Remus. "Decides that?" finished Romulus.

It had been a habit of theirs since they had first started talking, on the same day. Twins were often close, but everybody remarked on just how close - and how similar these two were. Sometimes it seemed like they were just one person, but taking it in turns to act the part.

"The point being," expostulated Remus, "that we want to decide." "And to do that," explained Romulus, "we need to be, as it were, in charge." "In short," summarized Remus, "we want to be Kings in our own right."

"The eternal curse of young royal men. Trained to power but with no outlet for it. Well, that's it as far as I'm concerned. You and your gang of thugs are no longer welcome here. Get out of my city. And don't come back. Any of you."

They left the Megaron and went outside into the bright afternoon sunshine.

"That went well," said Remus.

"We're free," said Romulus.

"Where shall we go?" asked Remus. "There's adventure out in those hills."

"Look," exclaimed Romulus, pointing to the sky. "Eagles!"

Remus watched them fly north, up and over the river. They looked at each other. "North!" they exclaimed together. "Jupiter has spoken," concluded Romulus. "Time to get the 'gang' together for an expedition," said Remus. "We need supplies and weapons as well," concurred Romulus. "Let's get going!"

It confused both of them when they disagreed.

It happened so rarely, in truth, that they had never needed to develop a strategy for dealing with such conflicts. But one faced them now.

Having scouted the area thoroughly, they had found that two of the summits had small towns on them. Of the others, Remus liked one in particular. "It's a good place to found a town. There is a river close at hand. There is nothing to stop us building there, and then we can trade and deal with the locals."

"Or we could just take this town Palatium. It's already there, it's occupied, there is a queen and well, you know how it goes. Kill a few men including her husband, and then, there you are: king of Palatium, a town that already exists."

"Except we're already married, Romulus. Just because you didn't bring your wife with you doesn't mean you're not."

That was a continuing bone of contention between them. Romulus' wife had not felt inclined to leave a life of luxury in Alba to take to the dubious life of the campaign trail; Remus' had not wanted to be left behind. Romulus felt that in the absence of his wife, he was at most a widower and was free to marry again. Remus felt the gods would turn their faces away from them if he were to do anything so impious.

"A compromise?" asked Catalus, coming up to them. "We need to build a camp. Yon hill would be a good site. As you say, Remus, it's easily defensible and has good access to water. We could build a camp on that hill. Then we can see what the neighbours are like and, well, you know ..."

"If they were to attack us ..." pondered Romulus. "Then we could defend ourselves and take Palatium," finished Remus.

"Bright boys," said Catalus, easily ten summers their senior. "And that's all but inevitable, isn't it? I mean, if there were an armed camp within five stades of you, and they kept stealing your sheep from that valley, I mean, who wouldn't try and defend what was rightfully theirs?"

Romulus and Remus broke out laughing. "Too right," said Romulus. "Let's get going," said Remus.

They took all their building materials and their gear up the Aventine and started to build a camp.

Evander came up the hill with his sword drawn but reversed.

Behind him were two hundred or so warriors - certainly more than the hundred or so Romulus and Remus had at their disposal.

"They wouldn't be able to break in," commented Romulus, "but they could beat us in a straight fight."

"Then I suggest not getting into a straight fight," counselled Remus.

"But I do want to get into a fight with them," said Romulus. "They have something I want."

"Palatium," said Remus.

"True, but also the heiress of Carmenta."

"It's the same thing."

Romulus looked at his twin thoughtfully. "I suppose it is," he said, "but now that I've met her, I want her for herself."

"Except you're already married," Remus reminded him.

"My wife is dead to me," Romulus prevaricated. "He's getting close, let's go."

"You know, if you want the Queen, then that's the man you need to kill," said Catalus as they went out through the gate. "If he were to attack you under the gods' truce, I mean, in sight of all his men, just come at you..."

Romulus looked at him quizzically: "do you really think he's likely to do that?"

"Oh no," smirked Catalus, "I'm sure he would only do that if he were provoked beyond reason. And, I mean, you are not so stupid as to offend him by telling him about the affair you're having with his wife behind his back."

"I'm not," shouted Romulus, offended.

"Yeah, I know that," said Catalus. "But he doesn't. So just don't mention it and everything will be fine."

Realization dawned on Romulus as he started walking again. Damn, but Catalus was clever. Like a modern day Ulysses.

They met about a bowshot from the wall. Evander was neither so trusting or so stupid as to come closer.

They met as individuals on the hillside. Their respective armies waited just out of earshot: Evander's 200 further down the hill and Romulus' 100 just outside the wall at its summit.

"When is it going to stop?" demanded Evander, angrily.

"What?" asked Romulus, innocently. "It seems to me that this stand-off can finish whenever you decide to turn around and march your men back through the valley and up that hill and inside those gates."

"I'm not talking about this 'stand-off', you idiot. I am here to demand restitution for the actions of your men."

"My men? What have they been doing then?"

"You know as well as I do that they have been taking sheep from my shepherds down in the valley," Evander answered, gesturing vaguely behind him. "They have taken twenty sheep already this moon. It cannot go on," he insisted.

"Can it not?" enquired Romulus, deliberately raising the tension. "In that case, by all means, I suggest you stop it. I am unaware of my men taking anything that does not belong to them, although I can certainly ask around..." He waved his hand airily as if to suggest that nothing would come of such enquiries. "But I would suggest that if you find sheep are going missing that you look closer to home. I mean, surely if there were thieves abroad in the night, your very competent-looking guards could surely deal with them?" And again, he waved his hand, this time in the general direction of the men waiting down the hill.

"What do you mean, closer to home? Do you deny stealing my sheep?" thundered Evander, loud enough for both armies to hear what he had to say.

"Of course I deny it, my dear chap," said Romulus reasonably. "As I say, I can make a few enquiries, but I don't see how my men could even get out of our fort, let alone near your sheep. Quite frankly, the whole idea is preposterous. It seems much more likely that one of your men is involved in, shall we say, extra-curricular activities. And not always with your wife."

"What? What about my wife?" retorted Evander, spitting and starting to wave his sword around.

"Well, you know, even over here, we hear certain things, and your wife does have a bit of a, shall we say, reputation? I imagine that diverting a few sheep would be a good way of raising the funds to impress her..."

"Who? Who are you accusing?" yelled Evander, now threatening Romulus with the sword.

Romulus took a couple of steps backwards and started to raise his hands, the right still holding the spear.

At which point, Evander completely lost it.

The leading men of both armies gathered around Romulus and a seriously wounded Evander.

"I have no idea," Romulus answered Servius, Evander's second in command. "He accused us of stealing sheep, and I denied it. As far as I know, none of my men has been out at night wandering the hillside, never mind stealing sheep. And I don't see how they could have managed to dispose of a sheep without the smell of roasting flesh coming to my attention.

"I made those points to him, but he seemed disinclined to believe me. But it was when I mentioned the possible perfidy within your own ranks that he lost it and attacked me. I don't really know why, but he seemed to take the suggestion personally. Have you had those kinds of discipline issues?"

Servius looked at the man closely. He had no doubt that Romulus, scum that he was, was lying to him. Evander's temper was well known in Palatium; it wouldn't surprise him if this man knew that too and had devised a plausible plan to exploit it. But none of that would help him here. It was undeniable that Evander had attacked Romulus, even though he should have known better.

"No, of course not," he responded at length. "We all respected and admired Evander," he said, exaggerating slightly, knowing what was almost inevitable now. "But, whatever the provocation," he added, clearly indicating that he believed something more had been said, "I accept that he should not have attacked you. But his death expiates that offence against the gods. We will now retreat to our city to choose a new king."

"No," contradicted Romulus.

Servius and the others froze. What did he mean? Servius looked at him.

"His death expiates the sin of attacking me, but does not expiate his crime of accusing me. For that, I demand restitution."

"What do you demand?"

"A place in the funeral games."

Servius' expression froze in shock. Any doubts he had about whether all of this had been planned dissipated in a moment. Of course it had. Yes, Romulus might not be the brightest pea in the pod, but he could probably beat any of the other contenders likely to take part in the funeral games. He looked around and his eyes fixed on Catalus, a gentle smirk on his face and laughter rolling around his eyes. Yes, that was the one. The brains of the operation. Just not quite smart enough to be able to conceal it. He wanted you to know how clever he was. A serious flaw if you actually want to be that cunning. But Servius could not deny it: he was trapped. As the Queen's son, he obviously could not participate in the games. But he would rather it went to one of his friends than this man, boor that he was.

That he might end up marrying his mother didn't bear thinking about.

In the end, four men came forward to claim the throne.

In the eyes of the gods, it was all done to honour the man who had died. These men risked life and limb in combat to honour a man who had risked - and lost - all.

The sword fights were to first blood, of course. Each victor then drew a lot in the next round until only one man was left standing. With only four combatants, there were two rounds.

And Romulus, accomplished thug that he was, won both his matches easily. And claimed the prize.

Before the games began, the pyre had been prepared and Evander's body placed on it. At noon, the games had begun and a short while later, Romulus was declared victor. His first task was to light the pyre.

He took the torch that had been prepared and followed the priestess to the hearth of the constant flame. In these modern days, starting a fire was not so hard: a variety of techniques were available; most men now carried a flint, an iron striker and some tinder with them everywhere they went. But there had been a temple that held the constant flame in villages up and down the peninsula since time immemorial. And it was always there that the flame was found to light the torch for the pyre.

He knelt down and plunged the dry kindling into the heart of the fire. As it caught, he twisted it back and forth until not just the outer kindling, but the pitch-infused pine was alight. Then he removed it from the fire and, holding it stretched out before him, walked back to the pyre. As he approached, the priestesses chanted the words of power to ensure that Evander's soul would separate from his body and go wherever it was that souls went when they were freed from this mortal coil.

As the chanting reached its peak, Romulus stretched out his arm and buried the flame in the pyre.

As he watched it burn, his mind turned to the rest of his prize: Carmenta. She was very pretty. But, more than that, she was the key to absolute power. He now had two villages.
