8. Oschophoria

"The vines have brought in their bounty, now, Wife. It is more than a moon since the firstfruits. It is high time we celebrated the Oschophoria."

Hera looked at him sullenly. "Of all the festivals, that is my least favourite," she replied. "I do not feel that it sufficiently honours the Goddess."

"So, woman?" snapped Zeus. "You have your festivals to honour the Goddess."

"And you have your hunts and sacrifices to the Titans," she retorted. "Don't pretend you don't. Do you need another festival of drinking and eating?"

"You know as well as I do that the Oschophoria celebrates the harvest of the vine," he responded slowly. "There is no eating. Well, except grapes."

"Which just makes all of you more drunk," sighed Hera. "But I suppose if we must, we must. We must express our gratitude for the vine harvest. Just don't make me come."

"But you and Athene have to be there - you must cut and bear the vine branches!"

"Are there any still heavy with grapes?"

"A few, but only a few: that is why I say it is high time we celebrated the Oschophoria. And you and Athene must be there to bear the vines from the Goddess to Dionysius. And Athene must be there to award the prizes."

"To be the prize, I rather think you mean. At least I know you can't win."

He smirked at her: "Any time you want, any time at all. Just say the word."

The procession began in the vineyard.

Athene acknowledged the gifts of the Goddess, and that she was taking more than the fruit here - she was taking two whole branches of the vine. Even though they would be pruning the vines soon, that was not the same: that was done for the vine, which could not prune itself, ordained by the Goddess and shown in the healthiness of the vine in years to come. On the contrary, this was simple theft - taking what was growing, and thus belonged to the Goddess, away from her and giving it to Dionysius on the grounds that he represented some legend that his ancestors had brought the vine from somewhere to the north.

A likely story, thought Athene, once again. All that the men had brought from the north was violence and destruction into what had once been a peaceful and loving land. Even as she communed with the Goddess, she knew her power was waning. Once she had reigned supreme and mere men had had no say in her counsels. Now, that was changing and she often thought Zeus had more power in the village than Hera. It was the tribute that did it, of course: by having other villages in submission to them - to him, in all truth - the power of Hera, Athene and the Goddess, drawn just from this one villaged, diminished. She could sense the power in the man and knew her Goddess did too.

The violence she was about to commit against her own Goddess spoke of the imbalance of power in the village.

She took the ritual sickle in her left hand and grasped a branch near the base of the bush in her right. The ritual of destruction against a worthy vine required her to use her left hand: if she wielded the sickle with her right hand, she would declare the vine unworthy or diseased: a crime against nature. She could not do that.

With three sharp strokes, the branch came away in her hand. She turned and, bowing, passed it to Hera. Hera carefully accepted it, and placed it over her shoulder gently, ensuring she didn't damage any of the grapes or lesser branches.

Athene turned back to the vine and selected another branch. Again grasping it with her right hand, she slashed the sickle twice and the branch came away. Turning to her acolyte, she passed the sickle, then carefully lifted the branch onto her left shoulder.

Turning, Hera and Athene left the vineyard, the poor, abused vine weeping for its lost branches. Truly, the practices of these men who had conquered them were barbaric. But none could deny they had the power.

Dionysius was waiting for them outside the Megara.

Inside, everything had been set up for the drinking and the games. Two tables had been prepared in the centre of the dais for the branches. Hera went to the right and laid hers there; Athene laid hers on the table on the left. Then the two of them knelt in front of their respective tables. In one sense, the vines now "belonged" to Dionysius; but no man could take any grapes from them for himself. They had to wait for Athene's approval: then take them from her hand.

Meanwhile, Zeus did what Zeus did best: organized the men.

The first event was sword combat to first blood. Sometimes things went wrong and men could be injured - even killed - although it was hard enough to kill a man when you were trying, and in these games trying to kill your opponent was more likely to end up with Zeus thrusting his sword through your back.

The first duel was between Hector and Achilles. If he won here, Hector would probably be an officer by the end of the year. Face facts, Zeus told himself, he would probably be an officer within a year whatever happened. Achilles, now, thought Zeus.

Bravery had its place in the army. You couldn't go into battle without courage, but at the same time, there were so many other attributes you needed. Strength, discipline, coordination, height, speed. Yes, Achilles had all of those as well, in vast quantities, thought Zeus. But you also needed obedience and respect as well as a sense of teamwork. And Achilles had none of those: he was one who must be obeyed. And that, thought Zeus, could cause him a problem soon: come the New Year, Achilles could easily be challenging him for the leadership of the village, especially if he won here. And if he could beat Hector, he could quite possibly beat Zeus. Still, the will of the Goddess and all that. Zeus settled in to watch the bout with care. Both men knew the other well, and often drilled together. They were well-matched, if very different in style.

Zeus indicated the horn should be blown. When it blew again, both combatants had to stop fighting at once. Carrying on, even just one last blow, could be lethal.

The moment the horn sounded, Achilles leapt forward from his starting position and jumped to the attack. Zeus had expected this, and so had Hector who moved forward and slightly to the left to give himself some space and position himself for the onslaught. Achilles attacked with a three-blow combination which Hector easily deflected. His parries were true and in time and was then able to snap off a chanced blow at Achilles' shoulder which was easily parried in turn.

Achilles did not let up. He launched another combination, trying to force Hector backwards and find a way through his elegant parries. Hector continued moving left, eventually managing to get his back to the open space in the centre of the Megara. He allowed Achilles to rain blows on him, slowly working his way backwards, always forcing Achilles to reach further to land his next blow.

Suddenly he moved forward and lunged inside Achilles' reach. A desperate turn and downswing effected a parry without injury, but Achilles knew the move had outfoxed him and he jumped backwards. Hector now launched an attack of his own, a flurry of blows forcing Achilles back.

Hector swung a backhand blow straight into Achilles' parry. As the swords came together, Hector's sword shattered and pieces flew everywhere. As Hector raised his hands in defeat, Achilles flicked his sword out to lay a cut across Hector's chest. And then laughed at him.

Zeus was outraged. This was a desecration of the noble fight. But what could he do?

"I declare Achilles the winner and Hector's bronze sword the loser," he quickly declared. "A good fight, well fought. Now hold hands and congratulate each other."

As they bowed their heads, Athene placed a crown of laurel on Achilles' head, and handed him a bunch of grapes.

Nobody noticed as Hector slipped out shortly afterwards. Inside he was fuming. He would have his revenge on Achilles, he swore. But how?

The wine continued to flow.

By now, most of the men were incapacitated by drink, but fortuately the trials with weapons were long over. The feats of strength - lifting and wrestling - had now ended too, and only the trials of speed were left. Only the final trial remained: considered the ultimate - for no reason Zeus could fathom. Pairs of cadets had been running the length of the Megara as fast as they could. As each contest concluded, one of the cadets was handed a bunch of grapes in consolation and went to sit down. The winners proceeded to the next round. Now only two cadets remained. Polyxius and Heradenus lined up on the start line. For some reason, this seemed to matter more than the previous rounds - maybe because the ultimate prize was within touching distance. Literally.

The horn sounded on Zeus' signal and both men set off across the Megara. Each had already won three contests and they were closely matched. As they sprinted across the floor, it was hard to tell who was winning. At the finish line, painted white on the Megara floor, Apollo was sitting, given the difficult task of adjudicating. When he signalled, it was to indicate that Polyxius had won by a hair.

Both of them walked up to the dais and bowed low before Athene. Taking the last bunch of grapes from the vine, she offered them to Heradenus. Taking them, he went and sat down with the other cadets. Now we entered the time-honoured ritual which represented the pinnacle of the evening.

"I have no more grapes to offer you," intoned Athene.

"Does the vine have no more?" asked Polyxius.

"No," responded Athene. "There will be no more until the Goddess grants next year."

"Can you be sure of her bounty?" asked Polyxius, the ritual words dripping from him. "Can we be sure the vine will remember to flower and give us fruit?"

"I have faith in the Goddess," responded Athene, "but I am sure that she would approve us showing nature that we understand the workings of bounty."

And she unfastened the shoulder straps of her dress and let it fall to the ground. Reaching forward, she removed Polyxius' loincloth to find him already hard beneath.

"The Goddess truly is good," she murmured into his ear as she pushed him backwards and straddled him, honouring the Goddess in the traditional manner.
