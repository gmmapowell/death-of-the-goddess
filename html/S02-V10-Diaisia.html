10. Diasia

As the sun set, the women watching the stars rise saw the sisters appear. As they did, they sent a runner immediately to Rhea. She would know what it meant and what to do.

"It is the time of purification and placation," she declared. "When this moon settles, this year will have come to an end, and the new year will be upon us. When the year has but half a moon left, we will celebrate the Diasia. All those with jobs to do, should start to do them now."

Cronos led the hunting party that night. He knew that in less than a moon he would be joining the other spirits in the nether world and would desire that same connection with the living world. He, too, would want to be fed and placated and, he was sure, would be angry at those who withheld their largesse - or even stinted in their tasks. And, he swore to himself under his breath, he would have his revenge on those who did not feed him.

They hid in a thicket, patiently waiting for the boar to come and drink from the pond. As leader, Cronos had the most dangerous task: approaching the boar and attaching the first rope to its neck. Once done, the boar would not be able to escape since the other end of the rope was already tied around a tree, but there was nothing stopping the boar turning and attacking him. In general, a boar's first instinct was to flee, and often it was only that instinct that kept the hunters alive. The boar could not be killed - ideally, should not even be injured - since both its blood, its vitality, and its flesh were part of the offering to the spirts to be placated at the Diasia.

From their right, they heard a rustling as something moved through the bushes. In the dim light of the lessening moon, they could pick out motion, but not a clear outline of an animal. But drawing on all his experience, Cronos knew that this was a boar. And would be the first one he would take tonight. Combining all his senses, he stared out into the dark and "saw" the boar as it moved through the rustling bushes and reached the edge of the pond. Even before it had finished moving, he had tapped his colleagues on their shoulders and slipped noiselessly from his hiding place down the pathway he had reconnoitred earlier in the day. Noiselessly, he crept up on the boar and within heartbeats was alongside the shoulder of the beast. He counted five heartbeats to allow his colleagues to get into position, then reached forward and guided the loop of rope over the boar's head and down on its neck before pulling tightly, releasing the remaining rope and running back from whence he had come.

So skilfully had Cronos trapped the boar that by the time he stopped, a safe distance away, that the boar had not moved, but just stood there quietly sniffing the air, sure that it was threatened but still trying to identify the source of the threat and which way it should run. The tickling on its neck irritated it, and it trashed its head in an attempt to clear the brush away. When that didn't work, it started to realise there was something attached to it, and struggled harder to get away. Only then did it truly understand that it was under attack; roaring its bellow, it started to charge - unsure of the location of its attacker, it struck straight forward, directly into the pond and sank in the mud up to its belly. Within seconds, it was immobilized and thrashing for its life.

The hunters came back to the pond and looked at the boar thrashing itself towards its death.

"If it can't get itself out, can we? It is so strong."

"True," responded Cronos, "but do not forget that it is pushing against the mud, which gives no resistance. But we have the ropes to pull it out. When it has thrashed all its energy and rage away, we will be able to pull it out. When you can, attach your ropes and be ready to pull. Tie yours around the belly of the beast; that will give us good leverage. Truly the Goddess shows her favour on us; this boar shows his strength while at the same time wasting it trying to thash its way out of an impossible situation. When he is worn out, we will pull him free."

Looking around, Cronos saw a nearby tree.

"I am going to climb that tree with my rope and make sure his head is kept above water. It would not do to let him drown while pulling him out."

And with that, Cronos claimed the slack of his rope and started to climb.

After dark, they lit the bonfire.

Before long, it was roaring upwards, the branches crackling and settling onto the larger trunks beneath. Slowly but steadily, these caught too and started to glow a dull orange. On one side of the fire stood Cronos and a temporary enclosure which had been built to house the boars. As ritual demanded, as the intermediary between the living and the spirits that had gone before, Cronos stood on the fleece which marked him out as the messenger: in a place between worlds; if not a spirit himself, he was detached from the world of the living and separated from its concerns by a token of death.

On the other side of the raging fire stood Rhea, likewise separated from the world of the living by a ritual fleece. She stood naked, exposing all that she was to the spirits so that they might know her, recognise her and judge her on this day of purification and placation. For if the spirits were not placated today, who knew what harms they might visit on her village. Better then, that they should choose to take her in compensation for their failure than wreak vengeance on the whole village. That was what it meant to stand surety for the placating of the spirits.

"Oh spirits, know that we summon you today. Today is the annual day of the Diasia, the day in which we feed you and right your wrongs. You cannot live up here with us, for your time is past. But we know, on this darkest day of the year, that life will come again. As the moon turns this time, the seeds will sprout and grow ready for the harvest. And we know that, in the same way, we who live here today will one day be spirits like you, dependent on the charity of our children for sustenance at this dark time of year. And one day, you spirits will return to life to keep the chain unbroken.

"We have lived this year in accordance with the commands of the Goddess. You need not fear for us, nor need you fear that we will fail in our duty to you. Come, take our offerings! Be warm! Feel the glow that we send to you! Come near! Eat, and be filled! Today is the day of the Diasia and we will feed you!"

And with that, she lowered her head and stretched out her arms. "Now," she whispered fiercely and above the roar of the fire, "now, Cronos. Let the feast begin!"

Expertly, the handlers pulled the first boar forward. As it stepped onto the fleece of offering, Cronos reached forward and hit it with the ritual hammer. As it slumped, he reached forward and slit its throat with the flint knife kept for this ceremony. Quickly, before too much of the blood could escape, the handlers picked it up and tossed it onto the fire. Within moments, the fire started to consume it, the smell of roasting flesh filled the air.

"Come!" should Rhea from across the fire. "Come and eat! We offer you fresh boar captured in this phase of the moon, just for you! Come, O spirits! Come and dine!"

As the fire consumed the boar, Cronos and his handlers repeated the process. By the time they had finished, four boars had been offered for the placation of spirits. Then all the villagers, led by Cronos and Rhea, stood and watched as the fire burned down and consumed all of the boars. Nothing was left to be eaten by the villagers.

But that was the way it had to be. The Diasia was for the benefit of the spirits, not the villagers. As long as they were placated, nothing else mattered.
