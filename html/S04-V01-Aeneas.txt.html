39 Aeneas

Troy was no more, Aeneas reflected as the ship bore into land, and the story was spreading across the sea.

It was no fun being the leader of a band of mercenaries whose most famous achievement was to have seen their city destroyed. Didi of Carthage - a trivial colony really, set on a fertile strip by the desert - had basically laughed in his face when he proposed marriage. And then he hadn't even managed to beat her current husband in single combat.

No, they needed to find somewhere where "the Trojans" were not a laughingstock. Somewhere beyond the reach of the Greek colonies. Sicilia was out, because they had been rebuffed there and his father Anchises had been killed in a battle on the beach. North, then, north above Sicilia. Not as far as Massilia; and certainly not one of the other islands: they all had Greek colonies and would have heard the story Agamemnon had spread far and wide. How mighty Troy had fallen. Agamemnon never mentioned the treachery that had sold the city out - that had betrayed Priam and Hector, Anchises and Aeneas. No, he told a story of glory and honour. And heroes.

And that was the story that would stick.

Aeneas looked around his ship at the men rowing. They looked a motley bunch, hard and desperate. Forged in war - a war to defend their homeland. A band of men who had learned the hard way that you can only get what you take in life. A group of homeless exiles desperate for a new start.

Desperate. Yes, that was the word to describe all of them. They were desperate.

There were times when Aeneas almost found himself hoping that Poseidon would send a mighty wave and crush their fleet, sending all of them to the bottom of the sea.

What Agamemnon had known of course was that Troy held to the old way, the old beliefs and that once Hecuba was dead, the city was dead. Rape and kill the "old woman" and there would be nothing left. In his telling, of course, the rape and murder happened after the city fell, when there were a thousand heroes running through the streets.

He nearly barked with laughter. Not so!

And that magic touch: that Paris, prince of Troy, had abducted Helen, from some random kingdom in Greece. Not that the Greeks had tried to abduct the heiress of Troy, and been killed in the process, starting the inevitable rounds of retribution.

After Dido, he had decided he would abandon the old ways. No more kowtowing to women, the ways of women, the gods of women. What Aeneas had learnt from the sorry saga of the last decade was that might is right, that power is the only thing that lasts, and that men win wars; women lose them. He was going to go somewhere, anywhere, on the coast here and take land. If it came with women, good; if not, he would forge his own society, find women from somewhere, kidnap and enslave them. Whatever it took.

He was going to be a King in the Greek style.

"Beach ho," called the lookout.

A good place, thought Aeneas. He was fairly sure there were no Greek colonies here. The beach was rocky and covered in pebbles, but sloped up slowly to the land, densely forested, with a stream coming down from the higher ground. They still had some casks of water on board the ships, but fresh water was a requirement if they were going to stop here and build a kingdom.

He climbed higher in the rigging of the single mast and looked out to see what else he could see. There were no obvious signs of habitation, but if they had a lookout, he would be hidden in the woods anyway. They were hardly likely to keep their entire army on the beach at all times.

He signalled to the other two ships in his flotilla. He had started off with five ships when they left Troy. They had been split up from time to time, but these three had come back together. One had been lost in the battle in Sicily that cost his father's life. The other had become separated from them during a storm as they had crossed back across the sea a few weeks back. He still hoped to catch sight of it every day, but he had to accept that even if Poseidon hadn't claimed their lives, they were probably irretrievably lost to him. The odds of meeting up at a random point on this deserted coastline were basically zero.

The three ships headed into the coastline in a triangle, his in the lead, with the other two covering his beams. The rowers took them in as quickly as they could until he signalled for them to reverse stroke. In seconds, the speed had sloughed off and the ship gently ran aground, then twisted to port as it wedged. Aeneas leapt over the side into the gentle surf and ran up the beach. The off watch followed him.

The others stowed their oars and dropped the drag anchors over the side. Then, leaving the small anchor watch of five men with their trumpets behind, they followed him into the surf as well.

Discipline first and foremost. Taking the marker sticks, Aeneas marked out a square, twenty paces to a side above the waterline, but near where the stream came through. As soon as he had done so, the others filled in, quickly assembling a rudimentary, fortified camp behind which they would be safe if they were attacked.

The locals arrived early the following morning.

Aeneas estimated there probably weren't more than twenty of them, always allowing for the possibility that this was a vanguard and the main force was following behind or were lying in ambush in case they became overconfident.

"OK, men, up you get. Form arms!"

Quickly the men who had not been on sentry duty gathered their arms and armour and, as soon as they were all ready, opened the gate in the rear fence and marched out. In what seemed no time, three ranks of thirty men were lined up in front of the camp, spears to the ready.

The locals waited on the edge of the woods, apparently uncertain what to do.

Aeneas had his doubts too, but when faced with an impossible situation, there was only one choice: attack, but with safety in mind. Leaving his remaining men to hold the fortified camp, he gave the order for a slow advance. No point rushing up the gentle slope to arrive downslope of the enemy; there was plenty of time in the day. But he wasn't going to wait to give battle: these men had homes to go to; he needed to defeat them in order to ever have a home again. Troy was lost to him forever.As they came near to the enemy line, the locals charged downhill.

"Level spears!", shouted Aeneas.

As one, the men lowered their spears to face forward. As the enemy rushed headlong, they crashed into the spears; his men responded by thrusting them forward and impaling them. The screams of the enemy mingled with the shouts of his men, determined to win here.

"Forward step!", called Aeneas from his position in the middle of the front rank.

Every man took a step forward, and thrust again. By now, half of the attackers were down, and most of the others had some wounds. Those still standing took a step or two back, unsure how to deal with this new, organized enemy.

"Forward step!", shouted Aeneas again.

Once again, the men stepped forward and thrust their spears at the men who were still standing there. At this, they all turned and ran.

"Beware an ambush!", shouted Aeneas. "Losing ten men is nothing to get a good ambush! Dead detail party, now!"

While the others waited, three men rushed forward and, daggers drawn, rushed to the fallen, dealing each of them a swift blow to the throat, ensuring they could not rise and fight again. With that done, the formation started to move forward again slowly, one step at a time. Now more than ever, there was no incentive to rush.

They reached the treeline without opposition. From here, they could see where the enemy had staged and Aeneas presumed that there would be a clear trail back to wherever they had come from. Any ambush would be one side or the other of that trail.

"Tracker forward! Marching column! Ambush ready! Eyes open!"

Swiftly, the men reorganized themselves. These were good troops, thought Aeneas, whatever the Greek world might have said about them. Organized and disciplined. He and Hector had won every engagement they had fought in front of the walls of Troy, although he had no doubt that that was also in the process of being airbrushed out of history. History was written by the winners. Greek men.

"Tracker, proceed!"

The tracker led the way. In truth, the passage beaten through the woods by twenty men would not have been hard to follow even if it had not been the way the townsfolk would travel to the beach on a day basis. For their skilled tracker, it was almost as easy as walking down to the harbour from the Skean gate. Despite everyone being on the alert, there was no sign of an ambush anywhere in the forest.

Abruptly, the forest ended; Aeneas called a halt. Looking out, they saw a large clearing; nothing like the size of the Trojan plain, but large enough for a sizable village, or possibly even a town.

A few buildings were clustered together, probably four hundred paces or so away.

The tracker came closer and offered his opinions. "Given what I can see, I would have thought this village would maybe support one hundred or two hundred souls. They have the land for that, and there are probably enough huts. So I would think they would have at least 70 men they could throw against us. And we have seen what? 20?"

Aeneas nodded, thoughtfully. He knew as well as anyone else: just because there was enough land here to support 200 villagers didn't mean that there were that many here. They could have died, recently or long ago. There could have been a plague, or they might already have been attacked. They could still be out there waiting, or they could have run away.

But he knew one thing beyond any doubt: once he was out on those fields, he couldn't be ambushed the way he could be in the forest. If the worst came to the worst, he could burn the huts to the ground with any potential ambushers inside.

"Five torches, now! Prepare to burn huts! Keep in the defensive column and keep your eyes open!"

He looked around to make sure his orders were being followed.

"Now! Advance!"

Slowly but steadily, the column moved forward. They walked across the open field without challenge. The silence was almost eerie. Finally, they reached the perimeter of the village.

"Halt! Defensive columns, now! Keep those eyes open! We may be attacked at any time. Prepare to burn huts!"

The men rearranged themselves effortlessly and thrust their spears out on all four sides of the square.

"With me," he said to the tracker and the nearest torchbearer, and together they walked forward. They walked to the nearest hut and looked in. It was deserted. They walked around it and found the hut at the centre of the village.

"Be ready for anything," he counselled quietly. "If it even feels like we are about to be attacked, set fire to it and then let's get back to the men."

They walked into the hut to be greeted by a gaggle of women. Not a man among them.

"Where are your men?" Aeneas asked in Trojan, not even expecting to be understood.

In the middle of the hut, a woman sat on a carved, backless chair placed on a low dais. She raised her eyes to him and said something unintelligible.

"Do you speak Greek?" Aeneas asked, switching to that language.

She motioned to another woman to the left of the dais. Looking at her, Aeneas noticed facial features reminiscent of Greek women he had met in the past. The woman looked up from her kneeling position and quietly said, "I am from Mykenai".

Aeneas felt his rage rise inside. He had to curb his tongue to avoid ordering the whole building being burnt down around him. The Gods damn Agamemnon! Even here, miles from civilization, a woman from Mykenai was here before him. How was this possible?

He turned to her nonetheless. If they were going to get out of this without destroying the entire village, they would need to negotiate. Although the villagers had very little to negotiate with. They could give him the village and all that was in it, or he could burn it down around them.

"Where are your men?" Aeneas asked her, this time in Greek.

"You killed what was left of them", she replied.

"Not so," Aeneas countered. "We killed about half of those who came against us, but the others ran away."

"Cowards," she answered. "But those were all the men left to the village. Men are expendable pawns anyway; one is always enough and I'm sure you are more than capable of placating the Goddess in the way she demands, trampling on our blood moons." She looked at him expectantly.

It could be true. If there were only ten men left, they would be better served running away than staying and fighting. But had they run away or run for help? He needed to decide, and quickly.

But the truth was, the decision had already made itself. This was where he was, and he needed to hold it against those men or any others who tried to evict him. He was sure they would.

"In that case, I am now the King here," declared Aeneas. "This village and everything in it is now my property."

"No," the woman dared to correct him. "Lavinia remains the Queen here. You may gain throne-right by marrying her, but until that you are just a visitor."

"I will come back for your 'Queen' later," Aeneas assured her. "You can use whatever form of words you like, but I am in charge here." He waved her spear at her. "You may say I obtain the right by putting my spear in your Queen, but I assure you that this spear is the one that will command your respect and obedience." He paused. "Or you can all die."

The three of them walked back to the others.

"They claim there are no men left here, but we can be sure some will come from somewhere to contest with us. Build a stockade. Pull this empty building down. When that's done and secure, four of you go back to the beach - keep those eyes peeled for an ambush! - and get all the others and everything you can manage up here. Torch the ships. We don't need them anymore. This is our new home. The New Troy!"

As it happened, they had two weeks to prepare for the inevitable attack.

Not that the locals measured time in days or weeks; they still held to the old summer-moon calendar. They counted the nine months from the rising of the Pleiades as the "year" and then respected the "time of death" until the seven (now six) sisters rose again. Archaic.

No more archaic, of course, than the idea that women ruled the world. Lavinia held to this misguided belief, as did her people, and that had made for some troublesome times over the past two weeks, but Aeneas would not be gainsaid. Lavinia and her people could believe that his right to rule depended on him sleeping with her; but the truth remained that it was his seven foot, metal spear - and those of his men - that enabled him to rule here.

He had learned some of the history of the village over those two weeks as well, and some of the local language, called Latin. Unsurprisingly, the area of the world where he found himself was called Latinum by the locals. They had been attacked by some people from further north, called Etruscans since time immemorial - here that probably meant anything over two generations - and the men who had fought had generally managed to do enough to hold the village but the process of attrition meant that when Aeneas had landed on the beach, Turnus - Lavinia's spouse-cum-warleader - had only had the twenty men he had brought with him at his disposal. In fleeing, he would have been reduced to a mere ten fighting men. The only hope he had was to find allies. Let him.

The preparations for the inevitable battle had proceeded apace and Aeneas had not just built a central fort - the citadel as he thought of it - but had worked to build an outer wall around the central encampment. There was nothing he could do to protect the fields of course - standing crops were always the first thing to be destroyed in a war like this - and he would not give up his strong, defensible position in order to protect anything.

In reality, there wasn't any real need to protect the village or the women in it. If you believed that the right to rule and the right to wealth ran through the female line, then obviously you wanted to claim the women alive. They might be forcibly raped after the battle, but it was probably no worse than what they endured on most nights with their "legitimate" husbands. Certainly Lavinia had accepted him willingly enough: no force required there!

No, while he wouldn't admit it to them, the reason he wanted to keep the women inside the wall was to hold onto them. If Turnus were to return and claim Lavinia, in the eyes of all the locals he would be king, even if he couldn't manage to hold that title in battle with other claimants. It was an antique way of thinking, but Aeneas relished the advantage that came from being the man who knew the truth. A pleasant change after Troy, when he had laboured under the disadvantage of being on the more old-fashioned side. Of course, ten years of war had hardened their forces, and by the final weeks, he and Hector had led the finest forces on the battlefield. They would have won and sent the Greek forces packing if they had not been betrayed ...

He cut off that endless, circular thought. Troy had been betrayed and the traitors were dead and the Greeks had won and he was here in Latinum preparing to defend a pathetic little fort in the middle of nowhere against some local bumpkins. Still, better than the alternative of being in a watery grave somewhere out there at sea.

He was reviewing his defences from the outside when the shout came.

"Soldiers to the north!"

He immediately turned and looked in that direction. He had plenty of time to get back inside the fort before they arrived and from here he was closer to the enemy. In addition to the trees they had hacked down for logs to build his fortlet, they had burnt through significant parts of the forest to clear sight lines and travel lanes. It was now possible to see well over 800 paces to the north without there being anywhere for soldiers to hide. Not that this lot seemed to understand the value of hiding. Maybe they thought that they would just overrun them, and take Lavinia and the village for themselves with no difficulty. Clearly, they had not been at the siege of Troy. Not a Nestor, Agamemnon or Odysseus among them. Time would tell if they had a soldier with the strength and courage of Achilles, Ajax or Ajax.

His initial impression was that they were coming in strength. He could see forty or fifty soldiers emerging from the trees, and they were coming in a way that suggested there were more behind. He turned and looked at his fortlet again. A solid defensive position gave the defenders an enormous advantage - everyone knew that. And yet, in this backwater, there had not been a single defensive structure to stop him: no wall, no citadel, not even a ditch. The biggest obstacle was the river and that was only an obstacle if you were attacking from the north. And given the size of it, only really if you were a child.

It would do, he told himself. It wasn't perfect; but even the strongest structures were only as strong as the men (and women) holding them. He had given all his men clear orders: he would rather kill all the women in the village than run the risk they would betray him to Turnus and his allies. Any - he repeated any - woman who was acting suspiciously or came out of the hut they were assigned to was to be killed. Instantly. No questions. He was not going to be betrayed again.

He walked back around to the south of the fort and walked in through the gate. It was closed and barred behind him. Four reliable men were assigned there at all times. Their job was simple: the gate stayed closed unless he personally ordered it open. Anyone who had a different opinion was to die. Or else they would.

Once again he considered the value of the river as a defensive position. Rivers were hard to cross, and this lot probably didn't know about things like covering fire. But it wasn't as secure as the fortlet, and he would probably lose any men he used to defend it. It would be worth it if ten men could kill forty as they came out of the river, but he really didn't think that would be the case here. This mob would probably stretch the length of the river and cross willy-nilly. His men would probably be quickly surrounded and killed. There was no obvious way to bottle them up: this river didn't need a ford to be crossed.

It was nearly noon by the time the enemy horde had made their way from the forest, crossed the river, and gathered on the south bank opposite him. Already Aeneas despised this foe. They had just sat down where they were when they arrived, a mass of humanity resting and sunning themselves. Their baggage had been dumped wherever they finished up, and there was no order to anything.

Pothides came up to him. "Do you think," he asked, "that we could sally out and drive them back into the river before they could even pick up their weapons?"

Aeneas looked over the troops before him. He had been wondering something similar. A simultaneous attack from east and west, using the river behind them as an anvil - it would be a strategy. But he was drastically outnumbered. Given he would have to leave at least twenty men behind to secure the fort, could seventy get the job done? Was it worth it?

"Three ranks of twelve each side? Could we?" he asked Pothides. "It just defies all credibility." He glanced around again, counting groups of men as he did so. "At least 300, I'd say. If they attack us, the wall helps us out, but if we go out there, we could easily be flanked, surrounded and eliminated. That's one for the poets my friend. If you want to die a hero's death, that's one way to do it."

"We did stupider things in front of Troy," Pothides countered.

"Did we, in truth?" asked Aeneas. "Never with such small numbers. My company never had less than 200; and Hector generally wanted 1,000 men with him. And we had a strongly held city behind us. This is not the same. Even 90 men could not hold this fort against Agamemnon. 20 would be impossible, even against this rabble. No, I think we have to stay here." Wistful for the strategy that could have been with just another fifty men, he looked out over the field, wondering just how primitive and stupid these men were.

"And thrust!" he shouted as the enemy came again.

When they had come, it had been predictably disorganized. They had come as individuals and groups, and thrown themselves against the wall, trying to climb it and get inside. Did they even have missile weapons? Did they not appreciate how difficult it was with the height advantage he had built into the wall by digging a ditch on the outside and piling the earth up inside? This was the dumbest thing he had ever seen. The men on the wall had an easy time of it: pick a target, thrust your spear, and wait for the scream. From the roof of the Megaron, he and Pothides could see the battle playing out in front of them. There was no plan to the attack, just men rushing forward to the slaughter.

"Thrust again!" he called. Not that the men needed his encouragement: difficult as it would be to get over the wall, any man who managed it would bring death to those men around him even with the short, stabbing swords that they carried.

Aeneas looked over to where the 20-man reserve unit stood behind them. He nodded at them and leaned over to Pothides: "Time to take them in the rear?"

Pothides looked out and saw what Aeneas meant. The enemy were two or three deep trying to navigate the ditch, the dead bodies, the wall and the spears. Those that came too close were dying, the others were milling about. A disciplined charge now would break them - physically and mentally. It was a risk, Pothides knew, and knew that Aeneas knew. The reserve was a reserve. They didn't have enough men for a sally company. But it was a question of risk and reward. They might need the reserve later, but only if the battle went on later. Break them now and there would be no need for fighting later.

And they had only lost two men so far; other than that, they were holding their own.

"Yes," he agreed. "Now would be the time."

"What are you waiting for?" he asked and turning, shouted to the men guarding the gate. "Open for sally out!" he shouted. "Then as you were."

Pothides jumped off the roof and ran to the reserve company. "On me!" he shouted, and ran with them to the gate which opened just before they arrived and closed after the last man passed through. He led them on a tangent away from the village, to the river and then back behind the men still attacking the wall. Forming up, he called "Charge!" and ran into the backs of the men waiting there, a glittering wall of steel spears leading the way. Within two charges, the enemy were breaking and running for the river.

"Discipline!" called Pothides. "About face! Charge!" A rout was always difficult to manage. Men threw their weapons and armour away, believing that their only hope of salvation lay in their speed. But any man that held on to his dagger or sword knew better: just kill the man nearest you and you know he won't kill you. Among these sheep fleeing for their lives would be a few wolves. You always had to be on the look out for the wolves.

"Beware wolves! Beware daggers! Look out for swords! And charge!" he shouted.

The river was soon running red with blood.

Aeneas lost four men in total that day. The handful of enemy survivors were drafted in to replace them.

This is the life for me, thought Aeneas as he lay in bed with Lavinia that night. Let her think she is important if she wants to, but nobody is going to stop me being King in my lifetime. There's nobody here that can manage it. Even these Etruscans don't scare me.
