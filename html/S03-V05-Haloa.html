5. Haloa

"To the river, ye mystics!"

As she spoke those words, Athene led the villagers down to the river for the ritual of purification. Without being pure, they could not truly express their gratitude for all the blessings they had received. And all too well did they know the importance of purity and gratitude: why, without gratitude, would the Goddess continue to bless them with all the bounty they knew every harvest?

The walk to the river was solemn, not joyous: the rite of purification was not a joyous rite, but one in which each individual needed to recognize their own part in the life of the community, for both good and bad. Each man and woman walking down to the river had the opportunity to consider, in private and in detail, every action they had taken over the past year.

Year to year, season to season, life repeated itself: the planting, the waiting, the growth, the harvest, the plenty, the joys of autumn and then back to the time of burial and planting: the new life waiting to spring up as the seasons turned once more.

And their own lives were no different: feasts followed by famines; sex followed by pregnancy followed by birth; the interleaving of peace and war; parents rearing children who in turn became parents rearing yet more children.

And in the same way, the festivals observed the cycle of pollution and purification. Everyday activities led to pollution: from the simple act of dirtying one's hands in the soil to the extreme acts of taking life, life so carefully nurtured by the Goddess. And, of course, there was the metaphorical pollution as well: acts of cruelty and unkindness to fellow villagers. Only through the frequent acts of purification could anyone - male or female - expect to continue in the cycle of life with the Goddess.

Which is why the men of the village accompanied the women down to the river. Ritually prohibited from entering into congress with the Goddess herself, and thus from the full celebration of the Haloa, they were nevertheless included in the ritual of purification which preceded it.

They walked en masse; there was no sense of order or procession to the progress to the river. In some sense, Athene led the way, but it wasn't like there was a single villager who didn't know the way to the river, but it was up to each individual to find their own way: that was part of the ritual of purification. Finding your own way to the river was a metaphor for finding your own way to the Goddess: each individual was unique, yet formed part of a community. Each life was unique, just like each year was unique, but the pattern remained the same, year upon year, generation upon generation.

Even families did not travel together. As ever, the village collaborated effortlessly to make sure the little ones did not get lost or come to grief, but there was no sense today any more than any other day that a mother or father should be responsible for their children, or that this was a ritual of family purification. Each adult sought out their own purification and the children wandered along with the crowd of villagers, thinking less about purification and more about playing on a lovely day out.

And it was a lovely day today. The midsummer heat was starting to fade as they set out with the sun little more than a span above the western horizon. The long summer days brought joy and, now that the harvest had all been gathered in, the hardest of the work was behind them too. This was truly the best time of the year. Anyone who struggled to praise the Goddess on a day like today had real issues.

Athene stripped off at the riverside and walked in to the water.

It simply wasn't possible to purify their clothing and nobody would want those impurities dragging them down as they tried to purify themselves for the Goddess. Athene - as the chosen of the Goddess, leading the women in her worship - would remain naked for the rest of the festival. Others would follow her example - the junior priestesses, devoted to the worship of the Goddess, of course - while any married women who wished could dress again if they chose - in new or freshly cleaned garments, of course. The men, excluded from the ceremony, would generally don a clean loincloth for the walk back to the megara.

Athene immersed herself in the waters, covering herself in the gift of the Goddess, purifying every inch of her body. On the inside, she focused entirely on the bounty of the Goddess and the joy that came from being her representative on earth. As she felt the waters wash over and through her, she felt the release from all the pollution that had accreted to her over the past year.

Hera followed Athene into the water. Although married to Zeus, she would also remain naked for the evening's festivities. Only naked did she ever feel truly close to the Goddess, in the same way that only naked did she ever feel truly close to Zeus. She often wondered what it would be like to be Athene, bound not to a man of this earth, with a man's thoughts and desires, but bound rather to the Goddess, ethereal and remote. But now she sank down into the river, choosing to immerse herself in the shallower waters, rather than walking out into the deep waters of midriver.

Slowly, all the villagers followed the two women in. A mixture of men and women, all of them stripped off on the riverbank, metaphorically stripping off their impurities and then stepping into the river of life. Some walked into the middle of the river until the waters covered them; some sank down in the shallow waters; and others swam into the river and dived down to the bottom. What you did mattered little; the important thing was to partake in the purification that came from the Goddess, offered by the river.

As they felt themselves washed free of pollution, the villagers came back out of the river and stood, dripping but drying in what was left of the evening sun. There were no rules as to how long you had to stay in the water: each stayed according to their own sense of the ongoing purification. Athene stayed a long while in the middle of the river, floating up to breathe and then allowing herself to sink back down, once more to commune with the Goddess who ruled her life. But even she eventually moved back to the bank and walked out.

As the sun finally set in the west, all of the villagers turned to face the orange disc and say the ritual prayer of purification:

    Thank you for our lives

    Thank you for our year

    Thank you for the bounty you give us

    Thank you for the peace you bring

    

    Now we are cleansed

    Now we are pure

    Now we can give thanks

    As the year turns over

And as the sun sank below the horizon, the villagers turned the other way, to face east, and to see the silver disc of the moon rising behind them.

    

    We give thanks for your constancy

    We give thanks for your mercy

    We give thanks for your care

    We give thanks for your bounty

And then, as one, they turned and walked back to the village.

The return trip had more the feel of a procession.

The women led the way; the inferiors - men and children both - followed behind, since they were excluded from what would follow. Athene and Hera led from the front, and the women tended to bunch up in groups behind them - the priestesses together, staying close to Athene; family groups together closer to Hera. And they walked with more determination now: this was not a wandering to the river weighed down by pollution, but a march to an appointment with the Goddess, cleansed and pure.

As they arrived at the Megara, Athene stood poised, with her arms outstretched, communing with the Goddess. The others arrived in turn and formed up behind her. Then she turned and face the villagers.

"All have now been through the ritual of purification. Those who feel themselves pure may now enter in the hall of offering. Those others may now leave."

None of the women left; the men and the children moved away to go and camp for the night in the fields, away from the luxuries and pollution of the village, and to be as close to the Goddess as their poor forms could manage.

Athene turned again and led the women into the Megara.

The tables had been laid out earlier in the day, before the ritual of purification. All of the first fruits were here: cereals and fruits: figs, grapes and nuts. Across all the tables was a lavish spread.

Athene spread her arms and gave obeisance to the Goddess

    

    Oh, Goddess, we share with you

    All that you have first given us

    Knowing that we are pure and ready

    And that you have our ever gratitude

And with that, the women laid into the feast, a feast that would last all night.
