4. The Senate

"Don't think you can do it all by yourself, Romulus."

"And what, I should hamstring myself with some talking shop of fools?"

"Fools, is it? Hmm. It didn't seem that way when I told you how to kill Evander in plain sight of everyone and still get away with it. If I remember correctly, the word Ulysses passed your lips."

Romulus waved the comment away. "The thing is," he said, "only one man can be King. And that's me. Don't try and tell me otherwise. It just won't work."

"Not so," countered Catalus, as ever with an example to hand. "It's common in a lot of the Greek communities to have two kings. One to lead the armies, and one to rule at home when the army is on campaign."

"But that is not what you are suggesting, is it?" argued Romulus. "You are suggesting something along the lines of letting every adult male in on the job of governing. We would be overrun before everyone had even managed to get their long-winded speeches out. We are young; we are small. You know as well as I do that we are under threat from all sides - Sabines, Etruscans - hell, even some of my own people are conspiring against me!"

"And can you blame them?" asked Catalus reasonably. "You promised them the earth, and a share in ruling it. And now you have what you came for, what do we have? We're living in some little stockade that can't hold to candle to Alba; we don't even have any women - except the few who came with their men - and we hardly ever see you: the man who is supposed to be our king. Mutiny is about the least of your problems if you don't address it."

"So, what do you want me to do about it?" asked Romulus, irritably.

"I've told you what you need to do: you need to appoint a consultative body to discuss issues and make suggestions. You need to listen to them and answer their concerns. And you can take advantage of it by delegating a lot of the boring - and undesirable - tasks to them: either as a body or as individuals. You could view them as a body that does a lot of the thinking for you and then you just make decisions. You could even appoint a couple of them - probably on a rotating basis - to keep your informed of their thinking; advanced knowledge as it were."

"Of mutiny, you mean?"

"Well, that would be an extreme case. Let's face it, they are going to be going out scouting. They will hear things. They can get together and discuss things. Then the - shall we call them praetors? - can come to you and brief you on what's happening. You wouldn't need to spend hours in long discussions, but everybody would feel more involved in the process."

Romulus nodded. All that made sense.

"Make it so."

"Oh no, my friend. You need to make it so. Stop humping your pretty wife for a day or two and come over to the Aventine. We'll sort it all out then."

The men gathered under a clear blue sky in the centre of the camp they had established on the Aventine.

The moment he arrived, Romulus could feel the tension. He understood what it was that Catalus had sensed - that the mood was ugly and ready for mutiny. As yet, he did not understand why. He hoped that Catalus' solution worked, although he had serious doubts about that.

He looked around him, noting faces here and there. Yes, some of these men were ready to overthrow him, possibly even murder him. Maybe they thought of themselves in his place, but could they really? Did they have the blood? Still, if you believed that all it took to be King was to be married to the Queen, then anyone had the blood.

"My friends, we grew up in Alba together; we have travelled here together; we have fought together on many occasions; and together we conquered Palatium without bloodshed. Now we are rulers here."

"You are," someone heckled.

Romulus pondered. Which was true? "No," he enunciated clearly and slowly, dragging out the thought. "We are the rulers here."

Someone near the front spoke up: a Brutus, but Romulus could not keep them all straight. "Yet, you rule from a soft bed with a soft woman underneath you. We live here," he swept his arm around in a circle indicating the extent of the village, "with not even captive women to use. How are we rulers? What do we rule? Ourselves?"

"It is true," he said slowly, "that not all of our plans have come to fruition as yet. It is true that you do not have the wealth, riches or women that you have been promised. But all these things will come. I have been busy, it is true, and yes, I have a woman to use. But I assure you she has been taking up very little of my time.

"I have been dealing with many difficult and trying issues. Being King is less fun than I had imagined. So many issues crop up on a daily basis. Issues of farming, and fields, rotas and crops ..." he paused theatrically. "It is, to say the least, overwhelming. Maybe I should have stuck to the life of a brigand. And I have nobody I can trust to help me with this work."

"You have us," yelled a third man. Romulus knew him: Gaius Vatius. Well, well, well. Was he on the verge of mutiny too, or was he a plant by Catalus to nudge the conversation in the right direction?

"You? You men are soldiers. I could not demean you by asking you to deal with daily trivia!"

"And yet you demean yourself."

Romulus pondered this, giving the men time to see that he was considering it in all its aspects.

"True, but that is what I must do. I am the King."

"We would help you in this as much as we would risk our lives fighting for this town or any other."

A ragged roar of assent went up from the assembled men.

Romulus was prepared to act surprised, but even so, the genuine surprise of the moment overcame him. "Really?" he gasped. "It would never have occurred ..."

The tension had drained out of the meeting. Almost at once, the body of men who had been disgruntled and mutinous were back on side. They were supporting him, helping him as they always had.

"Well, I suppose if that's what you want, I suppose ... how would this work?"

Before the sun had set, he had conscripted a hundred men to run things, discuss things, rule things and basically take all the hassle out of his life. And they had volunteered to do all of that in exchange for a few baubles: the right to be called "fathers of the country", to be collectively his "senate", and to appoint two praetors annually to oversee the kingdom.

Oh, and one big giveaway: they also had the right to sit in judgment upon him. Should they vote unanimously, they could strip him of his title of King and execute him.

Not going to happen, thought Romulus.
