7. Battle

Zeus was grateful for the opportunity to rest.

Since the messenger had arrived in the middle of the night, Zeus and his soldiers had had to gather their equipment together and then run the length of more than thirty furrows - mainly in the dark - in the hope of being here before the attack started, which Zeus had assumed would be at dawn. The sun was now two palms high in the sky but he had not seen any attackers. His scouts were out in the direction the messenger had indicated, but he had not heard any of their bird calls either.

Had the messenger been wrong?

Or worse, had the messenger been a deliberate plant, causing Zeus and his finest warriors to rush over to defend this village while an enemy - a cunning, clever enemy at that - circled around behind them and attacked their own village? If that was what had happened - and he ever found out who was responsible - heads would roll. Hopefully, if his own village were under attack, the villagers who he'd left behind would be able to resist them while a messenger came and found him here. They'd have to run all the way back in the heat of the day, of course, but hopefully they would be able to attack the enemy from behind.

He looked around, trying to make an enemy army appear out of the rocky landscape. Still nothing. Although it was very unlikely he would be able to see anything if none of his scouts had called out yet. But he knew the importance of being alert. If you didn't stay alert, there was a risk that you would fall asleep, particularly if you had been up all night as they had.

The downside of having other villages under his control, he reflected. When you said, "pay us tribute and we'll defend you" you did, in fact, have to turn up when they said "we are under attack". Even if, as in this instance, there appeared to be no attack in progress. Had the attackers decided better of it? Did they find out that a messenger had been sent to fetch him and run away in fear?

The time kept ticking, the sun climbed higher in the sky and the day became hot when an egret hooted mournfully. Zeus waited and then heard it again, slightly further to the west. He turned his gaze that way but still saw nothing. Not even an egret. Because, of course, there wasn't one out there. Not that far from water. One of his scouts had obviously spotted something. Already alert, Zeus tensed, reaching for his spear. Around him he could hear his two hands gathering themselves. As yet, they knew nothing of what was out there, but they believed that it was coming.

Zeus told himself to relax and settle in to wait.

The sun had risen another three fingers before Zeus finally spotted the dust that indicated the path of the advancing forces. Even further west than he had thought. Once the original signal had been given, the scouts were not supposed to give further signals unless "something happened". The enemy circling the village rather than coming straight in would not be enough for that strict rule to be overruled.

He watched as the dust plume moved to his right, still circling the village. Not a bad tactic, he presumed, trying to catch them off balance, but it really did require them to either do it more quickly with a little dust or do it at this speed with no dust. He had run all the way from his own village in the amount of time they had spent deploying this morning. Again, he asked himself if he was missing something.

Suddenly, the morning calm was broken as screaming and shrieking broke out from where the dust cloud was and three or four hands of men came charging at the village, following a route that went west and north of him. Did they know he was here? Was this all their force, or did they have more men in reserve? Or possibly about to attack somewhere else? All the possibilities and all the wrong choices flashed through his mind, along with his own dispositions. Apollo's two hands were much closer to these foreigners, but would be outnumbered. Should he go in support, or should he wait to see what developed? The other squads were scattered around the village: how would they react?

None of his other scouts had reported any sightings, but surely nobody would be so foolish as to attack a village with such small numbers. Decide, Zeus, decide! The whole outcome of this battle depends on what you decide now.

From his left he heard the repeated honking of a goose. Well, several geese in fact. Turning his attention that way, he saw another plume of dust coming towards him. Apollo would have to make do, or find one of the other squads to help him out. This group was coming almost straight at him, just slightly to his left. They would pass within an easy bowshot, maybe even a good spearcast. He watched a moment more and then turned to his men.

In total silence, he indicated to them what he wanted done: the bowmen were to wait at the rear, stand and start pumping their arrows into the enemy on his signal. They were not to leave the culvert where they were hiding until they had fired all their arrows. The others were to follow him out when he charged, bringing spears and swords to the party.

As the enemy came closer, he saw two of his scouts jump up and start chasing after them. So they had not spotted his scouts then. Unsurprising: they didn't seem to have any scouts or outrunners of their own. Now that they were closer, he could count them, after a fashion. He would need more than his own two hands to count all of them, but he wouldn't think there were more than four. So about two men for each one of his. But he had the element of surprise. Assuming they didn't see them down here before they drew close.

Zeus felt himself tensing again, but this time he knew that it was time. He was ready to give the bowmen their signal - a jerking of his spear - and then as the first couple of volleys struck home, to lead the charge amongst the confusion as they tried to turn, rally and understand the situation. Hopefully they could bring two or three of them down before they even knew they were fighting.

Another few heartbeats ... just a couple more ... yes! He jerked his spear forward, and felt the bowmen behind him jump up and start firing their arrows. As they launched the second volley, he heard the thunk as the first arrows hit. That was his signal: they knew something was up now, so it was time to charge.

Jumping up, he ran forward without even checking whether his men were obeying orders. He didn't need to: he knew they would be. They would follow him out of habit and instinct. He crossed the twenty or thirty strides in a few heartbeats, but felt that slowing of time that always came on him when the battles began. In slow motion he saw the men in front of him, sorting out the dangerous from the weak, saw who was paying attention and who was not. The man in front of him suddenly had an arrow in his leg - one of the arrows from the third volley had struck him. Momentarily distracted, he looked at his leg rather than Zeus: all the encouragement Zeus needed. He leant all of the weight of his charge into his spear and thrust it through the man's neck. Blood spurted everywhere and the man fell backwards, taking Zeus' spear with him.

Pulling his sword from his belt, he swivelled to the right, facing a man who was still running; hadn't understood yet that they were under attack. A weakling, thought Zeus, using the man's own momentum to drive him onto his sword. The man fell and died without even drawing his sword. Zeus stood on the body and pulled his sword back.

There were no more easy victims. The men still standing around them were aware they were under attack and defending themselves. Zeus felt he was still outnumbered, but the bowmen might be making up for that. As long as they didn't hit their own men, he reflected.

The next man he faced crossed swords with him. Left, right, step, twirl. This man was good, thought Zeus, embracing the mystery of the duel, the hypersensation that went with battle, the peripheral awareness that another man was striking at him from the side that enabled him to jump aside and defend himself from that sudden attack. He drove that man back, snapping his sword, before turning back to his original opponent. He laughed out loud at the joy of it all. If these turned out to be his last minutes on earth, at least he would enjoy them.

And then, suddenly it was over. The man opposite him threw down his sword, and fell to his knees, admitting defeat. Looking around, Zeus saw that the other men had too. Only a hand and a half remained fit, while Zeus had only lost one man. Surprise had won out over the numbers.

The fight might be over, but the battle was not. Leaving three men to deal with this fight, he took the others off to help Apollo.

As if Apollo needed help. By the time they were halfway there, they found Apollo and a hand of his men running towards them - to help them. Zeus stopped and bent double with laughter. This had been too easy. Was it all a feint for another attack? Zeus didn't think so. But even if it were, they had so thoroughly blunted this one that they could deter another attack just by threatening to kill the men they had captured.

Once they had cleared up the battlefield, they sat the men they had captured down on the ground. As honoured guests.

Apollo had sent to the village for one of their "domesticated" pigs. A victory like this required a celebration, and the only meaningful celebration was to make a meat offering to the Titans of Victory and to share what was left with the survivors. Only in this way could the victors truly display their greatness - by sharing the fruits of sacrifice with their defeated enemies.

The fire was soon crackling, and Apollo was given the task of killing and eviscerating the pig. The ritual required the execution to be done with one of the swords captured from the enemy. He examined the swords lying in the trophy pile and found one that pleased him. Picking it up and taking a few exploratory swings, he found the right balance point and approached the pig. Walking up to it, he put his arm around its neck and pulled it close. As he held it with his left arm, he used his right to plunge the sword into and through its neck. As he started dragging the blade back, the pig flinched and let out a squeal; a squeal that was quickly drowned in the gush of blood erupting from its throat and mouth. Its whole body jerked and shook as the life drained from the pig as its blood pumped out. Nevertheless, Apollo held it tight as it thrashed and did not let it escape or run around. Once it had settled, he turned it over and began dressing it for the fire. Applause broke out: it was the mark of a skilled warrior how well they could slaughter a pig or boar like this.

Once he had finished carving up the pig, Zeus came forward and picked up a cup he had filled with wine. He poured the wine into the flames, causing a whoosh as the alcohol ignited. "O Titans, we thank you for our victory. Know that our foes were brave and valiant."

Apollo approached the fire, still holding the bloody sword. Reaching forward, he placed it among the flames: "O Titans, in proof of their bravery, we give unto you their swords." Several among the soldiers came forward, took swords from the trophy and placed them in the fire. By doing so, they were giving away vast wealth - a bronze sword, even if damaged, was a valuable item: hours of effort would have gone into mining and preparing the bronze, let alone turning the raw bronze into a weapon suitable for combat. If nothing else, it would make sense to take this bronze back for Haepheastus to turn into new weapons.

But it made no sense to cheat the Titans of their spoils.

As the pig continued to roast, each man, led by Zeus, came forward to take portions of the entrails or blood and place it on the fire. No man might eat blood; the life-force of the animal belonged to the Goddess, and she only accepted blood through the intervention of the Titans. Even the defeated soldiers offered to the Titans, even though they might have had reason to feel the Titans had not helped them this day.

Although as they looked around, they knew the Titans had been there for them this day: many of their comrades had died this day, their blood spilt on the ground as the pig's had been by Apollo. Theirs might have been there too, draining into the dry soil tonight before their bodies where burned on the trophy at dawn. Instead, they would feast with their conquerors tonight, and then face the choice in the morning.

As they meat sizzled, ready to eat, Zeus and Apollo handed around cups of wine. "Let us drink to the Titans and to those who died today," said Zeus. Each of them drank a third of the cup of wine. "Let us drink to those who lived today," said Apollo. Each of them drank another third. "And let us also drink to those who could not be here today," said Zeus, remembering those of the army and village who could not participate. They drained their cups.

"And now, let us eat, drink and celebrate our life," shouted Zeus. "Let no man want for wine or meat!" And with that, the men by the fire starting hacking at the pig with their swords.

By dawn, all of the dead had been piled high on the trophy of swords.

From time to time during the night, a survivor of the attacking army had taken one of Zeus' men with him, found a comrade who had died in the assault, and asked help carrying it to the trophy, and dressing and arranging the body for his final journey - to meet, and hopefully to join, the Titans. Every warrior hoped to be worthy of that ultimate accolade when he died, and now, as they placed the bodies reverently on the trophy, they spoke the words that they believed would earn them that right - reciting their valiant acts and ending with the epitaph - the ultimate summary of their greatness.

With only three dead on their side, Zeus and Apollo carried all three. Not between them of course - that would not have done them honour - but with chosen members of valour from among the attacking army.

The living were now separated from the dead: the dead in a pile, the men in a line facing the horizon where the sun would soon rise. The fire had been fed during the night, but now had largely died down. Two long branches had been placed in the fire to start burning, and kindling had been placed in the trophy to make it easy to burn.

As the sun rose above the horizon, all the men watched, then bowed. Zeus walked to the fire and pulled out one of the long branches and placed in on the trophy, lighting the kindling. The leader of the attackers took the other branch and did the same thing.

As they stood between the flaming sun and the burning trophy, they stood in the "land between" - the land between the life of the sun and the death of the Titans. And now it was time for the living to choose.

"You know the ritual," said Zeus to the men left alive from the attacking army. "There are two doors to follow. You may go east, to the rising sun, and return to the lives that you led before. If you choose to do that, you must go, knowing that you were defeated in battle by men who were greater, and you will be diminished in the eyes of the Titans. But you know that you have life, and that those of you who have wives and children can be returned to them in the way that the dead cannot.

"Or you can choose death. By choosing the path of death, you say that you know that, having been beaten in battle by greater men, the path of honour is to join those greater men, and live what life you will have in their service, denying your former life because you died here. But you will be enhanced in the eyes of the Titans, as you will have sworn to forgo all other ties in the pursuit of greatness and heroism.

"This is not a choice I or anyone else can force on you: it is up to each man to decide whether he is a Hero or a Family Man. There is no disgrace in either choice. Here you will not have a family; there you will never be able to be sure if you should still be alive. Here you will have the opportunity to display your bravery and heroism; there you will be able to show your love and dedication.

"Now Choose!"

He stepped towards the rising sun, joining one of his soldiers in holding a spear at waist height. To choose life and return home, a man had to walk towards the sun and pass underneath the spear of life.

Apollo moved the other way, holding a branch of an olive tree shoulder high with another soldier. Any wishing to choose death and begin a new life in this village as a dependent warrior of the village, allowed to fight but denied women and family, had to walk towards the burning trophy of the men who died while they lived and accept that there lay their ultimate fate.

It was for each man to choose his own destiny. And, at that moment of choosing, every man knew whether he was a warrior or not: did he wish he had died with those others or, when the chips were down, would he rather live in a village without sword or spear, but with his wife and children?

As Zeus said, there were not right or wrong choices, per se: but each individual knew his own character.
