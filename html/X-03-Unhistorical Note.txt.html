54 Unhistorical Note

At this point in books like this, it is usual to add a "Historical Note" explaining how what I have writtern deviates from what I have read in the history books.

But what is history? I think at the very least, it depends on us having some kind of written record of the events of the time and at least the names of those who were involved.

For the first three sections of this book, we don't have that. Any of it. For the fourth, we have very, very late Latin sources (such as Livy), writing about a time so remote from them they can barely understand it. For comparison, consider writing a story set in Italy 500-700 years ago without reference to written sources (or, if you prefer, consider writing a story set in the Americas before the arrival of Columbus). Without a comprehensive understanding of the feudal system, it would be impossible to explain the actions of most of the players, or even what was happening at a large scale: how, where and when were wars fought; what happened during the reformation, or the renaissance?

We obviously don't know what records Livy had available to him, but they were unlikely to have been as rich as the information we have about the middle ages; more like what we know about the dark ages. So, is his account really history? Most historians don't think so, and don't accept it as such. Livy's accounts seem to project a great deal of the thoughts and morals and laws of the late constitutional period back onto the time of the Kings, to which they clearly do not apply.

And everything is just too neat. Romulus sets up the state and organises a fantastic army. Numa comes along and institutes religion. Tullius comes along and gives good governance. No, these things did not happen like that.

But how did they happen? Almost undoubtedly from two factors: evolution and conquest.

So let's go back to the beginning. I said we don't have any written records. I'm not actually sure that's true, but what is true is that we don't have any written records that we can read. We do have a lot of texts in Linear A, but nobody has been able to decipher them. It has been shown that Linear B is consistent with being an early form of the Greek language written in some other script - a script which is remarkably similar to Linear A.

We do know - or at least strongly believe - that the Greek language, and the Hellenic people, came to Greece from somewhere else; we call these people "Indo-European": not because that is where they came from, but because those are the places where they went and spread their language, culture, morals and values. By extension, they could not have been native to Greece. So a consistent story would be one in which a native population could speak a language distinct from Greek - and all other Indo-European languages - and write it down, and then be conquered by another population which spoke Greek but could not write. The merger of these two peoples could leave some group of them writing Greek in the existing (native) script. For the record, this is what I imagine happening at the end of Section 2, when Zeus overruns the empire of Cronos.

The other thing we have is a set of random stories much like the legends that Livy repurposed in describing the early history of Rome. And, as we read between the lines of those stories, a different narrative emerges from the one that they appear to tell.

In telling the story I have told in these pages, I was by no means starting from scratch. My personal starting point came in the early 1990s when I read Merlin Stone's "When God was a Woman" and Robert Graves' "The Greek Myths". Both told a story of how, back at the beginning of civilization, men and women worshipped not a God or Gods, but a Goddess. And did so for a spectacularly good reason - women had all the power, and all the utility - so surely the divine was just like that?

The key to this story is understanding that in primitive times - and primitive societies to this day - what seems to us the fundamentally obvious fact that children are produced by the sexual activities of women with men, was simply not recognised as such at all. People believed that seeds were carried through the wind, through rivers or through the consumption of seeds in a woman's diet. If men were not involved in the process, men did not have children. Women clearly did have children and thus had the ability to pass on wealth that they had accumulated in their lifetimes.

And when agriculture was discovered (or invented), probably by women, for the first time in human history it made sense to stay put, to stop being nomadic, and then suddenly there was wealth to inherit - fields, buildings, pottery, jewels. And so, for probably 5000 years (estimates vary wildly, and I am no expert), women across southern Europe and north Africa held titles to lands and goods that men shared in but, you cannot help but feel, as very much second-class citizens. In Section 1, I have given all my male characters names associated with pet dogs to make this point: men were useful, often loved, but were viewed in the same way that we today view our pets. I have no justification for this: I am using what Robert Graves describes as the analeptic method: I have immersed myself in the period, looked around and seen what must have been. Although I accept I could be wrong. I would love to know.

But things changed. At some point, somewhere, probably somewhere remote, men discovered that women who went for a long enough time without having sex with a man never became pregnant, no matter how many rivers they bathed in, windy fields they stood in or nuts they ate. And at some point, men determined the causal connection between sex and pregnancy. And then, somewhere, probably more than once, a man realised that if he was the only man to have sex with a given woman, then any children she had were by extension his children.

That probably happened somewhere far away from the Greek setting of my story. But Cronos in Section 1, and Zeus in Section 2 have acquired this knowledge and come from male-dominated, nomadic, pastoral tribes. Here, I feel I am on much more solid ground in treating of the traditions. Every major western tradition looks back and sees in its past its patriarchs, and sees the same people. Abraham, Isaac and Jacob in the Bible were all of this type: tribal leaders who understood the importance of only one man impregnating any single woman (although it was fine for him to impregnate any number of women) with hideous penalties for women who defied this rule.

But also in the Bible (and elsewhere), we pick up echoes of something else: a time when all was pleasant and happy before man dominated women (in the Garden of Eden; is the true "tree of knowledge" the knowledge that sexual intercourse is key to pregnancy?). And the stories of the Kingdoms of Israel and Judah redound with stories of "the Queen of Heaven" - Ashtoreth, Astarte - and her servants, the priestesses, all of whose crimes appear to be freedom-related: free to own wealth, free to worship in their own way, free to have sex with those they want to. There is no doubt that this is a story of a cover-up: the men have taken control, and want to airbrush the women out of history. Except that they always do it very badly, leaving evidence all over the place as to what they did. Reconstructing the actual truth is beyond us; identifying and stripping back the lies, and thus looking behind the curtain give us a glimpse into what the truth might have been. And then, as Robert Graves says, you use the analeptic method to reconstruct the history that might have been. It undoubtedly was not as I have written it, but I hope that I am not a thousand miles off.

In that case, what do I claim is "probably" true, or at least, "truth-adjacent"? In other words, stripping away the narrative, what do I claim are the facts that underlie the story of my first two sections? (Section 3 should be recognizable to those who have read Greek legends: the Iliad and the Odyssey, for example, and Section 4 is based entirely on looking behind what Livy wrote).

For me, the story starts with Dr JG Frazer's "The Golden Bough", which apparently caused a sensation when it was first published over a century ago, but, surprisingly, I had never heard of this bestseller before I started researching this topic. I suspect it became a bestseller by the simple fact of taking a lot of detailed scientific research and summarising it for a broader audience. Based on this, and giving it significant credit for her conclusions, Jane Ellen Harrison wrote "A Prologomena on the History of Greek Religion", which looks at the written evidence for Greek religion in the classical period and then, much like a patient furniture restorer, peels off layer after layer of misinterpretation, redirection and gloss in order to reveal the most likely origins of the various Greek festivals. Notably, she identifies four as being "for women only" and as having some very unusual rituals. Furthermore, much of the "technology" of these rituals seems stuck far back in time, being unaware of wine or the keeping of animals. Moreover, she correlates this with similar Roman festivals, such as the Bona Dea, which have similar characteristics.

This is backed up by archaeological evidence in the area. Maria Gimbutas spent much of her career finding and examining prehistoric evidence from caves and other locations across middle Europe. In doing so, she found a surprising level of consistency between artifacts at all the different sites she visited. And all of the artwork dwelt on symbols and imagery drawn from the cycle of life and from women as the givers of life. The contrast to the later European emp[hasis on the male-dominated pursuits of war and contests of strength could not be more clear.

Against that backdrop, and given the fundamental assumption of Robert Graves' analysis: that the Greek myths are there to account for the difference between the earlier iconography and ritual and the beliefs of the days in which the myths were told - probably long before they were written as we have them today - the explanations the he gives for the meanings of the myths, and the details of the societies they set out to obscure, become apparent and acceptable on face value.

Having said that, the myths cover many parts of Greece, and many different local rituals and interpretations, and across at least two seismic changes in power structures (I have condensed that in this book to represent two major changes between Sections 1 and 2 and between Sections 2 and 3). The myths are often repetitive, showing the same transition happening in different parts of Greece. At other times, they show similar transitions happening, but with different original customs leading to different rituals. For example, in my "Cronos" phase (Section 2), we see Kings being murdered by their "sons" - generally a reference to the man who became King after him - or we see them dying in chariot crashes, or we see them going mad and killing themselves.

At the same time, we often see the sons of the Queen leaving the place of their birth and going wandering. Quite simply, these are men who might see themselves as being "royal" by accident of birth (and to a lesser extent upbringing) but simply cannot have a place in a power structure in which men can only gain positions of power by marrying a Queen - initially their mother, later their sister or their niece. In other places - most notably Egypt - brothers and sisters frequently, if not always, married for the same reason - the title to the throne came through the female line, while the males of the family wanted to retain power by marrying into that female line. Only marriages that we - and the Greeks - would consider incestuous can achieve that goal.

In his novel King Jesus, Robert Graves alludes to the same principle in discussing both the succession of the Roman Kings and the reason for their ultimate demise. The right to rule Rome descended, he argued, from the Goddess Carmenta, considered incarnate in the female line of Queens of the Sabines. As I have indicated above, Livy's account of all this is garbled and, at the very least, fudged, to fit with his preconceived notions of what would naturally happen in a patriarchy. And, in consequence, he has his characters frequently acting with surprise at the events that unfold around them.

Taking a different initial stance, and trying to remove the varnish that Livy has put on his story, I came up with the sequence of events that unfolds in Section 4 - I won't repeat that here, except to say that it seems to me that the most likely story is that Rome was already a "city" (or at least a large town) when Romulus arrived with a small army from Alba Longa or somewhere else to the south. The members of that army were almost undoubtedly the men who formed the senate and, if it does not reflect just a normal story of annual kings, I suspect that Remus had been such an annual king whom Romulus murdered when conquering Rome. The so-called "Rape of the Sabines" was not so much a cunning plan as quite simply the outcome of a conquest - a story Livy cannot tell since he has Romulus build the town from scratch.

Originally, I wanted to focus in on the exact steps by which the women lost power and the men gained it. At each step, it seemed to me, the men must use their superior strengths - in particular, literal strength - in order to take power from women and wield it themselves. But I ran into two problems: one was the treacle-like speed with which these changes took place, and the other was I could not understand the nature of the compromises that seemed to be reached.

The closest parallel I could really see was with the Roman Empire and its frontier with the barbarian tribes in the fifth century. The barbarians were stronger, in the literal sense of having the numbers and the level of strength and violence to overcome the Roman soldiers, but they had been held at bay, literally for centuries, by the fact that the Romans had society, civilization, coordination and the ability to move their (lesser) numbers from one place to another as the tactical situation required it. But nevertheless, everywhere that the barbarians conquered, although a period of chaos inevitably followed, some version of proto-Roman civilzation - the rule of law, cities and towns, military organisation, ranks and titles, not to mention Christianity - eventually came to predominate. Why?

In this case, I believe it was that the structures were there and their roots went deep. The barbarians came, and they defeated the Roman armies, and sacked and pillaged the Roman cities, and broke up the Roman empire into smaller, disconnected territories. But at ground level, the citizens and civilians of Europe carried on much as they had for centuries past, in their villages and towns, growing crops, working in industries and paying their taxes - albeit to different overlords. Meanwhile the Church operated to reconnect disconnected territories, even sending missionaries to re-convert parts of the world that had returned to (a different) paganism after the barbarian invasions.

I have tried to work on the theory that the two waves of invaders - roughly corresponding to the Ionian and Doric conquests of Greece - have followed this same pattern. I have shown first Cronos, then Zeus, coming down across the mountains from the Eurasian Steppes, bringing with them knowledge (such as the role of men in producing children), technology (the raising of animals) and their own religion (in particular, the belief in Gods rather than Goddesses, but also a more linear rather than cyclic life). I have shown the point of impact with a given village, which had no previous experience of these beliefs. In reality, of course, any such wave of invaders would be both more general and more diffuse. It is also much more likely to have been encountered somewhere much further north (say Delphi), and assuredly not on the Peloponnese. If I were a historian, these things might bother me; as an author, they worry me not at all.

Both times, I have then skipped forward a couple of hundred years, allowing the impact of the "conquest" to settle down, the froth to disappear and see how much has remained unchanged. No matter the spin I may have put on this as an author, there is no question that the reality was something like this. A thousand years later, during classical times, classical authors reported festivals and rituals that were still practised only by women (see Harrison). Other rituals retained the characteristics that seem out of keeping with the Indo European traditions and thus, presumably, were part of the indigineous rituals which the conquerors were either unable or unwilling to eliminate. Given the ebb and flow of human history, and in particular, the history of dark age Europe I am using as an analogy, I can only assume that this process was incredibly detailed, sporadic and spread over decades if not centuries, and probably involved interaction between villages and tribes as wars continued over the time. The only real evidence that I have is drawn from the sources I have quoted: people looking back over time and accounting for why things are the way they are now through myths, and the modern science of archaeology which constrains how things could have been at various points in history.

Thus, in each case, although the previous section ended with the conquest of the female-run village by an external, patriarchal society, the patriarchal society does not win. In fact, as I read the history I am referencing, the patriarchy only wins at the very end of this book: with the death of the Goddess Carmenta. At that point, the leading men of Rome - the descendants of the violent gang of Romulans - are left in charge of the field and have no constraints on how they shackled and treated their womenfolk. The men would reign supreme in Europe for the next 2,500 years.
