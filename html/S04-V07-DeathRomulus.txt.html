45 Death of Romulus

Romulus called the Senate together.

"It has come to my attention that some among you are discontented with my rule. I would have this out with you now. I have indicated that I wish my son Titus to rule after me, and it seems that many of you are not content with this. I will have a division. All those who wish Titus to rule after me pass to my right, all those who do not, pass to my left."

"Not so fast, Romulus," countered Fabius. "We need to discuss this first. I, for one, do not feel that your son Titus has what it takes to rule this community. He is selfish and thinks only of himself. He does not care about the greater good of the community, and he does not care about our relationships with our neighbours. In short, I do not think Titus would be interested in serving anybody except himself. And, I for one, would find that intolerable and demand the right of combat to contest it."

"Furthermore," expounded Hostilius, "this is not the way in which we have ordered ourselves in the past. Yes, I know that the Greeks did it, but we are not in Greece. Even the Etruscans do not blindly follow father with son. We need to follow our own traditions, and the tradition that we follow is that after the death of the king, all those who would be king present themselves in the forum and fight to first blood. That is our way."

"Although," interjected Catalus, "it is not necessarily the best way. We are a group of one hundred men here. The best men, may I say, in Rome. Why can we not look through our ranks and choose the best man for the job?"

"Or indeed, outside our ranks," remarked Horatius. "I, for one, would much rather have King Titius than King Titus. There is a man who understands both men and the needs of a community. Why not ask him to move from Cures to Rome and become our King?"

"Because he's too old," mumbled Fabius under his breath. No point replacing one old geezer with another one more likely to pop his clogs first."

There was a smattering of laughter around Fabius, those that could hear him.

Lucius Forfatius rose to his feet. "I feel that it is my burden to remind the King that when he appointed us - or our fathers - to this body, he agreed to be bound by its decisions and, most important of all, he agreed to be bound by the unanimous verdict of this body that he should be deposed.

"I move that all those who feel that Romulus has outstayed his welcome pass to the right. If unanimous, I declare that Romulus be deposed immediately."

The house flew into uproar. Surely that was treason? But enough men there had been alive when Romulus had formed the Senate to nod sagely: yes, it was true. Others had heard it from their fathers. The hubbub grew louder as senators argued backwards and forwards.

"In conclusion, there is one point above all that I want to make, esteemed fathers," enunciated Gaius Julius Proculus carefully at the end of a long and meandering speech during which much of the house had simmered down, "which is that heaven knows that the only valid grounds for kingship is to be married to the familiar of Carmenta - who in this generation is the youngest daughter of Romulus, that is the fourth Romula.

"This young lady, as can be imagined, since she is still but eleven summers old, is still unmarried and indeed a virgin. But the genuine right to rule here can only be held by him who marries her and makes her fruitful. Given her current virgin state, it matters little to the discussion at hand - we can still find a man to our taste and marry him to her. But, of course, the man we find may not already have a wife."

Marriage in Rome was for life (or at least until death); divorce was at that time unheard of. Nor could any man marry more than one woman. In short, any man who wanted to be king had to be single. That reduced the field dramatically, and made it much more likely that a man from outside the community would be chosen.

But, more than that, if Proculus' view were to be accepted, it positively ruled the king's son out of the running. Among other things that were strictly forbidden by the Romans was an incestuous marriage - as one between the king's son and daughter could not help but be.

"No, no, no, a thousand times no," screamed Romulus. "You are not getting away with that, Gaius Julius. That is the old way, the way we have rejected. In Rome, men rule as men, not as mere adjuncts of women. My daughter has no special powers or privileges; those go to my son."

"I did not say your daughter, O King," responded Proculus, remaining calm under the weight of this diatribe. "I said the daughter of your wife. Specifically the youngest daughter of your wife. That is how the territory of Rome has always been ruled, and how you obtained your mandate to rule."

"I gained my right to rule by conquest," objected Romulus. "And I will defend my right to rule by the sword and the spear. Do you challenge me, Gaius Julius?"

"Not I," replied Proculus. "I am now too old and infirm. I will give you counsel, and I will point out the truth. But challenge you I cannot."

"But I will!" exclaimed Fabius, moving into the centre of the meeting room. "I challenge you to a duel to the death, for I will not lay down under this tyranny. It is my right to contest for the right to rule here, and I challenge you to deny that. Come down off that throne and fight me!" And he drew his sword.

Romulus stood, ponderously, and stepped down off the dais, drawing his sword as he did so. Two steps later, he swung hard at Fabius' head. Fabius snapped off a quick parry, and then a riposte. Romulus was experienced and quick, but he was old and no match for the vigour of the younger man. Within a few blows Romulus was down on the ground.

"Finish it!" he demanded. "It is the only way I will give in." And started to rise.

Fabius finished it.
