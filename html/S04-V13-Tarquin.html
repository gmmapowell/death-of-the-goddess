13. Tarquin

The relationship between Tarquin and the Senate rapidly entered into a vicious circle.

The politest interpretation that could be put on it was that Tarquin was a natural "military man". Although the grandson of a king, he had come up through the ranks until he had become a general. And now he was used to everybody following his orders. The Senate, filled with generals, couch generals and ambassadors, was not used to - or indeed, willing to - obeying orders. It wanted, if not control, at least to be consulted on all matters. And considered Tarquin's approach to be high-handed at best.

The general opinion of most of Rome expressed behind closed doors was that Tarquin was a spoiled brat. That he had been born grandson of a king, and a proud king at that, and had been brought up with the expectation that one day he would be king, and that all of that would one day fall into his lap as long as he did the minimum to marry the king's daughter. And yes, he had fought in the ranks and "worked his way up" to general, but that was just a sham to cover for the fact that he had been pre-ordained for it. He had not been born a peasant and won his way to general the way that others had - through sheer merit.

Senators, it is true, began to murmur about Tarquin. Some, in private, gave him the cognomen Superbus, meaning "the proud" or "the arrogant". But none seriously planned any action: what action, after all, could they actually take?

Meanwhile, Tarquin felt simultaneously threatened and empowered. Starting with some of the leading Senators, he trumped up charges against them, hauled them into court, dismissed the jury and acted as judge himself. Naturally, he found them all guilty, fined them their entire fortune and sentenced them to death.

As the years went on, he found more and more reasons to dispose of more and more Senators. This one was incompetent in a battle; this one had been a coward; this one was plotting treason. All were found guilty, executed and had their fortunes seized.

In total, Tarquin had five children who survived to maturity.

His only daughter, Tarquinia, he married to one of the leading men of Tusculum, Gaius Octavius Mamilius. His four sons he brought up to follow after him in war, knowing that the kingship he had given to Octavius. A man he was very careful to stay away from any time he was in Rome.

As part of their training for war, Tarquin sent his sons off to be legates or generals in the various theatres of war which he was pursuing at any given time. And they were generally succesful in these endeavours.

When he entered into a dispute with the Rutulians, he desired that his army should capture and sack the town of Ardea. He consequently sent his most senior general, Volesus, to take charge of this army and sent all of his sons to be legates, along with Gaius Octavius and his relation Collatinus Tarquinius. To entertain them as the butt of their humour, he also sent his nephew Lucius Junius, noble enough of birth (being his sister's son) but recognised everywhere as a fool - so much so that he was given the nickname "Brutus".

Upon arrival in front of Ardea, Volesus thought it would be easy enough to storm the city. Dividing up his army into the appropriate units and placing Sextus and TItus in charge of one part each, he ordered them to attack the walls. They did so at once, and battle was swiftly joined. But the walls were too high to be scaled, and too thick to be knocked down, and the gates were too solid and too buttressed to be broken through. In short, there was no way into the city. Both Sextus and Titus found themselves repelled from the walls and Volesus sounded the retreat.

"Right, men," he said when he had gathered his commanders in his tent when the battle was over. "We tried to do it the easy way, and that wasn't as easy as we'd hoped. So now we need to do it the hard way. We are going to starve them out. I want the whole city circumvallated in three days, or I'll decimate your legions. Don't bother waiting for the morning, or the men to be rested. Get half of them building three camps, one at each of the corners, and get the other half digging out the fortifications. Then they can take it in turns to carry on building the fortifications, rest and be on sentry duty. I'm not expecting anyone to come to the aid of Ardea, but we need to be ready to deal with them if they do. Any questions? No. Good."

The young men went off to do as they were told, and within just over two days, the fortifications were built, with the three camps providing secure accomodation for the men, and the walls having watchtowers from where they could see both into the town and for miles around. They were safe, well supplied from Rome, and the town of Ardea was cut off. They didn't know how much food they had with them in the town, but they knew there was a spring providing water.

Now, it was just a question of sitting back and waiting.

Waiting did not come naturally to Tarquins.

They were men of action. They sat around at night, drinking, and telling each other that "with just one more attack" they would have made it within the city. That they would have won the battle, had it not been for Volesus sounding the retreat to early.

They did not get along well with their co-legates, at least, whenever they saw them. Gaius Octavius was always keeping busy dealing with the myriad problems of command: from keeping his troops supplied, to keeping them occupied, to breaking up fights and establishing discipline. Meanwhile, Collatinus was frequently busy doing many of the same tasks, as well as leading raids on the city, trying to figure out where a weak spot might be. His heroism and discipline upset the Tarquin brothers, who felt that they should be doing something equally dramatic, but quietly Volesus had overlooked them - they just weren't up to the task.

So they sat around, and drank, and made fun of their cousin Lucius Junius. "Come here, Brutus," they would say, "and fill up my beaker of wine." "Brutus, go and empty the chamberpot." Not content with teasing him, they gave him all the most demeaning jobs - the jobs properly to be carried out by a slave.

One evening, when they were drinking, but were still sober, Octavius and Collatinus came into their command tent.

"Oh, oh, oh," exclaimed Arruns, "to what to we owe this unexpected pleasure?"

Snapping his fingers, Gaius addressed Lucius Junius. "Brutus, quick, get our guests beakers of wine. And be quick about it. None of your usual sluggishness."

"You really shouldn't treat him so," urged Collatinus. "He is not a slave; he is near as well born as you. He cannot help it if he is not altogether there."

"He may not be able to help it," retorted Titus, "but that doesn't mean we should give him ideas above his station. Would you make him King?"

"You know he cannot be King," chided Collatinus. "He is the daughter of the King's sister. It would be improper for him to marry any of the children of the Goddess."

"Unlike you, eh?" commented Sextus. "You are married to the King's cousin, are you not?"

"Indeed," replied Collatinus, "but she will not inherit the power, because Lucretia is not the youngest of the youngest. You know that as well as I do. That falls to your sister Tarquinia."

"Who is married to our esteemed legate Octavius. Who decided that you would be our next King?" asked Sextus sourly. "What fool decided that? Brutus?"

"I think you will find the fool in question was your father and hers, King Tarquin," replied Octavius levelly.

"Are you calling my father a fool?" challenged Gaius, misinterpreting the conversation.

"Not at all," responded Octavius. "I was just answering your brother's question as well as I could."

At that moment, Brutus came back with beakers of wine for the two men, and they sat down on the camp stools. And started to drink, albeit more slowly than the king's sons.

The messenger was breathless as he hurried in.

"Sirs, I have the gravest news."

Immediately, he had the attention of all seven men in the room, even, had the others noticed it, Lucius Junius.

Kneeling before Gaius Octavius, he murmured the formal words, "it pains me to express the passing of the lady Tarquinia".

Octavius looked at him blankly, in complete shock. "How... Why... When...?" he mumbled.

"Can this really be true?" asked Collatinus. "What happened, man? Speak."

"Yes, it is true," replied the messenger. "It is, as you know, the time for the fevers in Rome. The Lady Tarquinia had caught a fever, but laughed it off and said it was not worth distracting her husband from his duties. But she steadily grew worse until this morning, she died. Your father-in-law immediately summoned me and gave me this sad duty."

"Yes," murmured Octavius. "That sounds like her. Prioritizing our duty over her own comfort. And it is true that there is nothing I could have done. I assume she had the best doctors?"

"Yes," replied the messenger. "All of the best Greeks were called, but there was nothing they could do."
