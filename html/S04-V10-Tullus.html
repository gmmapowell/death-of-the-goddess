10. Tullus Hostilius

A few years later, Egeria turned 16, and became eligible for marriage.

Against his better judgment, Numa invited all the young noblemen from all the cities around about - together with those of his Senate - who wanted to compete for the chance to become his son-in-law and, by extension, his heir.

Of the twenty-two men who presented themselves, there could only be one winner: Tullus Hostilius, grandson of the legendary fighter Hositilius who had first supported, then opposed Romulus. He had inherited his father's seat in the Senate (who had, in turn, inherited it from his father) and had both supported and opposed Numa by turns - but always intelligently and with cogent reasons. Inasmuch as Numa would have preferred somebody else, he would have preferred a more peaceful man. Over the years, they had argued more than anything about the merits of war and peace. Numa could only hope that Tullus would calm down as he matured, and as he faced the actual choices and consequences of those choices. But Numa knew, too, that part of the problem was of his own making: having pursued a policy of peace for thirty years now, none of the young men of the city had ever experienced war at first hand - the occasional skirmish or conflict perhaps, but not the all out footslog of a major infantry engagement where your friends died in the front lines.

By the end of March, Tullus and Egeria were married, and Tullus became heir apparent to the throne. For better or worse. Now aged 30, he pushed for a more pro-active foreign policy, interpreting even trivial slights as a major affront to the dignity of Rome.

And Rome was now very dignified. Having been at peace for over 30 years, and having a stable family structure, the people had grown rich. Any number of temples and other public buildings had sprung up in and around the forum, and there were men to spare from farming for other trades, even as merchants. As the population grew, so did the number of houses and even the new insulae: a version of a house with two or three stories, allowing multiple individuals or families to share the same ground area. In fact, Rome had grown so rich that it could no longer feed itself: it had to import grain and olives from the neighbouring towns. A problem if it ever decided to go to war with those towns again.

"It is the Etruscans we need to be wary of, my son," intoned Numa to Tullus.

"But the Albans are attacking us on an almost daily basis," retorted Tullus. "It is an affront to the dignity of Rome."

"And our men are raiding their cattle too," sighed Numa wearily. "It comes from having too many cattle and too many men and not enough land. Both Alba and we are at peace and are prospering mightily. That is what is attracting the Etruscans to our neighborhood. They are old and weak; we are young and vigorous. We need to work together with the Albans to evict the Etruscans."

So the argument raged between the two: patient father, headstrong son. It went on for almost ten years - the ten years that Tullus was learning his trade from his father-in-law. It took place in private, at dinners, in the Senate, and in the public forum at Rome. And all the while Tullus led his small bands of raiders out to attack the Albans; cattle were carted off and farmers killed. All the time Tullus railed against the fact that he could not declare war on the Albans without his father-in-law's say-so.

Until Numa died.

This time there were no challengers.

Tullus had cemented his authority as the heir apparent and it only needed one interrex to have both the Senate and People vote through their approval. Tullus went into the Forum and addressed the People:

"Today I become King of Rome. While I understand what my father-in-law did, and why he dawdled and tried to negotiate with our enemies, I say that I will not put up with it a moment longer. These cattle raids must stop, and they must stop NOW! It is intolerable to the dignity of Rome that we should put up with it any longer.

"But, in light of the delicacy of relations between states, I will this day send two ambassadors to Alba Longa with our final demand: either they apologise and make full restitution for the damage they have caused to our farmers, or we will visit war on them such as they have never seen before.

"For, my fellow citizens, we are ready to fight. While we have been at peace for these last decades, we have still prepared for war. We have grouped our people into centuries, and they have drilled with spear, javelin, sword and shield. Our centuries are ready to fight and to storm Alba Longa, should they refuse us requital!

"Now, come here, and let us choose two messengers from among us, that they may be sent unto the King of Alba Longa."

Horatius and Fabius were shown into the court of King Cluilius.

"Sir, with all good deference and judgment, we require requital of the indemnity against which we have previously informed you forthwith. Our lord the King informs us than in the absence of an admission on your part as to the forthcoming nature of this indemnity, we will be left no choice but to declare war on Alba Longa, and to raze it to the ground and deport all its goods and populace."

Cluilius tried to keep his stunned reaction off his face. First off, the amount that they had demanded in compensation was out of all proportion to the damage done in a few cattle raids - even if they hadn't been mutual; he had sent his own delegates to the court of the new King Tullus to try and sort it out.

"My friends, surely you cannot be serious. I accept that there have been cattle raids; and I accept that there has been fault on both sides. In fact, I have sent a delegation to your new king to try and resolve these very issues."

"That can be of no concern to us," interjected Horatius. "We have very strict instructions from our King: either pay the indemnity, or feel the weight of Heaven fall on your heads. We have lost patience. You have thirty days."

Meanwhile, Alba's representatives, Curiatius and Fuffetius, were meeting with King Tullus.

"Please, my friends," Tullus implored, "you must be tired from your long journey. Take your ease, take a day or two to rest and refresh yourselves, and then let us meet again in a couple of days."

Taking Tullus at face value, and thinking that he wanted them to be in a good frame of mind to enter into discussions, they did as he bid, and took a couple of days to rest and recover their strength. And to indulge in the food and drink that he plied them with. So that, by the time they finally settled down to negotiate with the King, Horatius and Fabius had already returned with King Cluilius' refusal to pay the ridiculous indemnity that they had demanded.

"So," Tullus began, "how may I be of assistance to Alba."

"We have been instructed by the King to see if we can put a stop to these annoying cattle raids," replied Curiatius.

"Indeed," Tullus responded, "has your King had a re-think?"

"It was never our King's policy to support the cattle raids," replied Fuffetius. "They have been individual farmers, generally acting in retaliation against your farmers' aggression. The King would like to see an end to them and harmonious relations with Rome."

"That is not what my delegates told me," he said, snapping his fingers at Horatius. "Tell us what the King said, Horatius."

"He said that he refused to pay any indemnity and that if we wanted anything from him, we would need to take it by force."

Curiatius and Fuffetius looked at each other. Something, they knew, was wrong here, but they could not outright call the King of Rome and his accredited ambassador liars to their faces.

"Is that so, indeed?" temporized Curiatius. "That is not something of which I was aware. We were sent with instructions to see on what terms we could agree peace, and how we could enforce that peace. Is there no room for discussion on those points?"

"Apparently not," lamented King Tullus. "It would seem your own King has sold you out. We will see you on the battlefield, gentlemen."

The battle was arranged for three weeks later.

The heralds had agreed the location, and where the armies should stage, along with the date and time that the battle would start.

Horatius was commanding the Roman right wing, while Fabius had the left. Naturally, the king had taken the centre for himself.

Opposing them, Cluilius had the centre, and Curiatius the right, opposite Fabius. Fuffetius marshalled the left, opposing Horatius.

At the appointed time, the heralds blew their trumpets, and the two armies advanced on each other. The Romans were in a slightly disadvantageous position, marching uphill into the morning sun. But, as Tullus had observed, it can often be an advantage to appear to be at a disadvantage.

As they came closer, the Romans threw their javelins and charged, running the last twenty paces up the gentle slope into a force that was dodging missiles. Using their locked shields as a ram, they battered into the Albans and kept running. The Albans, unprepared for the violence of this assault took a step back and lost the battle then and there.

It took another hour of hard slog for the Romans to convert that initial impetus into a full-scale rout, but the discipline of the line held. Press forward; open shield; stab with the short gladius sword; close the shield; repeat. The continual pressure was what did for the Albans: they were more used to individual combat and swirling longswords; in this confined space, the longsword could neither stab through the gap in the shields, or strike a blow over the top.

It was Horatius on the right who finally crushed the resistance, pushing Fuffetius back and back, bending his line from where it met the centre until it broke. Horatius, fighting in the front line, was one of the first through the gap, from where he could start stabbing the Alban defenders from behind. As they turned to face him, more gaps opened up in the Alban lines, and more Roman soldiers poured through the gaps. Until, finally, the whole of the Alban lines crumbled and they turned and fled.

At which point, it was Fabius and his men on the left who had the most energy remaining. They charged after the fleeing Alban soldiers, killing them indiscriminately in their hundreds.

Finally, they reached the walls of Alba Longa, which kept its gates open to allow the fleeing soldiers back in.

And the pursuing Romans.

The sack of a city is never pleasant.

The sack of Alba Longa was particularly brutal, since it began with the end of the battlefield massacre. Naturally, men ran to their own houses, hoping to defend individually what they had failed to defend as a collective. It didn't work, of course, as the Roman army followed not as individuals but as groups of anywhere from a decury to a century. Against one or two men, the results were a foregone conclusion, as were the knock-on consequences for the women and children left defenceless.

And then there was the fire.

Inevitably, even had some Roman soldiers not used fire as a weapon of terror, fires would have spread from fire spilled from hearths. But many of the soldiers, frustrated by women who tried to resist rape, or tried to keep their children safe, picked up brands from the fires and deliberately set fire to the women or to the houses. Soon the whole settlement was burning and everybody - Roman and Alban alike; soldiers and civilians alike - was fleeing out of the gates into the hills around.

Tullus ordered such soldiers as he still had control of to round them all up and organize them for the march back to Rome.

At Rome, Tullus changed his attitude. Instead of consigning all of the captives to slavery, he honoured them as citizens of Rome. The men he enrolled according to their status and wealth at Alba, even though much of that wealth had (literally) gone up in smoke; he expected that they would make more.

And so came to Rome some of the names that would redound to glory in the years to come: Julius Caesar, Claudius, Marcellus.
