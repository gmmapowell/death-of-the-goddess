title=Harvest

Clematis swung the scythe from left to right.


She wasn't perfect at it, but it was a skill she had limited practice with.  Reaping, was, after all, men's work.  It wasn't at all intellectually demanding; it was just a repetitive task that required strength and coordination.  Most men at least had those skills.  But this looked like it would be a bumper harvest and that meant all hands to the scythe - not the senior women, of course; and those who were looking forward to giving birth to their own life too were excused. But while there was still this much standing barley, exposed in the fields and at risk of being destroyed by the occasional storm, or of being eaten by pests in the fields, everyone who could be spared was hard at work from before dawn until the sun was down.  Hard, hot work in the fields swinging a scythe, with very little rest.  The Goddess had ordained these weeks as weeks of work: once the harvest was in, and all the barley had been threshed, there would be time to give thanks, celebrate and relax.  But for now, the Goddess demanded work.


The villagers worked in a long line, from north to south, working their way across the fields in the natural way, from east to west.  The same way the Sun travelled across the sky.  The way the Moon traversed the night.  The way the stars spun around them.  Life moved from east to west, and thus the barley was harvested from east to west.


She dropped the latest bundle of stalks she had reaped behind her.  The older children - as she knew from her many seasons doing it herself - would collect the stalks that the reapers dropped and bundle them up, tying strings of flax around them.  Some of the older boys were then giving the task of carrying the bundles to the Megara where they would be stored until there were enough women free to do the threshing.  Threshing!  A mystery which she had seen performed for many years but which she would attempt for herself for the first time this year.  Now there was a skill that was beyond even the most intelligent of men.


As she swung her scythe again, she looked at the man to her left, Pip.  He was an expert at reaping, years of experience went into that smooth, effortless swing and the rhythm of collecting and dropping the stalks.  Every time, he grasped the stalks with his left hand, swung with his right - in perfect time with his whole body, the hips providing most of the power - and then in a single motion pulled on the stalks and dropped them before him, before taking a step forward.  In contrast, she shuffled to get in the right position, took an age to grasp some stalks and then swung unevenly near, if not at, their base.  She then had to turn to drop them behind her.  It was hardly surprising that she kept falling behind.  But when she did, one of the men to either side of her, Pip on the left or Dirk on the right, would lean across and do a few handfuls further ahead for her while she cleaned up the patch immediately in front.  And then she would scurry forward a few steps to find her proper position in the line.  It wasn't just her, of course, a number of the younger folk - spread out up and down the line - were in much the same position.  Hopefully they would all be better by the time the moon turned again.  There was certainly no lack of oppotunity to practise!


"New scythe here," somebody shouted to her left.  She, of course, just had a wooden scythe - sharpened as much as it could be given that wood hardly took an edge.  But the more experienced reapers had the few, valuable bronze scythes.  She didn't really know where they came from: somewhere to the south, she thought,  or maybe more to the east, there were villages which knew the mystery of making bronze, just as Chrysanthemum understood the mystery of turning river mud into pots, plates and other useful items.  A few women in the village knew enough to keep the blades sharp, and even to mend them when they broke, but making anything from bronze - let alone the making of bronze itself (what was it made from? also mud?) was a complete mystery to all the women in the village.


One of the children brought forward another bronze scythe to exchange, and hurried off back to the village huts to hand the blunt scythe to one of the women responsible for cleaning, sharpening and reforging bronze.  Such women were obviously excused the raw labour of harvesting the barley - it was much more important to ensure a steady supply of sharp bronze scythes.


She looked up at the sun: it had hardly moved since she had last looked.  Would this backbreaking work never end?


--


As they came down to the final areas of barley, the pattern changed: those working faster moved to the outside and started to encircle the remaining barley.  Everyone was more experienced now, and with that experience came an increase in tempo and rhythm, and the work started to be done faster.  Even so, some workers were still stronger, more experienced - faster - than others.  As time moved along, they spread out to the wings and started to work along the edges and around the far side.  As the slower reapers moved into the centre - where Clematis found herself, now without Pip covering for her - the line spread out and around the remaining field of barley and the reapers worked towards each other.


When the encirclement was complete, finishing the reaping started to become a competition - who could cut the most of the remaining barley?  And as the circle closed in on itself, those who could not compete - like Clematis - found themselves literally left behind, without a space in the circle to swing a scythe.  And inevitably, as time drew on, only the most expert reapers - who all had the bronze scythes to further aid their efficacy - were left in the reaping line.


But the others did not retreat back to the village yet. Another of the Goddess' gifts would soon reveal itself to the weaker reapers and the children who were increasingly relieved of their duties bundling and carrying barley to the safety of the Megara.


For now, though, they were able to stand back and have a rest; for most, the first real rest they had had during the daytime in the past two weeks.  And as they did so, they were able to appreciate the skill and effort being put into the reaping by those still left in the game.  And to choose favourites and to cheer them on.


"Pip! Pip! Come on, Pip!" yelled Clematis, appreciating his efforts - and skill - even more now from a distance than she had been while standing next to him.  In her eyes, he truly was among the best of them out there.  All around her, others were screaming for their champions as they battled it out to be the last few standing.  Inevitably, in a contest like this - of strength and dexterity - it was now mainly men still in the fray.


"There! There!" came a screech cutting through the partisan cheering.  A young woman, onlyslightly older than Clematis, was pointing low behind the line of reapers.  And it was immediately obvious what she was pointing at.  A small hare, obviously frightened, had jumped out of the ever shrinking field of barley and was desperately trying to escape.  The woman who had first pointed out the hare ran forward and dived, trying to catch it.  A desperate move at best, the hare just jinked left and dodged the lunge.  But for one hare, there were just too many villagers, equipped with scythes. The fourth blow struck the hare a solid blow to the head and felled it.  One of the children ran forward and quickly finished it with a flint knife.


No sooner had that excitement ebbed away than another screech went up as a vole came running through the line of reapers.  Again the villagers mobbed it, quickly dealing a killer blow.  As the reapers kept reaping, more and more animals emerged from the ever diminishing field into which they had been concentrated over the past two weeks.  Quickly the number of animals making a dash for it meant that some of them overwhelmed the villagers and escaped.  But by the end of the day, the Goddess had provided them with enough meat to feast on that night.


--


Clematis took her designated place in a circle on the floor of the Megara next to her mother.  While reaping was rough work best suited for men, they were redundant in this skilful labour.  Instead, they were delegated to the role of children, bringing sheaves of barley to the various groups around the floor.  Each woman had a threshing pot to catch the seeds and a threshing grate - a small wooden grill on which was placed a small amount of barley. The sheaves were placed in the middle of the circle, and, as she needed them each woman took some of the barley, broke it onto her grate and then started the threshing motion: a combination of tossing, shaking, grinding and rotating which - so Clematis had been told - would end up with all the grains in the pot and a pile of waste on the grate, which was dumped back on the floor to be swept up at the end of the day.  As with the reaping, there was a competitive aspect to this - the bigger the pile of waste at the end of the day, the more successful the circle.  And if nothing else, there were serious bragging rights for the year to come to be a member of the most successful threshing circle.


Clematis knew she had a lot to live up to - for the last two seasons, her mother and her friends had been the winners in the threshing competition.  By joining this circle this year, as a complete novice, was a responsibility she felt keenly.  And as much as her mother had explained how threshing was done, she knew that until she did it herself, she would never truly understand.  After all, if it were easy, they would let the men do it.


Her first attempt was a disaster.  As she tried to toss it up and catch it again, it ended up going everywhere - the floor, her mother, the sheaves of barley waiting to be threshed.  Her mother looked at her and laughed.


"Nobody said it was easy," she consoled.  "It's a very tricky skill, that combination of strength, dexterity and gentleness.  You need to be more gentle.  And use your wrists to _flick_, don't _throw_."


Clematis gathered some more raw barley and tried again.  She tried to be more gentle, but this time, the barley didn't even leave her grate.  "Flick, flick," her mother chided.  "It's not going to jump up by itself.  You have to give it some oomph, just not too much.  Watch!"  And she effortlessly did six complex motions with her hands that sent the raw barley up in the air, then separated the seeds from the husk, dropping the seeds into the pot; she repeated the same process six or seven more times until no more seeds fell into the pot.  She then tipped the rest of the husks out onto the floor.


Clematis tried again.  This time, the barley went up into the air and came back down onto the grate.  An improvement to be sure, but no seeds came loose.  She tried again.  And again.  Still no seeds feel through into the pot.


"Better, much better," her mother encouraged.  "That's the toss sorted.  You are getting the barley up and back down again.  But when it comes down, the grate needs to be moving from side to side to shell the seeds. You are just throwing it up and down.  You could do that with a plate.  And you'll notice you're not using a plate."

"Hyacinth is right," one of the other women in the circle added.  "It's not an easy skill, but you need to toss the barley up straight and then catch it on the grate while it's moving.  But not too much - or it will go everywhere."


She tried again, throwing the barley up in the air, and moving her grate to the side as it came back down.  This time she ended up with something in her pot - but mainly whole barley kernels that hadn't been threshed.


"It's a much finer motion than that, love," her mother's friend confided.  "If you jerk your hand like that the grate won't be anywhere near where the kernels come down.  You need a small, fast, late motion."


She tried again.  And failed again.  This was not easy.


--


Two days later, she had filled her first pot with barley seeds.  The other women in the circle had filled countless pots.  And from somewhere, more pots and more sheaves of barley kept appearing, a never-ending torment of more failure.  From behind her, a man appeared and whisked away her full pot, replacing it with a new, empty pot.


"The first pot you fill takes the longest," her mother told her.  "It will go quicker and quicker as you perfect your technique."


"Will I ever be as good as you?" she asked.  "You are all so accomplished."


"Oh yes," Hyacinth reassured her.  "I can remember saying exactly the same thing to _my_ mother.  She was so proficient at threshing, so elegant while doing it.  And almost not paying attention while she talked to her friends in a circle just like this one.  But eventually I got the hang of it."


Clematis crushed another handful of kernels onto her grate and tossed them up in the air.  Some came back down in the right place, some went here, some went there, and a little seed fell through into her jar.


"Watch the seeds, luvvie," said one of the other women.  "Don't watch your hands, and whatever you do, don't watch the grate.  You need to know where they are and what they are doing by instinct.  But the corn is doing its own thing.  You need to watch it up into the air and back down again.  And when it's about to land - flick!"  She demonstrated by doing the whole thing - including an eye roll - in exaggerated slow motion.  "If you want to perfect your technique quickly, what you need to do is perfect where you put your attention.  Your grandmother may have been able to do all of this while barely paying attention, but I can assure you the moment I stop watching what the kernels are doing is the moment they start doing their own thing."


--


Another week went by and it was almost the end of the month by the time the threshing was done.  The Lady in charge of accounts confirmed that it was, indeed, a bumper crop; possibly the biggest in living memory.  While Clematis had been a drag on her mother and her friends, they had nevertheless been competitive for the most corn threshed.  At the end of the day, the bragging rights had gone to another circle, but Clematis had learned a lot and was sure that next year their circle would be back on top.  Next year!  Next year, she would be ready.


But now it was time for the festival.  The Haloa - the Festival of the Harvest - had always been one of her favourites, even though as a child she had been excluded from most of the ritual.  Somehow, there seemed a freshness here at the beginning of the year; with all the hard work of the harvesting complete, and (this year at least) a supply of food guaranteed for the year ahead; with the sun shining down and promising more warmth and daylight as the year grew into its full splendour; what was not to like?


And this year, as one of the village's women, she would get to take part in the whole of the ritual.


On the morning of the last day of the month, she rose just before dawn as the watch sounded the morning trumpet.  There was, of course, no moon to guide the watch in tracking the passing of the night: they watched the stars and waited for the purpling gleams of the morning sunrise.  All the villagers walked to the east to greet the Sun in her majestic appearing, to express their gratitude for her part in their plenty.  As the Sun rose, Bougainvillaea spoke the ritual words of greeting and added their heartfelt thanks,

When the Sun was fully up the men were dismissed.  They were sent forth, away from the village and the cisterns; sent into the forests and wilderness around the village; they and the male children were to spend the day and the night in hunting, practising hunting and passing on the secrets of hunting.  Meanwhile the women  would offer the Goddess her rewards from the bounty she had provided.


Bougainvillaea led the women back to the Megara, where tables had been set up overnight.  Around each table gathered groups of women and girls.  Near the tables were some of the pots of barley, along with oil that had been kept from the previous harvest.  At each table, they collected some barley seeds and mixed it with the oil.  They pressed the mixture together to make a paste.  Once they had made the paste, they used a flat stone to press the paste into a cake.  They repeated this over and over until the cakes were firm and flat.


Cakes complete, the young girls were now also dismissed, leaving only the women behind for the most sacred part of the ritual.  Once again, the women collected barley seeds and mixed them with the oil.  Again, they crushed this together and pressed them not flat, but into shapes.  Into, specifically, two complementary shapes.  One set of women formed a phallic tower, while the others shaped a vulva.  Together, these were the representation of sex in this ceremony - as men were excluded, and the actual performance of the sexual act was prohibited until the Haloa was complete - this was how the sexual act, the act of sympathetic magic by which men helped induce the birth of children, was incorporated into this ritual.


The cakes and symbols were placed on platters out in the heat of the Sun.  For such an important ritual, no other preparation was allowed.  To heat them with fire would be to pollute them.  The cakes dried over the course of the afternoon as the women sat in the glare of the Sun, giving thanks for the harvest and praising the Goddess.


As the Sun reached four fingers from the end of her traversal, as She hung in the western sky, Bougainvillaea signalled, and the women gathered up their platters and set out into the West, following the setting Sun as she guided them to the land of the Goddess, the cisterns of the dead and the caverns of her representatives on earth.


As the reached the cisterns, the tip of the Sun touched the rim of the earth and Bougainvillaea lifted up her platter in respect.  The other women followed suit.

Starting with Bougainvillaea, each of the women in turn carried forward a platter and placed it on the edge of one of the cisterns.  As they did so, they bowed to the snakes they knew lived inside and would welcome the gift.  Then, in turn, each retreated to form a line behind those still waiting to offer their gifts, chanting and praisig the Goddess.  The Goddess who had given them so much bounty.  The Goddess to whom they now returned a small portion that was her due.


When all the gifts had been offered, the women bowed one last time in one long line, turned, and retreated to the village.


--


But the celebrations were not over yet.  Back in the Megara, Bougainvillaea called for champions.


"There will be three events.  The winner of each event will be honoured with position for the year to come, and the opportunity for rank in the future.  The events are: sprinting, wrestling and potting."


Quickly the contestants made themselves known.  From much practice, Clematis thought she stood a chance in the sprint; she knew she was too small, light and weak to be a contender in the wrestling bouts; and, though she had carried buckets since she was a child, had no illusions that she could even compete with her mother at the heavier weights used in this competition.


Quickly, the first heat of the sprints was organised.  Ten women raced across the floor and the winner was quickly determined.  Clematis was part of the third heat.  Already, she sensed that she was not going to be competitive - the runners in the first two heats were so quick!


She started quickly as Bougainvillaea called the mark, and held onto the lead for the first twenty paces or so across the floor, but a couple of places to her left, Jasmine came cruising past her as if she wasn't even trying.  By the end of the fifty paces of the course at the other side of the Megara, she was probably fourth or fifth.  Jasmine was the clear winner.


With all six heats complete, there were six winners who started the final.  On the mark, two of them moved off easily, but within thirty paces, Jasmine had tracked them down and won the overall race, and the laurel necklace, without difficulty.  Clematis felt better for seeing how easily even these experienced runners were beaten by the clearly excellent Jasmine.  Next Year, she thought to herself again, Next Year I will be better.  I will be older, stronger, faster.  Maybe I will even be as good as Jasmine.


The wrestling was organised into pairs.  Twenty or so pairs started the competition, and, as each pair was decided, the losers returned to sit with their friends and the winners paired off again until it came down to just two women who had beaten all their opponents.  Kittentail and Lavender eyed each other respectfully.  Both just over twenty seasons old, they were in their prime as wrestlers.  Both had had a number of children, but were not at the moment obviously pregnant.  Both had well developed physiques and strong arm and leg muscles.  In truth, they both recognised, there was little to choose between them and the issue would probably come down to luck.


Kittentail leapt forward, hoping to catch Lavender unaware, but Lavender stepped back with her right foot and blocked with her left.  Kittentail countered by grappling Lavender with her right hand across her buttocks and attempting to push her to the floor. Lavender resisted the thrust and used her right hand around Kittentail's neck and tried to tug her off balance, shifting her left leg forward and under Kittentail's crotch to dislodge her grip.  Kittentail gave way and fell back, but more out of deliberate intent than under duress: as Lavender followed she used her left arm to continue Lavender's momentum and push her down to the ground.


But Lavender had, in turn, prepared a feint: as she fell, she went down on one knee and, using the arm she still had around Kittentail's neck, pulled her over her outstretched thigh and into a braced position.  Thrusting upwards and over, she managed to turn Kittentail and push her down onto the floor with her own weight on top.


"To Lavender," announced Magnolia, who was one of the judges, and judging this bout.  "Congratulations to Lavender, best wrestler this season."


"Congratulations," echoed Kittentail, levering herself up from the ground.  "That was clever.  Until next year!"  And, smiling, she took the laurel from Bougainvillaea and placed it around Lavender's neck.


"On to the potting," said Bougainvillaea and Clematis received the biggest shock of the night as her own mother stepped forward as one of the contestants.  The potting competition was more of endurance than speed, although the question was of distance rather than time, so the winner was often the fastest as well as the one who lasted the longest.  In all, six women had come forward, and now took their positions on a track that was created around the outside of the Megara; the other women now moved to the centre which had been the scene for the first two events.


Clematis gasped as two women each carried one of the big pots used for the storage of grain, filled it with water and carefully lifted it into the air.  As Bougainvillaea called the mark, each of the contestants bent and ducked under her pot.  As she stood up, she transferred the enormous weight onto her head, neck and back.  Once steady, the two assistants released the pot and the woman started walking.


One lap, two laps, three ... Clematis could hardly imagine what it was like to have such a heavy pot on her head and could not imagine being in this competition.  Meanwhile, her mother seemed to be breezing around the three hundred paces of the Megara without a care in the world; as if she were just out for an afternoon stroll in the summertime.  To be fair, most of the other women seemed to be just as comfortable with the task.


Suddenly, one of the women wobbled and started to lose her balance. A gaggle of the watching women hurried forward and steadied the pot, letting it gently slip to the floor so that neither was the woman hurt or the pot damaged; amazingly, Clematis saw, hardly any of the water was spilled either.  But, of course, the woman was eliminated.  She had done ten laps, and was by no means last, but it wasn't about speed: it was about going the distance.


Twelve, fourteen, eighteen ... the laps kept on going and none of the other women looked like dropping out, until, suddenly, one of them took a misstep and another nearly crashed into her.  More women from the audience leapt to the task of rescuing women, pots and water; but in doing so, inhibited a third woman, who in turn had to drop out.


Now only Hyacinth and another woman Clematis did not really know were left in the event.  Another six laps went by and both women started to look as if they were starting to wear thin.  But they were carrying an enormous amount of water, and had already gone further than to the river and back!  Finally, the other woman stopped and called, "Congratulations, Hyacinth! You win again."


Quickly, women came to relieve both contestants of their pots and to congratulate them on their efforts.  When Hyacinth came back to join Clematis, she was wearing a laurel necklace.


"Mother, that was amazing.  I mean, I knew you had won things in the past, but ..."


Hyacinth just hugged her daughter.
