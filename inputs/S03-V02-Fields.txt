@Chapter
title=In the Fields


Zeus knew it was his job, but that didn't mean he had to like it.


What he wanted to be doing was drilling with his soldiers, preparing for the inevitable combats the year would bring.  Since becoming Zeus eight summers ago, the village had never been successfully attacked, but he had managed to lead several successful campaings against neighbouring villages.  In truth, he admitted to himself, he didn't really want to be _drilling_ with the soldiers, he wanted to be leading them in a raid.


There were now four nearby villages that were paying them an annual "tribute" to not attack them again.  In exchange for a stipulated amount of foodstuffs, pottery, cloth, wine and other goods, Zeus had agreed not to attack them and, indeed, to join them in defending themselves against - and exacting retribution on - any other village that should attack them.


Instead, he was here in the Megara, sorting out which work details - which groups of men - would be allocated to each of the women who wanted help in their fields.


This was, of course, the season of preparation.  When the harvest came, the headaches would be less - all the villagers would pull together, harvesting from one end of the village to the other until all the grain had been brought in and threshed.  But now, the decisions to be made were tricky.  Which was more important - making sure some bindweed in Xanthe's field was cleared before it spread, or watering all the grain on the high ground in Canippe's field that might die if it didn't rain in the next couple of days?  There were always more jobs to do than men available to do them, but most of them were not _really_ necessary.  If the bindweed didn't spread, then the small amount of it there today would do little harm.  Likewise, if it rained today or tomorrow - and his bones told him it would rain overnight - then all the backbreaking effort of lugging amphorae of water up from the river to water the fields would have been wasted.  Time that men could have spent drilling for the army.


On the other hand...


He consulted briefly with Hera and made his dispositions.  He ended with those most vexating women: "Xanthe, you can have Apollo's crew for the morning; when they have finished, they can go down and help Rosia with her raking and hoeing.  Canippe, I believe the Goddess will answer your request directly and send rain for us all tonight;  if I have understood her wrong, I will send you Herakles' crew tomorrow.  That's the kind of back-breaking work he was made for.  You never know, he might just choose to divert the river rather than shuttle those amphorae!"  He chuckled at his own joke: strong as he was, Herakles' reputation was mainly for laziness and a bad temper.


Which is what I will have if I don't get out of here soon, thought Zeus.


--


In the end, it was nearly noon by the time he was actually out in the fields, but even so, he was not free to go and join Ares and the soldiers in the drills.  Instead, he had to go on a round of the fields, checking on the work ethic of his various gangs, and handing out on-the-spot punishments as needed.


As he expected, Herakles was lazing off, sunning himself in Doris' field.  Doris, of course, was no match for the brute Herakles.  To be fair, Zeus himself was no match for the brute Herakles, but at least he could defend himself against the massive blows of an outraged Herakles.


He was supposed, as ordained by Zeus and Hera earlier in the month, to be building a stone wall between Doris' field and the adjoining one.  When the harvest had been brought in, Doris wanted to fold some goats on her land, and didn't want them straying.  Come to that, almost none of the women who owned the adjoining fields would want Doris' goats wandering onto their land, eating whatever second-season crops they had planted.


His crew were working, but an incredibly strong man, Herakles could lift heavier stones more frequently and more easily.  By slacking off, he was depriving the team of the work of two other men.


Zeus walked over to a pile of stones that had been collected in the morning, ready for assembling into a wall this afternoon.  Choosing the ideal stone, he hefted it and carried it over to where Herakles was lying, and let go.


Herakles awoke with a howl and sat up, casting the stone to one side.


"I'm sorry," said Zeus genially.  "I thought maybe you were just having a quick rest while you waited for someone to bring you your next stone."


Herakles blinked the sun out of his eyes and growled.  Impressively agile for such a bulky man, he jumped up, picked up the stone and tossed it effortlessly onto the wall being built.


"Happy now?" he challenged Zeus.


Zeus smiled as sweetly as he could manage: "Always happy to see the jobs I've allocated being done, thank you, Herakles.  I'll move on now, assured in the knowledge that you are giving Doris your all.  I certainly wouldn't want to hear otherwise tomorrow or I might have to find something worse for you to do."


--


Zeus dropped in on the craft sheds.


Many of the crafts - pottery and linen, for example - were entirely in the hands of women.  Zeus left it to Hera to supervise those.  He was, of course, most interested in Hephastos' work in the armaments shed, although it was always hot in here, even in the winter months.  Today, with Spring in the air and the Sun shining down, it was truly stifling.  At the end of the hut, a large stone furnace was continually stoked with wood and charcoal - whatever that was; Hephastos claimed it was something of his own invention that would burn even hotter than wood.


But the details were of no interest to Zeus.  It was what Hephastos made in here that interested him: weapons.  Everything from bronze swords and axes to bronze-tipped ash spears.  Zeus was a particular fan of the missile weapons: bows and arrows lovingly crafted from local woods and then again tipped with bronze points.  Zeus picked up a finished arrow.  Delicately made, the shaft was a thin sliver of ash, as thin as it could be made but still hold up under the tension of a fully drawn bow, with three flights of feathers at the tail arranged in a perfect triangle.  On the end was a delicate point of bronze, beautifully forged.  The business end of the missile, this was what did the damage, burying itself deep into a man's head, chest or limbs.  Ideally, it would penetrate deeply into the tissue, breaking apart blood vessels and doing untold damage to the muscles or organs beneath.  Arrows rarely killed a man immediately, but they might incapacitate him for the duration of the battle, or cause him to die slowly if his injuries were not treated sufficiently rapidly.


The problem, Zeus reflected, was always the sheer _number_ of them that you needed.  A soldier could go on campaign with just one sword; although two might be better since bronze was inherently brittle and swords could often snap.  A man with a spear would generally want two or three to throw at an enemy before closing to go toe to toe with him using a sword.  But a man with a bow?  He would get to the battle site, fire fifteen or twenty arrows while the armies closed and then say "I've run out of arrows".  And they were so complicated to make: just an ash shaft took a man almost a day to cut, shape and whittle.  The bronze heads Hephastos could cast at a rate of twenty or so a day; but the feathers for the flights took _forever_ to gather: they had to kill the birds, pluck the feathers, straighten them, sew them together laboriously, then attach them to the shaft and make sure everything was aligned perfectly.


And then some idiot would fire it during practice and make a mess of the flights, and back it would come to be repaired or replaced.  He really needed more men on the job of making the flights, but could he spare them from other jobs in the village?  He didn't see how.


Zeus put the arrow back down and picked up an unstrung bow.  It always amazed him how, unstrung, the bow bent the opposite way to how it did "in real life".  And how very tough it was to attach the string to the bow, and yet how very supple the bow was that it did not break.  Zeus had asked Hephastos to explain the magic of the wood to him many times, but he could not understand the spells.  Hephastos had learned them from his father, of course.  He had been shaping bows since he was a child in the craft sheds working alongside his father.


"That one's coming along," he said to Zeus now.  "It should be ready for you by the end of the quarter-month, I'd say.  It needs some more bending and some more lacquer.  It seems strong enough, but you never can tell until you come to use it.  And then, if it snaps, it snaps and all my work is gone to naught.  Still..."


"Good man," said Zeus.  "You are doing fantastic work here.  How many arrows can I expect to have by the time I take my men out on campaign?"


Hephastos sucked a breath in between his teeth and thought for a moment: "How many bowmen are you thinking of taking with you?"

"Probably twenty or so.  They'll have spears and swords as well, of course; no point taking them all that way and not being able to fight when they arrive."


"Twenty men, twenty bows.  So, let's see ..." Zeus could see the other man's brain whirring as he tried to visualise the number of arrows he would make.  "Let's say they can have twenty each.  Providing, of course, that they don't destroy too many in training."


Zeus nodded sagely; that was always an unknown.  But he liked the idea of twenty archers putting twenty arrows into the enemy as they approached.  He would have to talk to Apollo about that.


--


Ares was drilling the soldiers when he started climbing the hill.


"And slash," he shouted.  "That's it.  And you, defenders, be sure to get your swords fully up and take the weight on those wrists.  This is not like pruning bushes, you louts!  Push back on that weight or you'll find a real sword coming through your guard and into your body."


They went through the drill again, half the men swinging their wooden swords horizontally into the space between them and their opponents.  The other half of the men brought their swords up and in line, the flats turned perfectly to catch the edge of the attacking sword.  The swords came together with a loud "clack" that always sounded wrong to Zeus - it should be the lovely chime that came from bronze swords coming together.  But that was a small price to pay for not damaging the swords - or the soldiers - in training.


Zeus came to stand beside Ares.  "How are they doing, today?" he asked.  "It doesn't look very promising.  We want to be taking them out raiding soon, not having them practising on this hill all spring."


Ares didn't take his eyes off the drilling soldiers for a moment as he responded, "they are doing better today.  But we don't want to take them raiding before we're ready.  Apart from the men we might lose, think about how it would affect our standing if we were to challenge someone and then come away the losers."


Zeus shuddered in spite of himself.  That was not a pleasant thought.  Either home or away, he could not afford to develop a reputation as a leader who could not follow through on a threat.  Before long, he would find himself being challenged on a regular basis, and then the villages he had subjugated would rise up - before he knew it, he would no longer be King of Kings, but an also-ran.  A prospect that did not bear thinking about.


"That goes without saying.  But we need to bear in mind that we are also committed to defending our allies.  If we can't do that, then they'll defect anyway.  We had messages from Maia's village a few weeks back that she thought she might be attacked soon.  A good defensive action along with her would be a good start to the season, especially if we could then use that as a launching pad to retaliate and maybe add another village to our clients."


"Providing it's not during the harvest season," said Ares.


"Nobody would be that stupid," said Zeus. "If it's the harvest here, it will be there, too.  Everybody knows that the Goddess would look with disfavour on anyone who interfered with the harvest.  It would be a disaster for everybody."


"Unless they had their harvest in first," countered Ares.


Zeus gave him a lopsided grin.  The idea was madness.
