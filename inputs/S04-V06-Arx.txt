title=Arx of the Capitol


Rome now covered three hills, and had meeting points in the "forums" in the valleys between.


In addition to the Palatine and the Aventine, Romulus had fortified the Capitoline hill, distributing the men he had captured from his foreign wars over the three years that had elapsed since the kidnap of the Sabine women.  First Caenina, then Antemnae, then Crustumium had attacked Rome and fallen.  Finally, having seen three of his more powerful communities destroyed, Titius recognised that he had to act.


"There is no point in just charging in without a plan, however," he asserted to the gathered council.  "We have seen how that has worked out for Caenina and the rest.  Rome is a power to be reckoned with now, and we would be foolish to ignore its strength.


"They have built defences on all three of the hills that they have occupied; attacking any of those uphill is asking for trouble.  Instead, we need to take one of those and hold it against _them_."


"How do you propose to do _that_?" demanded Mettius Curtius, a longtime rival for the job of Sabine warleader.  "If we could occupy one of those hills, why not all three and call the job done?" 


"Because, my dear friend, we need help to get inside, and that help is not easily forthcoming.  Why do you think I have taken so long to act?  But I finally have the person I need on the inside."


"You do?  Who?"


"That would be telling, and I am not interested in revealing my sources at this time.  The chance of casual treachery is just too high.  Suffice it to say that I have a way into the Capitoline Citadel - the Arx as the Romans call it - for tomorrow night.  All that remains is to muster our forces and attack."


--


The night was almost pitch black, just a sliver of moon, as the soldiers crept up the north face of the Capitoline hill away from the river.


"We wait here," whispered Titius as they reached a spring a hundred paces or less from the fortified fence.  "Everybody keep absolutely quiet and still until our guide comes."


Time dragged on, marked only by the motion of the stars overhead in a largely cloudless sky, until they heard the chinking of a bucket chain coming down the hill.  Out of the darkness appeared a young girl.  She put her bucket down next to the spring and started to fill it.


"Well met, Tarpeia," said Titius, his voice above a whisper but pitched not to carry to the guards on the wall above.  "We have come as we promised."


The girl turned in shock.  "You!" she said.  "Where are my gifts?  You promised me an armring."


"Here," said Titius, pulling a silver ring from his arm and giving it to her.  "Now, quietly, without talking, pick up your bucket and walk back up the hill."


She did as he instructed and led the force of eight centuries up the path to the top of the hill.  When she arrived, she called out the password to the guard on the gate: "Jove Magnus Est".  Slowly, the gate creaked open and the girl was admitted.  Before the guards could close the gate, Titius drew his sword and, slamming his shield into the gate, barged through, stabbing the guard in the chest as he did so.  Curtius came through behind, shoving his shield at the first person to stand in his way, and snapping off a sword thrust as he did so.


Quickly, the men deployed and killed as many of the Romans as they could.  Before long, the noise had roused the Romans and they fled as quickly as possible.  By daybreak, Titius held undisputed control of the Capitoline Arx.  When they came to clean up the bodies, they found Tarpeia, still clutching the armring, crushed under a pile of bodies by the gate.  Titius calmly picked up the armring and put it back on his arm.


He would never have been able to trust her.


--


Romulus seethed.


Obviously he had been betrayed on the Capitol.  He didn't know how, but there was no other way that the Capitol could have fallen in the way that he was being told it had.  And, from what he was learning, the enemy had taken it with something like eight centuries of men and killed or evicted all of the inhabitants - not just his soldiers, but the womenfolk too.  Which meant that the prospects of convincing somebody inside the fort to betray it back to him were limited, to say the least.


He wanted to scream; to rant and rave; to wave his arms about; to hack somebody to death for this reverse.  But he knew none of those things would do any good.  No, it was time to be calm and think.  What could they do?


Eventually he asked the question out loud: "what can we do?"


"I don't think we have any choice," replied Catalus.  "Unless we want these interlopers in our midst for forever, we need to evict them.  And there's only one way we're going to do that: a frontal assault."

"Can that work?" demanded Romulus.  "Surely, with eight centuries in that fort they could hold it against two or three times the number we could possibly bring to bear."


"Yes, except..." countered Catalus.


"Except what?"


"Except that they will not be satisfied with taking one of the forts - and the least desirable at that.  They will want to take the other two as well."


"So we could just wait here for them; is that what you're saying?"


"Probably not.  If we don't move, they probably won't move.  They'll just sit there until we come out, and then they'll sally forth and attack us if they think they have the numbers.  Don't forget that we stored enough grain in there to last our garrison for a couple of years.  It will probably last them much the same, even if they can't smuggle more in.  Which we can't stop them doing without completely investing the place."


"So..."


"So, if we attack the Arx, then when they repel us, there's a good chance that they follow us out, attempting to finish the job as it were.  If we retreat back to the Palatine, we may even be able to get them to follow us up that hill.  And then ..."


"And then we can turn the tables on them and attack back down the hill," concluded Romulus.  "Yes, I can see how that could work.  What about the women?"


"Which women?"


"The ones who were evicted from the Arx last night."


"What about them?"


"Where are they now?"


"Safely inside the Aventine fort."


"Very good.  Keep them there.  And keep a century in there as well to guard it and keep them under watchful eye.  I know somebody betrayed the Arx, and I can't help feeling that it was a woman.  The same here on the Palatine.  Let's leave a century here and keep the women under observation."


"That doesn't leave us many men for a full frontal assault on the Arx.  Which, I don't need to remind you, was built specifically for defending against a full frontal assault."


"True enough, but if they have bribed a woman in one of the citadels to betray us, how can you be sure that they haven't bribed one in all three?"


--


The attack on the walls stalled, as they all knew it would.


Romulus shouted "retreat!" and the men fell away from the walls.


While they were regrouping, the gate opened and, with a loud bellow, Mettius Curtius led his century out of the Arx and charged down the hill into the middle of Romulus' army.  First to react was Hostius Hostilius, who led his century at the charge to meet Curtius.  Not a fair fight; the Sabines had the advantages of both the hill and the momentum.  Hostilius organised his men as quickly as he could, and built a shield wall three men deep with swords at the front and spears reaching over from the back; but it was all he could do to hold it together as Curtius muscled in from above.


Meanwhile, Romulus was able to stop his men from fleeing everywhere and gathered them together just on the Palatine side of the forum.  Invoking Jupiter Stator, he promised to dedicate a temple at that very spot if his men were victorious.


Hostilius was retreating steadilly, but holding his line firm.  All he needed to do was to slowly back down to the forum and then he could retreat through or around Romulus' line.


"Back step! Open shields! Thrust!" he called, over and over again.  With each motion, his line of men would take a step back, then move their left arm to one side, exposing themselves very briefly while thrusting out with their swords.  Meanwhile, the men in the back row were thrusting, thrusting, thrusting their seven foot spears over the heads of the front two ranks into their opponents faces.  The men in the middle row were mainly just shoving on the men in front of them, pressing them into the attacking Sabines.


"Aargh!"  Suddenly, Hostilius was down, bleeding from a spear thrust to his neck.  The man behind him quickly took his place in the front rank, driving hard with his spear.  Meanwhile two men in the back row reached down and picked up his body, quickly carrying it away to Romulus.


But the damage was done.  Not so much to the structure of the line as to the morale.  Hostilius had been the kingpin which had held the century together.  Without that, the structure fell apart.  First the spearmen turned and ran, then the second line, and finally the front rank turned tail and ran as quickly as they could for the safety of the forum and Romulus' men.


Not always quickly enough, though.  One of the problems with running away, rather than the steady falling back that Hostilius had been attempting is that you expose your back to the enemy's swords.  And in that heartbeat between stopping attacking and getting away, you are at great risk.  Twelve of the front rank were hacked down as they tried to retreat.


Curtius let out a bellow and a boast: "these men may be up to abducting Sabine women, but they cannot take on an army of Sabine men!  Let's get them boys!"  And he charged down the hill, losing the structure of his unit as he ran.


That might have been fine, had Romulus been waiting for him still on the downslope; or, had he not had time to discipline and organise his men.  But as Curtius ran headlong across the forum, he started to climb the Palatine hill.  Straight into the arms of Romulus' six centuries.  Romulus cast his javelin, hitting Mettius Curtius straight in the mouth.


"And charge!" commanded Romulus as the enemy commander fell to the ground.  "Keep a tight formation, though!"


The men charged forward as they had trained - oh, _so often_ - slamming into the Sabine shield wall in a perfect line, the second rank literally driving the first rank into the enemy, shields first; the third rank reaching over and stabbing, stabbing down with their spears.  The Sabines could not help but retreat, possibly even regroup.


From the corner of his eye, Romulus saw movement coming from the Palatine hill.  He didn't really have time to study it, but he couldn't ignore it either.  Some group of people were coming down the hill at a run, off to his right hand side.


"Prepare to defend right flank!  Reserves to the right!"


Suddenly the pressure eased on the line as the Sabines retreated.  Romulus took the opportunity to issue more commands:


"Hold fast!  Step back!  Wrap right flank!"


Having done that, he took the opportunity to look more closely at the party coming down the hill.


It was women.


"What is going on, here?" he demanded.


A group of women came up the small gap between the opposing forces.  Now it would be impossible to start fighting again without killing all these women - daughters on the one hand; wives on the other.


"Stop! Stop! Stop!" yelled the leader.  "What are you doing?  Yes, fathers, we understand that you have come to rescue us, but you have taken too long!  Had you come within days of our capture and rape, we would have welcomed the bloodshed and rescue.  But now we are beloved wives, parents of young children; we cannot return to you as we were without denying the parentage of our children.


"And you, husbands, we understand that you feel the need to protect what you have stolen from our parents, but consider this: they are still our parents, and we grew up with them.  Why should they die at your hands?


"Surely we can all be reasonable people here?"


Romulus could still feel the killing spirit coursing through his veins.  He wanted to lash out with his sword, kill these interlopers.  But slowly, so slowly, he came back to his senses, to find Catalus talking.


"You are quite right, we have no need to fight.  But we do need to have the Arx back.  We cannot countenance anyone - even your putative blood relatives - holding onto a key part of our defences."


"Surely we can do better than that," she responded.  "Can we not accept that some of them will want to come and live here?  Some of our mothers, who we actually know, could come too.  And some of us, those of us who want to, could go and live in the towns and cities of the Sabines?"


"Why would any man want to go and live in a place where he would be a second-class citizen?" asked Romulus.  "A place where he cannot own land or cattle?  Why would a man give up knowing who his children are, and just share in the collective sense of the population of the village?"


"I do not know," she responded.  "But I do know that I miss my mother dearly, and I would like to return to her.  I would be happy to forego my normal privileges of rank, and to take my husband and children with me.  I do not know if he would agree to go with me, but given that he is of no rank here, he would not lose anything really, would he?"


Romulus thought about this for a while, then looked back and Titius across from him.  Part of him still wanted to impale him on his sword, but the calmer part of him knew that many men would die here today unless ...


Catalus responded for him, "Yes, that would be acceptable to us if it is acceptable to Warleader Titius.  We would want control of the Arx back, but any who wanted to stay would be welcome.  And any families that wanted to leave and go to towns and villages of the Sabines would be welcome to do so. 


"But no woman may leave without her husband."
