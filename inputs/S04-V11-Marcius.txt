@Chapter
title=Ancus Marcius


Tullus was now old.  And vulnerable.


He had had a good run, all things considered, and had managed to grow the population of Rome considerably, mainly through conquest.


But his crushing and destruction of Alba remained a weakness and a point of resentment amongst the people.  Ten years after the destruction, he had been forced by Gaius Julius Caesar and Appius Claudius to institute a festival - the festival of the Alban Mount - to "propitiate the gods who had been desecrated through the destruction".  Caesar - a member of the descendants of the female line at Alba Longa, and thus one who considered himself worthy to be a king - arranged for himself to be appointed supervisory priest of the festival; a position he had held for the rest of his life.


And, as part of the festival, Tullus had to consent to being challenged for the right to his throne.  Only trueborn heirs of the queens of Alba Longa were entitled to challenge, and given that the female line had been stamped out in the destruction of Alba Longa, they were a dying breed.  On three previous occasions, Tullus had been challenged, and each time he had emerged the victor.  Three dead from combat; many others - such as Caesar - had died of old age, having lived a good life without feeling the need to challenge Tullus for the kingship of Rome.  His children, of course, did not "inherit" membership of the royal family of Alba - that could only be passed down directly from the mother; and there were no mothers left.


Which meant that now, some 45 years after the destruction of Alba, there were three men left who were capable of challenging Tullus.  Two of them - a Junius and a Claudius - were too old and infirm themselves to challenge Tullus; but Ancus Marcius, born in the year of the destruction of Alba, was 46 and in the prime of life.  This, he felt, was his time.  All he had to do was challenge Tullus; kill him in a duel; marry his youngest daughter, the very pretty Hostilia, currently a vestal virgin but that could be sorted out; and become the new King of Rome.


--


The herald blew his horn to announce the start of Day 3 of the Festival of the Alban Mount: challenge day.


"Do any here among you, claiming direct descent from the goddess of Alba Longa, wish to challenge our noble King Tullus Hostilius for the title of King of Rome?"


Silence greeted this enquiry.  Tullus, sitting on his throne, looked around him, then turned to the herald and nodded.  The herald, noticing this gesture, lifted his spear, ready to thump it down to close off the challenge window, when a voice spoke up, clearly, distinctly, but not shouting.


"I do."


Gasps came from many of the onlookers who had not been expecting a challenge.  Slowly Ancus Marcius walked out in front of the crowd and faced the king.


"I, Ancus Marcius, do hereby challenge you, Tullus Hostilius.  Firstly, for the simple opportunity to become king in your stead: a place to which I feel entitled by right of birth.  Secondly, to atone for your cruel murder of my mother and father in the destruction of Alba Longa, lo, these 45 years ago."


Nobody said anything; the silence stretched for many heartbeats.  Then Tullus stood and drew his sword - the usually ceremonial longsword; the sword of the challenge.  Marcius threw off his cloak and drew his sword - likewise a long sword, ill-equipped for the day to day fighting in the front line, but ideally suited to single combat.  And then took two steps forward and struck.


The iron swords came together with a belling clang.  Tullus was quick to disengage and then to strike back.  Marcius was ready with his parry.  Tullus was stronger than he had realized, but Marcius knew that he was now an old man and would not have the stamina of a man two-thirds of his age.  All he had to do was keep striking, parrying, turning, circling and wear the old man down.


When it came, it came suddenly.  Marcius raised his sword and swung down in a large overhead blow.  Tullus was late on the parry, and couldn't hold it.  Without even trying, the strength of Marcius' blow went on, down through the parry, and struck Tullus in the head - an almost fatal blow.  Immediately Tullus collapsed.  Marcius went in for the killing blow, but when he saw Tullus lying there - his body sprawled out, his sword off to one side, blood streaming from the wound to his head, he found he couldn't deliver it.  And turned away.


"Somebody minister unto the King," he yelled.


Quickly, a couple of senators rushed forward and began the process of binding him up.  They carried him off to his tent, and there the doctors tried their best to help him.  But to no avail.  In the dark reaches of the night, word came to Marcius that the King was dead.  He was still standing there, on the challenge ground, unable to believe that this was how it felt.  He had assumed he would feel elated - giddy, even.  He had killed many men before, so it wasn't strictly the killing that worried him; it was killing a god on earth.


"Your majesty," Cladius greeted him.


He looked up, unfamiliar with the honorific.


"Yes, the King is dead," Claudius confirmed.  "And I don't think we need an interrex to tell us who the next king is going to be."


"No," responded King Ancus Marcius.  "But we do need one until I can confirm my kingship by a marriage to Hostilia."


--


Ancus and Hostilia were happily married for twenty years.


They had four children: two boys and two girls.  Both of the girls were called Marcia, of course, but the younger one had the nickname Tanaquil (or "gift of God") because her birth had been difficult, but she (and her mother) had nevertheless survived.


She was a strange child.  Some described her as "not altogether there", while others saw in her the kind of prophetic abilities attributed to Kassandra of old: seeing the truth, but not being able to convince anyone else of it.  At age 12, when playing a game of "I Spy" with one of her governesses in the forum, she pointed to a man and said, "I see my husband".  Her governess was flummoxed by this remark, since obviously the girl was too young to be married and, moreover, as the hieress to the throne was not even engaged yet.


"What?" she mumbled.  But the girl was already away, striding up to the man and plucking a flower from her hair.


"Hello, sir," she greeted the man.  "I see you are not from around here, but I see that one day you will be King.  Come with me and let me introduce you to your predecessor."


Lucumo looked at her startled.  Not because he thought her mad, but because it seemed she had divined his innermost secret; for indeed, he had come to Rome with the express intent of winning some kind of power.  Maybe not as King, but at least as a general.  And here he was, barely an hour into his time here, and already this woman was offering to introduce him to the king.  Was this possible?  Was it destiny?


He opened his mouth to respond, when a woman intervened, gabbling an explanation in fast latin that he did not really understand.  He turned to her and asked who she was.


"I am Fabia, and this is Marcia, who we call Tanaquil, the daughter of the King of Rome, Ancus Marcius."


"So she can, in fact, take me to meet the King."


The governess looked at both of them, wondering what was happening here.  Then Tanaquil spoke again, "Of course I can, Lucumo.  I said I would, and I will."


"How ... how did you know my name?" asked Lucomo.


"Why would I not know everything about my future husband?" asked Tanaquil, laughing softly.  "Of course I know you are called Lucumo Tarquinius, and you are an adventurer recently arrived from Caere.  You will not return thither, but will stay here, marry me and have a daughter.  It is her son who will make the trip back to Caere that you cannot.  But not, obviously, until our granddaughter has died."


Lucumo stood with his mouth open.  This was more than he could process.  But how could he doubt her, when she knew his innermost thoughts?


"She says strange things at times," interjected the governess.  "We do not always know what she means by them.  But at other times, she can be quite rational."


"Things only seem strange when you have no context for them," responded Lucumo gently.  "The fact that we do not understand what she says merely means that we cannot see what she can see.


"Please, take me to meet your revered father.  I would greet him and offer him my services, such as they are."


--


"And you are?"


"I am Lucumo Tarquinius, sire, from the Etruscan city of Caere."


"Which explains your strong accent, no doubt."


"Sire, I am son of the King there, and was taught Latin from an early age.  I apologise if my accent offends you."


"It doesn't offend me, young man, I just notice it, that's all."


"And what are you doing here?"


"As I said, I am son of the King in Caere, which means, as I'm sure you are aware, that I have been trained to be a king, and a leader of men, but I am automatically disallowed from being the King in Caere, since that title goes to the husband of my youngest sister.  So I set out to try my luck somewhere else, where a man of wit may gain advantage."


"And you decided to take advantage of my daughter?"


"No, father," interjected Tanaquil, "that was down to me.  I saw Lucumo coming through the gate and recognised him as my future husband.  I went and greeted him with a kiss and a flower, as I am sure you would have expected the lady my mother to greet you on your return from travelling."


"And yet this man has not returned from travelling, but is simply arriving from another town - and another country!"


"He is my husband, for all that, as you will see once he has defeated the citizens of Politorium."


"Politorium, ha! That city has twice defied my attempts to reduce it.  And yet you say that this pup will manage that."


"Yes, father, he will do this deed in the coming campaigning season.  He must, otherwise you will never let him be my husband, you see."


Used as he was to his daughter's tangled logic, even this was too much for him.  But if he was destined to reduce Politorium, then he was indeed a gifted general.  A small price to pay.


"So be it! You have until the end of the year to bring me the gods of Poltorium.  If you succeed, you will marry my daughter as she says.  If you fail, you will suffer the fate of all fraudsters and cheats in this city and your head will be separated from your shoulders.


"Do I make myself plain?"


"Indeed, sire."


All too plain.  And he didn't even know where Politorium was.  Let alone how he was going to take it.


--


Not something that Tanaquil had any sympathy for.


"What could be easier?" she asked, ingenuously.  "You simply follow the path up the hill, wait by the spring, and then when they open the gate, you march in.


"I'd do it at night, if I were you, because otherwise they might see you coming.  And make sure to muffle all your weapons and armour, otherwise they might hear you."


"What are you talking about?" said Lucumo, wearily.  If she had "seen" this as clearly as she had seen their future together - down to the fate of their grandchildren - then she would be right.  It was just a question of getting the story out of her straight.


"The gate to the spring is barred and locked, obviously," she carried on, oblivious to his tone.  "But the servants come out to collect water day and night.  All you need to do is to walk up there in the dark, when they can't see you, making sure they can't hear you, and wait for them to unbar the gate and let some servants out to collect water.  Although you can't see the spring from down below, if you follow the watercourse up, you'll reach it.


"Nothing could be easier."


--


Politorium fell, Lucumo looted the city and brought Ancus Marcius the figures of the Gods.


In the New Year, he and Tanaquil married and Lucumo became heir apparent.  Six years later, Ancus Marcius died in what might, in another world, have been considered "suspicious" circumstances.
