@Chapter
title=Thargelia


Cronos and Rhea led the villagers out to the east to greet the dawn.  Rhea led the chorus of the priestesses in welcoming the Sun on this day:


@Poetry


We greet you, O Sun,
Visible face of Her we adore,
Giving to us all things
And asking us for nothing
As we receive, so we shall share
With those of your persons
With whom we share this life


Today is the day of collection
In which we gather our bounty
We share with all who live
And hold nothing back.
Today is the first day of the season
Today is the glimmering of life
For which we give your praise.
@/


The freshly made platter of offering was handed to Theano, who then followed Cronos and Rhea in the procession through the lands of the village.  For this festival, for reasons obscure to the villagers, no goats were offered to the Goddess.  But she was offered fruit from the trees, especially the figs that were starting to ripen, and others that were ready.  From the barley fields, they plucked and shelled ears of barley and added them to the platter.  From there, they went to collect beans, just starting to be ready for plucking, and then the procession moved west, collecting almonds from the trees there.  With all the fruits gathered, Rhea and Theano stopped to arrange the platter for the presentation.


Platter ready, Rhea bowed to her husband and dismissed him.


"Husband mine, know that in the presence of the Goddess, I will be safe and protected.  In her arms, I will be comforted.  No needs that I have will she not meet and, loth as I am to be separated from you, she has ordained that only women should come into her presence to make this offering."  So saying, she bowed to him again.


In return, he bowed to her and said the formal words of release.


"Wife mine, keeper of the village, I know that with the Goddess you will find all your needs met.  I cannot stray where she wishes me not to be, so, for the good of the village, I leave you here."


And, so saying, he bowed again and returned to the village.  In his wake, all the men followed him.


--


Rhea reached the first of the cisterns to the west of the village just as the Sun climbed to its zenith.


She placed the platter on the ground and, together with her chief priestesses, retreated a few paces and sat to observe how the Goddess would receive their offering.  The other women, who had remained in the procession, had stopped a furlong or so back from the cisterns: included, but not included.  And most importantly, not at risk should the Goddess express her extreme displeasure with the ultimate sanction: death.


Rhea sat, and waited for the Goddess' response.


--


The Sun was closing on the horizon when the first snake emerged, tentatively, from the cistern.


The wait had been long and nerve-wracking.  Particularly in light of the signs they had already received that month, for the Goddess to reject the offering of the Thargelia could devastate the harvest in the eyes of all of them.  Why would the Goddess do that?  Did she want to destroy them and, by her own implication, herself?  Was there a bigger game at play here that they simply could not understand?


The snake hissed aggressively.  And slowly, suspiciously, slithered over to the platter.  It raised itself up, and slowly sniffed the air.  Then it moved closer and sniffed the offering.  It moved around the platter, and sniffed in two or three more locations before suddenly becoming erect and clearly on guard.


Another snake came out of the cistern.  It too seemed on edge and slithered over to and then around the platter.  Whatever had alarmed the first seemed to have spooked the second also.


Suddenly, an eagle swooped out of the sky and landed directly on the platter, overturning it.  The eagle spread out its wings and batted them in the face of the first snake, which retreated, dazed.  The second - now to the bird's left, instantly snapped forward, attempting to strike the eagle.  But with surprising reflexes, the eagle hopped back, batting its wings again.  Both snakes now retreated back into the cistern, leaving the eagle in charge of the field.


--


"Why didn't you do something?" demanded Cronos, when Rhea told him about it.


"Do something?  Like what?" she countered.


Cronos was frustrated.  As a man, he was excluded from these rituals which involved communion with the Goddess, but even he could see what had happened here.


"Scare it.  Chase it off.  Kill it," he retorted.  "The Goddess tells you, _specifically_, that she is afraid that there is going to be an interloper who comes and attacks her and then, there she is, coming out for the offering you made her, and _you let her be attacked_.  She _specifically_ said that we needed to be ready to intervene on her behalf, both to protect the village and her herself.  _And you did nothing?_"


He stopped, exasperated.


He could feel their whole world, possibly their whole way of life, closing in on him and he didn't like it.  The defence of his village was his responsibility, and he had but the one year to do it.  When he had just been a soldier, striving for excellence, he had not realised what a responsibility it was to be Cronos.  He had seen men he admired, taking on the role for their year in turn and had seen the glory, the power, the authority.  But not the responsibility.


And now, through circumstances outside his control, he felt he had failed in that responsibility.
