"Lavi says so, that's who," retorted Ascanius angrily.


"Well, she doesn't know everything," answered Aeneas.  "If I say you will be King after me, you will be King after me, whatever Lavi and her husband want.  The days of women ruling because of birthright are gone, and people need to realize that."


"Well, tell that to one of your other sons," countered Ascanius, somewhat mollified, but still angry.  "Apart from anything else, I don't see *the point*.  I mean, yes, I could rule here, but I could easily go elsewhere, conquer a town, marry the queen, show them the true way of Jupiter Indiges and exercise power forever.  And, yes, if my son wants to wait and take over, he can.  But, I ask you, father, _why would I wait_?"


Aeneas had no answer to this brutal logic.  He was basically forced with a choice: he could kill himself to make way for his son, fight his son and have one of them kill the other, or let him go.


It seemed among his other talents Ascanius was a mind reader: "I could kill you father, or ask you to kill yourself, or otherwise die honourably in order that I can take over.  You have worked hard for what you have, you have struggled for more years than I can imagine.  You deserve to enjoy what you have.


"And I'm not really sure that I could cope with just being given all that on a plate.  Lavi's husband Cornus is a good man; he will wait, and he will rule after you, and the people will be happy with that.  It will be easy and undisputed and you can go to the pyre knowing that what you have built will be left behind you."


Aeneas raised his hands, accepting what he could not beat.  This son was too much like him: he was a soldier born and an adventurer.  He would find his place in the world, and that was not administering a few villages which were at peace.


"I see greatness in you, my son.  I know not what the future holds for you, but I know that you will do great and glorious things, worthy of your ancestry.  Go with my blessing, son, but know that as long as I live you have my support.  Now, how many men do you plan to take?"


"How many don't you need?  They may be mouths to feed, but give me enough and I won't have that much problem convincing people to feed us."


"Two squads, say 40?  It may not seem like many, but remember that when I first came here, I took this village _and_ held it against the Etruscans with less than a century."


Ascanius had hoped for more, but at least it wouldn't be hard to choose the 40: his father had shown his wisdom again, for they both knew he would take the 40 men who already looked on him as their personal commander.  And, in times of peace, the 40 men who would be least wanted here.  Men who _wanted_ to fight; not men who trained simply in order to defend their families.


Aeneas finished up the planning: "Go and spread the word.  You go next new moon.  And, Ascanius: no women go with you.  You can find them anywhere.  We need all the women we can lay our hands on here.  Any man that is so foolishly attached to a woman that he can't go without her can't go.  Tell them that.  It will soon straighten them out."


--


Inland, this country - Latinum - was hilly or even mountainous.


Ascanius had come inland before, scouting out what he could, and raiding for cattle and slaves from time to time.  He might not recognize it, but he had acute wanderlust and often went much further afield than was really necessary.  Maybe he hoped to replicate the travels of his father's youth, but if he did, he had no idea how far Carthage was from here, let alone Troy.  From time to time, he had considered taking up with some of the merchants who sailed up and down the coast of Latinum, but always the thought of war and glory in battle drew him back.  He had heard his father's stories often enough: of the fierce warriors of the Greek colonies; and of the People of the Sea with their swords made of a material stronger than bronze.  He had never seen one of these "iron" swords, but had heard tales, both from his father and from merchants who came through the area.  Out of Latinum, he was a minnow.


But how to grow strong?  He had three thoughts. The first was to move inland: further from the coast, it would be harder for roving bands of warriors to find him and subdue him; moreover, he would have more warning than he would on the coast.  Secondly, he understood war instinctively: he wanted high ground; a hill or a mountain top.  He could build a ditch, a wall, a fence: inside that, he would be secure.  Thirdly, he could watch and wait: sooner or later, a band of soldiers would turn up that he could ambush and discover what was going on in the outside world.


It was not a quick plan, and would probably not come to fruition in his lifetime.  But it would be _his_ plan, and he would pass it on to his sons, and to their sons.  And in three or four generations, when he was forgotten, a mighty empire would arise in these hills that would trace its origins to Aeneas and Ascanius.  To their powerful new idea: that the world belonged to _men prepared and strong enough to fight and kill for it_.


The men gathered around him in the pre-dawn light at the base of the hill.  A hill that Ascanius insisted on calling a "Mount".  Trying to make it sound more imposing.


The villagers here had not tried particularly hard to take advantage of its obvious defensive possibilities. There was no ditch or wall at the top.  As far as Ascanius could tell, short of killing or intimidating the fifty or so men on top of the hill, he was as good as King here.


He looked around at the men one last time.  "Ready?" he asked.  "Remember, every man we kill is one less man to work the fields and to recruit into our army.  If we can bluster and intimidate, let's do that.  But don't get sloppy.  I'd rather kill ten of them than lose one of us.  Let's go!"


He turned, picked up his two spears, one for throwing and one for jabbing, and led the way up the hill.  Forty men really wasn't enough to take a village, even one as unprotected as this - even with the advantage of surprise.  But he certainly wasn't going to wear his men out running up this hill like an amateur.  


--


"Hey! Who are you?"


Ascanius had made it a lot closer to the central plaza and huts than he had expected.  Even without a wall, a fence or a citadel, he had expected to be spotted and challenged as soon as he crossed the outer boundary of the village's lands.  As it happened, they had managed to take advantage of the cover provided by an olive grove to be within a bowshot of the huts.  Not that he had any bowmen, of course.  When you only had forty men to your name, you really couldn't afford the luxury of having men standing around at the back popping arrows in the air on the off-chance that one of them did some damage.


Now he looked at where the challenger stood, no more than twenty paces away.  He flicked his left wrist at Corridus.


"Kill him."


Yes, he had made a point of how he didn't want any _unnecessary_ killing, but he did want to intimidate, bluster, bully and generally convince these villagers that they were _all_ going to die.  And he didn't know any better way of doing that than to kill the first few who challenged him.  Their blood and their heads were significant currency when it came to trying to "persuade" the others they did not want to fight.


Corridus took five paces forward, then, spear in both hands, he thrust it up and into the man's throat, piercing it and killing him instantly.  As he twisted the spear out, another man came up with an axe and hacked his head off.


As quietly as they could, they carried on through the olive grove.


--


It really shouldn't be this easy, Ascanius told himself as he entered the plaza, unchallenged.


But it had been. Apart from the one sloppy guard whom they had killed, nobody had challenged them at all. And now they were here in the very heart of the village and, ahead, he could see the leading women of the village in a hut together. 


"Eyes sharp!" he called softly to the men around him. Realistically, if this was a trap, it wasn't a good one. Yes, they might all be trapped and all die, but they would take these women with them. 


"Well met, ladies," he opened in Latin as his men spread out around him. He had learnt his father's tongue, of course, but he had also grown up speaking his mother's language and knew that the villagers here in Alba would too. "It seems your menfolk have neglected to look after their most precious possessions." 


The Queen looked up at him disdainfully. "I am nobody's possession," she informed him primly and calmly. "I am the Queen and the right hand of the Goddess Venus." 


"Indeed," countered Ascanius, a smirk crossing his lips. "I have a spear here which claims otherwise. Would your goddess care to put in an appearance and dispute with me?" 


"You heretic! Disdain the goddess as you do, and you will not live long." 


"Longer than you will unless you submit to me. If you have any notion where your husband is, I will happily fight him for possession of you." 


"I have no husband, whelp," she retorted dismissively. "Here in Alba we keep to the old ways and I celebrate with the Goddess' chosen. No man is worthy to be called my equal." 


"Once again, we find ourselves at odds, my dear. But then if you don't even have a husband, you can marry me without restraint. And nobody even needs to die. Excellent." 


"I am not marrying you or anyone else." 


"A shame," he countered. "We were so close to getting out of here without anyone dying. Never mind, I'll marry your daughter instead." 


"Over my dead body!" 


"Sadly prophetic," he lamented, and, lashing out with his spear, killed her instantly. "Now," he continued, as if nothing had happened, "would one of you good ladies go and find the queen and prepare her for marriage? Corridus will come with you in case the young lady needs persuading. 


"Of course, if she cannot be persuaded, Corridus will no doubt solve the problem and line up another volunteer, if you take my meaning.  Meanwhile, my spear can become impatient and may end up accidentally killing one or two more of these ladies while I'm waiting." 


Corridus left with a squad of guards and two of the women. 


--


Ascanius was married by nightfall. Not something his young bride had ever expected, given that marriage was unheard of here. 


Not a bad trade, Ascanius considered. The old queen had been, how to put it, _old_. Whereas this queen was young and delectable, the youngest of the old queen's daughters. 


The vanquished men had had no objections to any of this. If anything, they felt their standing had gone up in the world. They were no longer mere men in a village entirely run by women. They were second class men in a village run by men. 


Men who took no nonsense from women.
