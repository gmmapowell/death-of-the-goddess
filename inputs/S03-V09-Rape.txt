Iphigenia was tending to her fields.


The harvest had all come in, the subble had all been burnt, and now it was the time of year to prepare the fields for planting.  Since her marriage, Doris had granted her two of her lesser fields, and she looked after them as her own.  She had ceased having her monthly moons three moons ago, and now was sure that along  with her crops in the spring, she would be harvesting a baby.


And that made her think of Nestor.


The nights that they spent together, her in his arms as he first held her, then massaged her, then aroused her, then entered her.  She felt herself going weak at the thought of it as she tried to focus on the task at hand: hoeing through the soil, breaking up what roots remained from the summer crop and turn all the goodness over.  The ritual of planting was one whose mantras she had learnt as a child, and now she applied to grow her own crops on her own land.


Each field attached to the village had to be turned over; Zeus and Hera allocated workers from the general pool to assist those who, like Doris and Iphigenia, owned the land in the village.  Men, of course, could not own land although married men often worked their wives' land, and then there were many women who owned nothing.  Athene - along with her priestesses - were still the largest owners of land, and they all worked exclusively on their own land.  All in all, it was a mess, Iphi thought.  Surely there was an easier way of organizing everything.  No wonder Zeus always seemed irritated with the responsibility.


Today, Iphigenia had been assigned Achilles' crew of eight men, and of course her husband was here as well.  The men, being men, were lazy.  Iphi knew the importance of breaking up the soil and knew that the prosperity of her field depended as much on how well they did their work here as it did on anything else.  But the men?  They were here today, and then off doing something else tomorrow.  All they were really interested in was fighting and the festivals where they could have sex with the priestesses.


She tugged hard on the hoe, pulling at something deep under the soil.  Eventually it came loose, and a stone came to the surface.  She picked it up with her left hand and tossed it onto a pile off to her left.  It seemed that the ground grew stones in the same way that the baby was growing in her belly.  But it made no sense - stones were not alive.


She put her hoe back in the ground and pulled, hard.


--


They ate the midday meal under the shade of an olive tree.


They had the usual midday fare: some olives and grapes, a few corncakes and some nuts.  They had a little wine mixed with water to drink.  This was not a banquet or a feast; it was just enough to give them some nourishment while they rested during the heat of the day.


"And it's just as well it's not a banquet, right, Hector?" taunted Achilles.  "I mean, if it were a feast, we'd probably have games afterwards, and I'd thrash your skinny hide.  Again."


Hector looked dismissively at him.  He was here to work and prepare his wife's fields for the next planting.  It was no real loss to Achilles if they spent all afternoon fighting or messing around - it wouldn't be his wife and child that would lose out.  Better to avoid the subject and focus on the tasks in hand.


"It is pleasant to rest a while," he replied diplomatically.  "But we will need all our strength to finish this field this afternoon."


"You might," replied Achilles. "But I could thrash you, finish this field and then service your wife with energy left to spare."


An insult like that was hard to bear, but Achilles could be like that.  Hector decided he only had two options: he could challenge Achilles or he could move on.  He felt that while he had lost to Achilles in the sword fight before, he could easily best him in a fair fight - particularly if there was a grudge attached to it.  But Achilles had the backing of his gang here, and that meant a fair fight was unlucky.  Better to go back to work and deal with Achilles another way - perhaps during training.


He stood up, picked up his hoe and went back to work.  The others, grudgingly, followed.


Iphigenia was relieved.  She might need Achilles' gang to prepare her field, but she didn't like - or trust - him at all.


--


"You don't seem to be working today, Hector," said Achilles.


Hector turned to face him.  He was going with Iphi to help her mother with the final preparations before planting began with the new moon tomorrow night.  It might seem to Achilles that that was not working, but he still had most of a day's work ahead of him.


"I'm going to Doris' fields to prepare them," he responded as politely as he could manage.  "You're welcome to come and help if you aren't on a work detail."


"I have more important things to do," Achilles replied.  A pause hung in the air, expectant.  "Like settling a score with a coward."


"I am not a coward, Achilles, and we have no cause to fight."


"Well, in that case, let me have a go with your wife, Hector."


Hector was shocked.  Even for Achilles, such brashness and coarseness was unexpected.  To belittle any woman like that was abomination, but to say so _to her husband_?


"Are you drunk, Achilles?"


"No, I don't need to be drunk to face you, Hector.  Fight me or be known as craven.  I don't even have my friends with me today, if that's what you're scared of.  Just me and you: fight for your wife.  Or don't.  But I intend to have her before this day is through."


That was too much for Hector.  In spite of his best intentions, he threw a punch at Achilles.  In short order, they were wrestling on the ground.  Both men were big and strong, although dwarfed by giants like Hercules.


"Stop! Stop! Stop!" screamed Iphi.  What had got into these men?  Did Hector not know there was work to be done?  Surely they could practise being soldiers when they were on duty?


Achilles picked up Hector's head and bumped it to the ground.  It smacked into the flat of a stone and Hector passed out and lay there, motionless.


"You've killed him!  You've killed him!" shrieked Iphi.


"No, he's still breathing," said Achilles.  "But you are still mine.  Come here."  And he reached out with his left hand and pulled her towards him, standing up at the same time.  Unsurprisingly, her dress tore and came away in his hand, leaving her standing there, naked.  "Very nice," said Achilles, taking hold of her again.


--


Doris and Hercules came running when they heard the screams.


Hercules took in the scene with a single glance.  Knowing the man as he did, he quickly - and correctly - figured out what must have transpired here.  With a single hand, he lifted Achilles off Iphigenia and held him upright as other men might hold a small animal.


"What were you thinking?" he bellowed, using his free hand to slap Achilles around the face.  "Last time I checked, Iphigenia was not your wife.  I think," he said, peering around ostentatiously, "that if anyone should have his dick inside her at this time of day, it would be that man there, not you.  And then I think she might be screaming in pleasure, not fear, don't _you_, Achilles?"


Saying that, he let go.  Cat-quick, Achilles balanced himself on his feet and came up in a fighter's crouch.


Hercules laughed.  "Oh, come off it, Achilles.  Do you want to die?  Are you seriously challenging me?"


"No, he's not," said a new voice.  "He is challenging the Goddess.  And the Goddess says that she will judge his case.  Take him to the Megara.  Zeus will join you there."


"Yes, priestess," acknowledged Hercules.  "Let the will of the Goddess be done."


--


Zeus could not believe his ears.


Like Hercules, he knew that Achilles could be erratic.  That he liked fighting far too much and that he lacked discipline.  More than once he had had to talk to him about it.  And he knew that sooner or later, Achilles would challenge him for the title of Zeus.  Well, assuming he lived that long.


But now he had insulted the Goddess.


To touch any woman - let alone violate her - without her consent was to trample on the prerogratives of the Goddess.  She was jealous of her rights, and no man could be considered the equal of any woman.  And while Iphigenia was not yet at the rank of _mother_, she was a landowner and Hera had said that she had confirmed that she had proved fertile.


On top of that, the rules of the village said that once a woman was married, her husband was her _exclusive_ sexual partner.  Even if she _had_ consented, what had happened today was an offence against marriage.  And Zeus and Hera were there to enforce those rules.


Any way you looked at it, Achilles had violated the laws of the Goddess and the rules of the village.  There was only one possible atonement: _blood_.


The only question Zeus had not yet resolved was _how_ Achilles blood should be spilt.  Should he acknowledge the rights of the Goddess and offer him the escape route of trial by combat, allowing the Goddess herself to judge who was the stronger?  If Achilles won, he could go free, assuming he never came back to the village.


Or should he stand on his own prerogatives as leader of this community, and punish Achilles as a lawbreaker and a renegade soldier?  If he chose that path, it was much simpler: he just ordered the man to die and a simple sword thrust through his heart would finish it.  Nobody would question his judgment, either way.


Achilles walked into the Megara with his head up high, escorted by four other men, but not restrained or dragged.  Achilles knew the punishment for his actions, and was not cowed.  His fate was written in his face: it would not do to simply execute him.  The villagers - and particularly the men - needed to _see_ the power of the Goddess.  They needed to see that _right_ was right, not might.  Just because Achilles - or Hector, or anyone else, could physically manhandle another man or woman did not make him supreme in this village.  The Goddess would always be supreme, and her will would be demonstrated through her Queen, her King and her Priestess.


Zeus looked him squarely in the eye.  Achilles looked back, full of contempt.  For a moment Zeus was tempted to just order his death, see him hacked to pieces in front of him: but that would be what Achilles would do.  Zeus had the maturity and wisdom to know that the long-term consequences outweighed the short-term satisfaction.


"I will not bother inquiring into the facts or ask for your version of events or explanations, Achilles," Zeus began.  "You were caught red-handed in the commission of an act which offends both the Goddess and the Village.  You must be punished and die."


There was silence in the Megara.  Achilles looked at him without fear; or indeed any reaction.  There could be no question that he had been expecting this, but even so, Zeus was impressed at his calm.  _If only you had learnt some discipline_, he thought.  _Where did I go wrong in your training?_


"The Goddess has decreed that you shall die by combat.  As her champion on earth, I will exercise her will.  If you should best me and survive, you may go free and leave the village, but to return or ever to interact with any villagers again will cost you your life.  Do you understand?"


Achilles inclined his head, then reached out a hand to signal that he was ready for Zeus to approach.  Zeus stood and climbed down from the dais onto the floor of the Megara.  As he neared Achilles, he reached out and swung his right hand at him.  Reflexively, Achilles raised his arm to block, and Zeus kicked him in the kneecap.  Where most men would fall, Achilles stayed upright and stepped back, trying to pull Zeus with him.  Zeus went with the pull - and his momentum - and pushed into Achilles, knocking him backwards onto the floor.


As they fell, Zeus raised his fist and, with one mighty blow, smashed his opponent's face.


Probably that would have been enough, but this man was a rapist, and Zeus had a point to prove.  Kneeling, he pummelled the man's face ferociously until it was unrecognizable.  Then he stood and walked away from the lifeless body.  Turning to Athene, he intoned, "what is left of this creature is in the province of the Goddess in her role as Death".


Athene stepped forward, the ritual bone sickle in her hand.  Removing Achilles' loincloth, she removed the offending member underneath and held it up for all to see.  "As you have all seen, the Goddess will not tolerate this behaviour.  This man who was a man is a man no more.  Not only is he dead, but he is less than a man."  Turning to one of her acolytes, she passed the offending member.


"Dispose of this garbage," ordered Zeus.


The men knew what to do.  Criminals were fed to the Goddess' snakes out in the cisterns.  They would take him there and throw him in.  The Goddess would clean up after him and then it would be as if he never existed.
