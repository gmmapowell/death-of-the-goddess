@Chapter
title=Haloa


At the time of harvest, it was all hands to the scythe, even if you were nominally a full-time warrior.


Macareus actually enjoyed this work: if nothing else, it made a pleasant change from the daily grind of strength training and working out with weapons.  While he enjoyed the sparring - particularly against strong opponents such as Cronos - at the end of the day he was always left with something of the feeling that he had not achieved anything tangible.  Particularly when he went home to Amphissa and she told him all the things that she had achieved in her day.


No, reaping barley was satisfying work.  As the day went on you could see where you had been, and the swathe that you had cut through the field.  And know that all the barley that wasn't there any more was being threshed right now in the Megara - he was sure Amphilla would be doing her part - and would provide food for all the villagers for the next year.


The rhythms were very similar to sword fighting as well - except you didn't need to worry about the man next to you _trying_ to lop your arm off with his scythe.  Step with the left foot, clutch the stalks with the left hand, swing with the right, turn and drop the stalks behind you, then step up with the right foot before starting again.  Over the course of the day, you walked from one end of the field to the other and possibly back again.  Meanwhile, boys and girls collected the cut stalks you dropped and took them off for threshing.


He stopped for a moment to inspect the blade on his scythe.  Much like his bronze sword, it was capable of slicing through anything when it was sharp, but once it lost its edge it became little better than a wooden training sword.  Some of the youngsters did, in fact, use wooden scythes during the harvest - for their own protection as much as anything - but a bronze scythe was much more effective: until it went blunt.  As he gingerly ran his fingers along it, he could sense what he had already felt while swinging it: it was starting to go blunt.


"New scythe, please!" he called, and stood up, pleasantly relieved to have the opportunity to get out of the continuous crouch.  Soon, a child ran up, proferring him a new, bronze-headed scythe with a long oak handle.  He took it with his left hand then, with his right, passed the child the used, blunt, scythe, making sure the child didn't injure herself with the blade - still quite sharp enough to do her a serious injury if she weren't careful.  Once she had left, he took a deep breath and returned to the job at hand: move left foot ...


--


The girl delivered the scythe to Beolus in the forge.


"Put it there, with the others," he instructed her, pointing to a stack of five other scythes leaning up against a wall.


He returned to the scythe he was working on.  Like everyone else in the village, the time of harvest was a time when he worked all the time, with a continuous level of pressure.  There were always scythes coming in, needing sharpening or even repair, and he had to turn them around and get them ready to be used again.  This one was blunt all along the blade and had a couple of chips in it.  His first job was to try and smooth those chips out - ideally without having to reforge the entire blade.  That was an option he tried to reserve for the blades that had hit a rock and literally shattered: when a blade came to him in two or three pieces, there really wasn't any option except to melt down the bronze and start again.


Once again, he found himself wondering where the bronze came from.  From time to time, travellers would come through the area with new bronze implements that they had "acquired", usually in the north, but usually in trade from other travellers who likewise knew little of where they came from.  He imagined that the bronze must ultimately come from somewhere but did it grow on some plant? Or was it the trunk of some tree?  Or was it found in the ground?  He had no idea.


He had learnt the magic secrets of working with bronze from his mentor when he had been an apprentice.  Secrets that he was now passing on to the apprentices in his forge.  How the bronze could not stand heat, but would first glow red, then melt, as you made it hotter and hotter.  His forge was kept beautifully warm in the winter - and stifingly hot in the summer - by a big brick fireplace which consumed a lot of wood when it was running.  For which reason, it wasn't kept running all the time: he would wait until he had a number of items that needed to be forged before stoking the fire and doing that work.


During harvest, he hardly ever lit the furnace.  The most important thing was to keep the reapers in the fields equipped with scythes.  And the quickest way to do that was to turn around the simple jobs - resharpening blades and, when necessary, as with this one, removing the chips before sharpening it.


He took another stroke with his flint scraper.  A few flakes of bronze came off, while others moved around, evening up the edges of the nick.  It was important to always stroke the right way, filling in the gap rather than opening it up.


Many strokes later, he was happy with the outcome.  The blade was not perfect, but it was a fairly even line.  He put down the scraper and picked up his whetstone.  Drawing it along the length of the blade, it left a beautiful edge.  Again and again he repeated the stroke.  Then he turned the blade over and worked on the other side.  Time after time, he stroked the edge and turned the blade this way and that.


Finally, when he was satisfied, he ran his fingers carefully over the edge.  Yes, that was sharp enough to do a day's work in the fields.  He stood up and leant it against the wall with the scythes ready to go out and be used again, then picked up the next one needing sharpening.  In the time he had been working on the last one, two more scythes had been delivered.  Oh, well, harvest was always a busy time of year.


--


Amphilla was nearly in tears.


Her mother had often told her how hard threshing was, and while she had always believed her, she had felt that it surely couldn't be _that_ hard; but now that she was actually trying it herself for the first time, she realised how very, very hard it was.


She was sitting in the Megara with all the other women, and specifically between her mother Arne and Arne's sister Desmontes.  They had years of experience doing this and were clearly expert.  She had been doing it for a week and had seemingly gained no skill at all.  Some women, she knew, never did get good at it although the pressure to get the work done meant that only a very few - usually those who had a great deal of skill at something else, which she did not - could be spared from the effort.


She placed more barley on her grate and tossed it, moving the grate to catch it and spinning it to try and separate grain from husk.  To no avail.  The raw grain just sat there on her grate as if she had done nothing at all.  At least, most of it did.  Some of it had drifted off onto the floor of the Megara somewhere.


"This is not for me," she cried.


"Keep at it," chided her mother.  "I think they're nearly done.  Tomorrow will be the day of contest.  You'll enjoy that."


"Yes, I will," she replied, cheering slightly.  "But then I'll spend the next year fretting over whether I will be able to do better at this next year."


"Fret not," said her mother.  "A year is a long time for the Goddess.  Anything might have happened by then."


--


Cronos and Rhea led a procession of villagers out into the field.  A stand of barley remained in the middle of the field and today was the day of contest: the male reapers competed for the title of best reaper, and the women and children were to chase down the corn spirits as they emerged from the remaining barley.  The individual - or team - with the most animals captured would take home the corn spirit prize.


Cronos led the men and picked up a scythe and inspected its edge.  Beolus had been working all yesterday and through much of the night to make sure that all the scythes were in perfect condition.  Even so, Cronos didn't want to take any chances.  But this scythe was perfect.  He ran his finger along the blade - so smooth, so sharp!


Macareus came up next to him and picked up another scythe, which, although it looked fine, seemed slightly off to his touch.  Rejecting it he picked up the next: yes, this was perfect!  He followed Cronos to the edge of the standing barley and moved to his right.


"Just like in the battle line, boss," he said to Cronos.  "I'll be at your right hand, making sure you can be at your best."


"Already given up, eh, Macareus?" asked Cronos.  "Or do you think you can out-reap me today?"


Macareus smiled: "I can only do my best, boss.  Let the children do the counting."


And with that, they were off: step, clutch, swing, turn, drop, step and repeat.  Through a boiling sunny morning, and a boiling sunny afternoon, all the men of the village kept at it.  As time went on, some were forced to drop out as the field of barley became ever smaller and the number of men who could swing a scythe at it at the same time fell.  Cronos and Macareus were still scything away: step, clutch, swing, turn, drop, step ...


--


Amphilla saw the hare before her mother did.


She shrieked and pointed to her right, where it had appeared through the line of reapers and was making a dash for the safety of the hedgerow.  She took three steps and leapt, arms outstretched, to try and catch it.  She managed to get one hand on it, and slow its progress: long enough for her mother to come around and deal it a killer blow with her mallet.


After the frustration of the days of threshing, she was exhilarated to be outside, all the more so to be able to chase the corn spirits.  And to catch the first one she saw, that was fantastic!


As she felt the inner surge of joy, from the corner of her eye she saw movement.  By instinct, without thinking, she dived to her left and managed to put both hands on a vole escaping from the barley field.  A few heartbeats later, her mother had applied the mallet and it went limp in her hands.


"Two already!" Arne exclaimed.  "That's as many as I've ever caught in an entire harvest.  I think we may set a record this year."


Amphilla couldn't help beaming with pride.


--


At the end of the day Rhea, with all the barley finally reaped and threshed, announced the winners.


"Among the men, there were some excellent reaping performances; I am pleased to say that my husband, your King, Cronos did an excellent job.  But by common consent among the judges, the prize goes to Macareus, who worked diligently all day and narrowly edged him, reaping the most corn today."


There was general applause; Macareus was popular among all the villagers.  Cronos went up to him and engulfed him in a bear hug.  "Well done, Macareus," he exclaimed.  "You certainly deserved it."


"With regard to the corn spirits, there can be no doubt as to this year's winners.  With an unbelievable total of five spirits collected, the winners are Arne, Amphilla and Desmontes.  They have designated Amphilla to carry the offering to the Goddess tomorrow."


"Gather round women," declared Rhea, "for it is time to make the cakes of offering.  Men, you are to keep to your tasks and drills tomorrow, but you are not to intrude on the offering of the Haloa, for that is reserved unto women."


The men dismissed, the women gathered in groups in the Megara, making a paste from the barley, and then shaping the paste into offerings of penises and vulvas.  The cakes were left to dry overnight.


In the morning, the women formed up in the Megara and Amphilla joined the senior priestesses in picking up a plate of the offering.  She carried it in front of her all the way to the cisterns on the western edge of the village, where the snakes awaited the offering of the first of the harvest - an essential before the rest of the village could profit from the harvest.


They bowed in reverence to the Goddess and to her earthly representatives, leaving the platters by the cisterns and retreated to the village.  They did not see the snakes come out and devour the offerings.  This time, the omens were good for the village.
