His thrust went wide.


It was tricky fighting in the uneven light of the volcano at sunset.  But this was the time custom dictated for the ritual, when both God and man walked the earth.  He realised his mind had wandered and snapped his focus back to the moment when his opponent's bronze blade glimmered in the red and purple light.


Technically, it wasn't a duel to the death: no property rights had been transgressed; neither had interfered with the other's women.  But in some senses it was worse.  To lose here would, in effect, to be to lose the life he had always known.  To lose here would be an outcast.  And worse, to be shown inadequate in the face of the Gods. Not to mention his father.


Regrouping his concentration again, he went on the attack.  He drove a hard line down, across, straight.  Boroon blocked each in turn, then twisted.  Cronos saw an opening and dived for it; too late, he saw the trap as Boroon opened his guard and delivered a counterthrust, cutting open the unprotected flesh on his upper arm.


"Good, good!" barked their father.  "Yes, yes, indeed the Gods have told us the answer.  You are my true son, Boroon, and you will inherit this tribe from me.  Cronos, the Gods have decreed that your destiny is to be your own.  But tomorrow is enough for that.  For now, let us celebrate!  I declare a feast." He turned to face the volcano and addressed it: "Come, oh Gods, come and join us.  We offer you the fat of the beasts in your element.  Come and join us, feast with us on this savoury flesh.  Come, celebrate with us the dividing of the tribe!"


For almost a year now, the tribe had been struggling with its size.  Tirzaan, the leader - and father to both Cronos and Boroon, although by different mothers, as if that mattered - had been astonishingly successful in growing his tribe.  He had been good in war; by conquering other tribes he had claimed their women for his own and taken their goats.  By breeding frequently - and successfully - he had had many fine sons.  The daughters he had managed to sell to other tribes with whom he chose not to fight.  And all the time he had been expert at breeding his flock of goats.


But success this magnificent came at a price.  Fortunately, tradition told him how to deal with it.


The main problem, of course, was finding enough food.  As a nomad, he could go wherever he wanted and stay until all the food had been eaten.  He could pluck almost limitless fish from the rivers by which they camped; he could drink milk from his goats, and from time to time, could even kill a goat to feast on.  But it took a large area to camp, the goats always wanted more grass or scrub, and the women had to wander a long way to find fruits and vegetables in the surrounding forests.


Besides that, of course, there were all the other problems that came from dragging such a large group around with you: the constant bickering between rival factions; the arguments about women; the occasional fights that broke out between highly charged individuals.  And, as the group became bigger, so it naturally started to split along the faultlines of friends and families.


"When the tribe is too big, find the two most powerful leaders," his father had advised him shortly after dealing with the same problem and making Tirzaan his chosen heir.  "Take them to the Gods and get them to fight it out.  If one of them dies, sobeit, but at the end the winner is your heir.  The loser is outcast; let them start a new tribe to raise up more seed in your name, but they must leave.  Don't stint in letting them take goods with them - the idea is to lose men, women and goats.  The tribe will naturally split down the middle: those that love the loser and are afraid of the winner will leave; the majority will want to stay because they know and trust your leadership.


"And then, obviously, you must have a feast to show your gratitude to the Gods for delivering their verdict.  Don't stint on that either.  Never stint on your worship of the Gods.  It always pays to have them as friends.  Particularly at a time when they could choose to leave you and go with the Outcast."


And so the feast they threw was huge.  Each of Tirzaan's grown sons - and a leader of a family in their own right - contributed a goat to the feast. Soon nine goats were on spits over nine fires, their blood trickling into the fire.  Each of the fires had been lit from the heat or flames of the volcano - the Gods attending in person and making their contribution.


When the meat was sizzling and ready, Tirzaan ordered his sons to make the offering to the Gods.  Each of them cut the heads and removed the entrails, and cast them sparkling into the fire.  They also chose some of the juiciest strips of flesh - full of fat and grease, essential for keeping warm in these cold temperatures - and threw them alongside in the fire.  The spirits of the Gods flared up and consumed the fat as it was thrown in, the flames rising high to meet the juices.


Only when the Gods had feasted did Tirzaan once again give a command to his sons: "Now, my sons, bring me your choicest flesh that I may feast."  Each of them quickly, but not hurriedly, selected the finest portions remaining on their animals and sliced it off and carried it to him.  Quickly, he gobbled the food as it was given him before it grew cold.  Ah, but it was delicious.  He realized he was going to miss Cronos.  Whatever his other faults - and he had many, most particularly disobedience - he was an expert chef.  The meat was tender, but runny with juices.  Lovely and hot but not to the point of burning him.  How the boy did it, he did not know.


Come to that, how many of his boys would still be with him tomorrow?  He looked around him at his nine sons.  Boroon obviously; likewise not Cronos.  Of the other seven, probably four would choose to stay.  He had no doubt that Kilionan would go with Cronos: the two had been inseparable since they were young children; born within months of each other some twenty years ago, Kilionan had been Cronos' sidekick his entire life.


Probably Ard-noon would leave as well.  He had generally been the peacemaker as strife had flared in the past year or so, but anyone who could use the word "peace" and "Cronos" in the same sentence must have a level of love for the man that even Tirzaan - his father - could not manage.


He looked at the others again.  Yes, that would be the split.  Three would leave and six would stay.  That would probably see him out during his lifetime; and then the tribe would probably split again on his funeral day when his mortal remains would be offered to the Gods.


His thoughts turned to that future once again.  On that day, would they judge that his life had been sufficiently worthy to be given the honour of joining them?  And, if so, how many of his sons would worship him that day?  After all, immortality was the one thing that made all this mortal struggle worthwhile.  Without the aspiration to reach the Gods, was life really worth it?


--


In the morning, he was up early.  Here in the mountains, sunrise was always a questionable thing.  The sky became brighter, but you were lucky if you saw the sun at any point in the day, let alone as it rose between the mountains.  But more importantly that that, he wanted to be able to see the light of the volcano one last time, to commune with the Gods and, while still in their presence, beg their guidance in what was to come.  If all went well, the division would be easy; it it didn't ... the thought trailed off.


He wondered if he would need to send his herald to find his sons and bring them to him; but he did not.  By the time he had finished his silent communion with the Gods of the Volcano, he turned to find all nine of them arrayed before him and, much as he had expected, already divided into two groups.  To his left, ready to depart, the trio of Cronos, Kilionan and Ard-noon.  To the right, his six other sons grouped around Boroon.  He nodded once.  It was as he had expected, what he would have ordained.  But the choice was not in his hands, but the hands of the Gods.


"Cronos, Kilionan, Ard-noon.  Today is the day of parting; the day in which it is your honour and obligation to leave your father and set off into the unknown.  You have the right to take all your property with you: your goats, your tents, your swords, your women.  All that is not yours you will leave behind.  And yet, as your father, it is my pleasure to offer you a boon: is there anything belonging to me that you would ask for youselves?  That which belongs to your brothers you may not ask."


Cronos stepped forward and bowed low in homage to the man who would ever remain his father but whose suzerainty he would leave this day.  "We take a hard road, father, since we must not only depart from you, but also depart from these lands, for, were we to stay, you and I would come to contention and to blows."  Indeed we would, thought Tirzaan, a fight you know you could not win: aside from the numbers, last night proved you not to be Boroon's equal.


"I spent last night in communion with the Gods, since this is a holy place.  And they sent me a sign:  I saw it, as did my brothers," he indicated Kilionan and Ard-noon.  "Having discussed the sign at length and asked for the guidance of our Fathers, I have come to the conclusion that it can only mean one thing." He turned to his right and added, "the Gods wish us to travel south through the mountains."


Tirzaan was shocked.  The Cronos he knew was vapid and quick to anger; always ready to take the easy, not the hard path.  One of many reasons he preferred Boroon as his heir: and one of the reasons the Boroon had _become_ his heir last night.  The way south was all but unknown.


"It is my belief that the slave Fidaah was born somewhere to the south, possibly even south of the mountains and came into your possession through a series of accidents that I have always considered bizarre.  But last night, as I sat praying, I considered.  Father, it is possible that the Gods sent him to us in order to help me in this very moment?"


Tirzaan looked at him blankly.  Of all the directions he had thought this conversation might take, this was not one he had even considered.  Clearly, Cronos had been thinking things through all night - or else one of his brothers had given him good advice.  Fidaah was nothing special as a slave, and, as far as he knew, had no particular attachment to any of the others in the tribe.  In terms of what it would cost Tirzaan, it was almost nothing.  But he could see the value to Cronos if he really did intend to try to force a passage through the mountains.  He decided to stall for time while he thought through the ramifications.


"Does he, in fact, know anything useful that would be of assistance to you?  Does he know a route south into the mythical warm lands there where the sun shines all year?  Do you think he still knows tribes down that way who can guide you or help you?  Can you, even with your brothers and this guide hope to force a way through whoever is in your way?"


Cronos shrugged, a large gesture from this giant of a man with big shoulders.  Had last night's contest been one of physical strength rather than skill with bronze, Tirzaan had little doubt that the outcome would have favoured this man.


A silence developed and lengthened.  Eventually Cronos relented and offered what little he knew.  "I have heard him tell stories to the children around the fire of a cold evening.  I don't know how much is story, how much myth and how much is memory.  But that he knows of the lands from which he claims to come I have no doubt.  He may be a help to me, or he may be useless.  I cannot see how, even as an extra mouth to me, he would be a hindrance."


Tirzaan nodded, thoughtfully.  All good points.  In the long run, it mattered little, and the conversation had gone on long enough that it constituted all he would ask for.  Tirzaan had worried he would ask for things of real value - goats or swords.  Or maybe something of trade value - there were some daughters in camp he could have taken for their trade value to other tribes as he travelled.  He nodded his head once, in finality.


"Good enough.  Summon the slave Fidaah.  He shall go with Cronos and his two brothers.  Let all be set in motion for the parting.  Cronos, Kilionan, Ard-noon: in the presence of our Gods, I release you from my family.  You are no longer my sons.  You are free to find your own path.  And yet, the path of three equals is a tricky thing that leads to bloodshed, so I hereby declare as my last act that in choosing to go with Cronos, who would have been heir had he won last night, you put yourself under his authority as you have been under mine until such times as you shall agree to another Dividing.  All of that shall be in your hands."


As he finished, Fidaah was brought into the assembly from the compound of the slaves.  "Fidaah, know that I have given you to my son Cronos.  You are now his property as formerly you were mine and his fate you share.  He plans to go to the south and would take you as his guide.  Your life and death are in his hands.  I suggest you guide him to the best of your ability."


--


Much of the year had passed in the mountains.  The three brothers, their slave guide, their gaggle of women and slaves, along with their herds of goats had been battling through the mountains.  From time to time they had encountered other parties in valleys and passes and, cautiously, made contact with them.  As yet, bloodshed had been avoided.  Human bloodshed, that is.  On every occasion a goat had been sacrificed to invoke the Gods and to provide meat for a feast.


As a guide, Fidaah had been a complete waste of space.  He didn't even know north from south, couldn't guide himself by sun or stars (if and when they ever chose to come out), couldn't tell a pass from a valley, had no idea about following trails or telling if there were other groups around.  But nevertheless, Cronos judged, he had been worth his weight in gold.


The thing was, Fidaah might not know the first thing about navigation, geography or tracking, but he had not forgotten any of the languages he had learned as a child.  Early in their travels, most of the groups that they met spoke a language familiar to Cronos - pretty much a dialect of the language he spoke himself.  He could have handled negotations with those tribes by himself, and certainly with the help of his brothers.  But as they had made their way first up into the mountains, and then, as best he could tell, back down again, the tribes started to speak ever more exotic tongues.  And to Cronos' surprise, Fidaah knew all of them, even when Cronos couldn't even make out the sentence structures, never mind the individual words.  For himself, he was reduced to the seemingly universal system of body language, hand gestures and signs to convey simple meanings.  When he had introduced himself and distanced himself from any violent intentions, he motioned Fidaah forward who, bowing lowing as was expected of an inferior, started to converse with the leaders opposite.  Sometimes, the tribes they met would also have an interpreter: such individuals invariably turned out to have a history as exotic as Fidaah's.


Santazin signalled a halt.


Given the uselessness of Fidaah as a guide, Cronos had scouted around for alternatives.  Santazin, one of his slaves and hunters of long standing, turned out to have a natural talent in these mountains.  Most usefully, he was hypersensitive to other living creatures: human or animal.  And, at this moment, it would seem he had noticed the proximity of another tribe.  Cronos closed his eyes and attempted to focus his other senses.  But, try as he might, he could not duplicate Santanin's efforts.  He could not hear, smell or feel anything out of the ordinary.  He opened his eyes again to see Santazin beckoning him forward.  He carefully and silently picked his way forward, then followed as Santazin started climbing up a nearby outcrop.  When they reached the top, Cronos was astounded by the view.


Spread out below them was a valley.  How they would get down there, Cronos had no clue.  But it was huge and deep, with a river running right through it and forming a lake in the middle.  And there, he could see as Santazin pointed it out, there was a tribe camped near the river, with tents and goats spread around.  As he looked closer he realised something else: this camp had more _permanent_ structures in it: as well as tents, tree trunks had been felled and used to build structures that he presumed people could live in.  He had heard - from Fidaah among others - of these more permanent structures: _houses_ was the word Fidaah had used from his youth; but to see them was a different thing again.  He was entranced.


"Notice the trunks in the river," whispered Santazin beside him.  There was no chance that the sound of their voices would carry that distance down below - Cronos was still unsure what Santazin had picked up on to be aware of their presence himself - but both of them felt an awe that caused them to feel the need to whisper.  Cronos followed where Santazin was pointing and was confused.  A set of trunks were laying ramshackle across the river.  Cronos struggled to see the point.  Were they halfway through moving them from one side to the other?  But they had these "houses" on both sides of the river and up to the shore of the lake.  And there were forests on both sides.  What was the point?  But clearly Santazin had grasped it.


"They make a ... across the river," he said, struggling for the word and holding his hands up and together to show what he meant.  Cronos looked at the river and back at his slave's hands but still struggled to grasp the what or the why.  Then Santazin pointed back down at the river where a woman was getting up near the trunks and walking towards them - and then onto them.  She kept walking, stepping from one trunk to another and - across the river!  These people, whoever they were, had managed to build their own artificial ford across the river in the middle of their camp site.  Cronos had never seen anything like it.  No, he corrected himself: he had never even *heard* of such a thing.  If they could do this, what else could they do?


"But why ... why would they do that?" he struggled to say.  None of this made sense.  Not only was this unprecedented, it said something even more strongly than the houses did.  His brain simply could not process what it was being shown.


"They have found a way not to move," Santazin struggled to form the words.  They simply did not have the concepts in their language for people who chose - who _had_ the choice - to stay in one place.  Everybody moved: there simply wasn't enough land to stay in one place.  The goats would eat in out in no time.  Although, these people seemed to have less goats that you might expect for the number of people.  And the land didn't look eaten out.


"How?" Cronos asked, wide-eyed, trying to make sense of it all.


Santazin could only shrug.  He had seen what he had seen and described it to the best of his ability with the limited experience and vocabulary at his disposal.  But he was not a great thinker, just a slave with a talent for navigation and tracking.  In his view, it was Cronos' job to make sense of it and to work their way through this situation.  He just sat and watched.


--


In all, they sat and watched for seven days. In that time, they saw many things that made absolutely no sense to them.  People came and went, they dragged sticks through the fields, they carried water from the river and then just poured it out on the fields.  These people were strange beyond belief.


While Santazin and Cronos watched, Kilionan had led a group of trackers around the canyon rim and found a route down following the river.  For five days they had tracked the route and then come back in just two.  As far as they could tell, none of the people down below ever came up into the hills.  They seemed to live down there _permanently_, never going anywhere.  Kilionan came up to Cronos to tell him what he had discovered.


"So, what should we do?  Can we fight them?  Or should we try to talk to them, maybe do a trade with them?" Cronos asked.  "Or should we just go around them?"


"I don't know.  So much of what they do is a mystery.  Do they worship other Gods?  Do they have other magics?  How can they live on so little land?  And this thing you say of taking water out of the river to pour on the ground?  Why do they do that?  Is it to show respect to their Gods?"


Cronos shook his head.  The only thing he was sure of was the portent that had brought him this far.  He knew the Gods had told him to make his way south for some reason.  This was south, further south than he even had known existed.  He was certainly out of range of his father.  Was this where he was supposed to come?  But then, how could he live here?  Certainly, he could see that these villagers lived here somehow, but their mode of lifestyle was obscure to him.  Could he defeat them?  If he did, could he work the same magic that they did, or would their Gods reject him?  And while all this was happening, he was eating his way out of the land around the canyon's rim.  He would need to move tomorrow, or the day after at the latest.  And by then he needed to have made a decision: go down into the valley below, or go around them.


--


As they came out of the river canyon into the valley, Cronos marshalled his men with the armed veterans at the front and the others holding the animals and women to the rear.  He wasn't sure if there was going to be a fight, although he had decided by now that he didn't _want_ a fight: if there was going to be a fight, it would not be of his choosing; but if there was a fight, he was determined to win it.


With his veterans at the front, he brought Fidaah.  If he had any hope of avoiding a fight, it would be through a combination of a show of strength with a reasonable conversation.  And if he was going to have any hope of a conversation, he needed someone who could speak the language.  That might or might not be Fidaah, but he was their best hope.  Before they had come down into the valley, Cronos had asked him if he knew anything of what was going on down here, and Fidaah only had a few words that might describe it: "field", "village" and "bridge".  But it was all too long ago and he could not explain, other than to confirm that yes, he had once lived just in one place where women had made plants grow where they wanted them to and that water was very important, so it had been by a river.  More than that, he could no longer remember.


As they walked around the lake and came closer to the "village", Cronos had his trumpeter play the challenge.  He didn't imagine these people would have the same trumpet calls, but he wanted to alert them to his approach and give them time to prepare.  If they chose battle, he would have lost the element of surprise, but while watching them, they had not seemed particularly martial.  He imagined that they would choose peace.


Slowly a group emerged from the village and came toward them.  It was a group of six, only three of whom were armed, and even then only with flint axes.  If it came to a fight, these six would be dead before they had finished exchanging insults.  But then the truly odd thing dawned on him - the other three, the unarmed three, were all women.  And the women were in the lead.  The women _were in charge_?  Cronos could not really believe what he was seeing.


--


Fidaah turned to Cronos and said, "I cannot really understand what they are saying.  Some words, maybe, but the language is different again to anything I have ever heard."


But it was clear that they did not have violence in mind.  They were curious: clearly, they did not have many visitors in this isolated valley.  Where had they come from?  That, at least, seemed to be the main question on the natives' mind.  How to describe it?  Was it worth describing?


They settled in to try again.


--


Cronos' temper snapped.


It was a lot of things, but mainly it came down to the fact that this old man opposite him seemed to think that the most important person here was the _woman_ to his right, and not the _man_ standing in front of him.  He would have long conversations with her, and then she would try and say something and Cronos just felt ... _disrespected_.


"What is wrong with you?" he yelled in the old man's face and then, yanking his sword from his belt, cut the man's throat before he could even react.  His pupils were still dilating as he fell to the ground, blood pumping from his neck.


Before he could turn around, his brothers had followed his example and all three of the men opposite them were lying in the dirt, dead or dying.  Which left the women.  The women who had been shown too much respect by their menfolk.  Cronos knew how to deal with uppity women.


Reversing his sword, he slashed it across the woman's garment and it floated free and dropped away to the earth.  A second stroke removed her loincloth.  She stood transfixed, obviously unable to process what had happened, although it seemed impossible that she had not been treated this way before.  Dropping his bronze sword, Cronos removed his own loincloth and exposed his other sword.  Then he simply pushed the woman to the ground, fell on top of her and started thrusting inside her.  At once, she started screaming and pummelling his giant chest with her tiny fists.  Oh, she had a temper, this one!  He would enjoy taming her.  He continued to rut her, trying to make it as painful for her as possible, to demean her, to expose her powerlessness, to reduce the standing she would have had in the dead man's eyes, but nothing about this woman's spirit gave way.  She might have been beaten physically, but she was still determined to outlast him: he could see that much in her eyes.


As he climbed off her, she spat at him and kicked him in the testicles.  She started to roll to get away, but, lost in a red mist as he was, he still easily picked up his sword and drove it straight through her belly, pinning her to the ground.  He laughed at her as she squealed and squirmed and the life ran out of her.


He looked around, saw his brothers were still raping the other two women.  "Kill them when you're done," he ordered.


Kilionan heard the order but decided not to wait.  It was more fun to choke them to death while you were still on top.  He knew this from experience.  He held her around the throat and stopped her breathing while he kept pounding away at her.  When he finally ejaculated, it was into the dead husk of a woman.


--


By the time they reached the village, all the other people and most of the animals had gone.  Cronos had no idea where.  He dispatched Santazin to try and find them, but it was a hopeless task.  All he could really say was that they hadn't all gone to the same place together by the same route.  This had possibly happened before and they had a plan for dealing with intruders.  Perhaps the trumpet blast had been a mistake.


--


Cronos looked for a sign.


Two nights later, it was dark but it was clear.  He had a clear view of the sky, and, while there were no volcanoes around to give him a sense of closeness to the Gods, he felt that they had left the sky clear so that they could send him a sign.


It had not been a good two days.  What the magic was that these people worked was beyond him.  There was nothing obvious to eat here in this valley, although there was a small amount of fruit to be found in the trees.  They had subsisted the past two days by killing some of the goats the people had left behind and the supply of fish in the river.  The fish presumably would not run out, but the goats would.  And either way, there wasn't an obvious supply of grazing in the valley for their own goats.


If this was the "south" to which the Gods had intended him to come, it confused him.  Once again, he regretted his loss of temper that ended in the death of the people that might have been able to explain the magic and the spells and the offerings to him.  Were there different Gods here in this land who worked in different ways and wanted different things?  He could not tell.  He needed a sign: either to confirm that the Gods he had known his entire life came with him here, or else to tell him how to adapt to life here.


Suddenly, he saw the shooting stars he had seen before, many moons ago after losing the duel with Boroon.  They started to his right in the north, and went clearly to the south.  Well, that answered that.  This was not the destination and he would need to press on with his journey.  But how?  Should he follow the track of the river, or should he climb back into the mountains?  What was he looking for?


Rushing out of the sky from behind him came another shooting star: closer by far to him than any he had seen before.  It tore through the sky in a blaze of fire, almost like a miniature volcano.  That this was a sign from the Gods could not be doubted for a minute.  He watched it as it fell, illuminating the river below and the canyon the river issued through before splashing down _in_ the river.


That was a sign that could not be doubted.  He was to build boats here from the tree trunks which had already been felled and follow the river to the south.  No doubt about it.
