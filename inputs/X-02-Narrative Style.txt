title=Narrative Style


In general, when writing I prefer to use a first-person perspective.  In this book, that has not been possible for any number of reasons.  At no point was there a character that I felt could tell enough of the story in their own voice.


However, as much as possible, I have followed the style I refer to as "third-person cheating", which is to say, I have tried to tell the story in the third person, but in every chapter there is a "lead" character that the story follows closely, showing much more of the _inside_ of that character than any of the others.  So, in Section 1, the girl Sprig, who becomes the woman Clematis, is at the centre of the story and much of what is told is told through her eyes.  In the first place, I made this choice because, as a girl becoming a woman, she was perfectly positioned to tell the story to people who likewise had never seen this society at work before.


In all cases, I have tried to avoid giving any descriptions or accounts of natural phenomena or human experience or other matters such as time, space or geography which would have been outside the experience or understanding of those present.  While I recognise that this may be confusing to some readers, I am trying to stay as close to the experience and worldview of my protagonists as possible given the constraints.


So, for example, I have refrained from using any kind of modern units, including, but not limited to, weeks, seconds, hours, preferring instead to relate concepts associated with the motion of the moon or sun to describe the passage of time and "heartbeats" where short units of time are concerned.  If you are a relatively healthy adult, a complete heartbeat should not be that much shorter than a second.  If it's longer, you are probably very healthy.


In the same way, I have tried to avoid using modern conveniences such as counting and writing.  It's my belief that all of the societies mentioned in this book would have had access to both, although in both cases, I would think that it would be at a primitive level.  Even up until the middle of the first millennium BC, the Persian army counted their casualties by having each soldier going to war deposit an arrow in a storehouse.  Those that came back alive picked up an arrow from the pile and put it elsewhere.  A very simple visualisation of the number of casualties was created by the simple expedient of comparing the two piles: the first pile was the count of those who did not return; the second pile was the survivors.


This novel may not flow like a complete story: jumping as it does across four epochs and two locations, it is not possible to create a single narrative.  Inasmuch as there is a single character for this story, it is the titular _Goddess_ who is slowly but surely crushed out of the story until she is "killed" in the person of Carmenta in Rome by the jealousy of the Tarquins and the founding of the Republic.  But even under the kings in Rome, her worship - like women generally - was ruthlessly suppressed.  The rape and suicide of Lucretia may have been the end point of this story but by the time it happened, it was largely irrelevant.


I have tried to tell this vast, sweeping story through a series of _vignettes_: each mini story illuminating some aspect of the society that existed and the relationships between Goddess, women, men, ritual, wealth and power.  Wealth and power are inextricably linked, and men only truly started to gain power once they were able to gain and hold wealth by identifying their own progeny and being able to pass on their accumulated wealth to _their_ children.  Before DNA testing, it was only by insisting on marriage and the sanctity of marriage that any man could truly know if a child was his.  And once men started going down that path, the complete suppression of women - in ancient Rome or modern Afghanistan - was inevitable.


The names of the characters have evolved as I have told the story.  In Section 4, I have mainly used the characters in Livy, and thus their names.  Auxiliary characters have been given other, typical, Roman names current in the era.  In Section 3, as Zeus takes control, I have used a lot of names of Greek gods, and other names from myth and legend.  Section 2 sees some of the names of earlier gods, such as Cronos, who were "phased out" by Zeus.  The society of Section 1 has been so thorougly suppressed from our history that, short of decoding Linear A texts (which I believe to have been from this period, in a language of this period which pre-dates all Indo-European languages), I have no idea what names would be used.  I chose to use flower names for the women, with more flowery names for the leaders, and names that we might use for pets for the men, representing their much lower status.  Obviously I have no actual evidence for this whatsoever, but I do feel that in this primitive society men were probably viewed much like pets or beasts of burden - they were good for three things: heavy labour, fighting and sex.  And their status and thus names reflected this.


I have tried to show how complete the reversal was by the end of the book, in which women in Rome not only had simpler names that those of the men, but all the daughters of one family had _the same name_: the _gens_ of the family; to distinguish them numbers or nicknames were used.
